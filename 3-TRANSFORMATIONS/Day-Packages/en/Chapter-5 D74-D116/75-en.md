## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the fourth and fifth verses of the fifth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Let us look together at this amazing method: guarding just our own mind protects us from fearsome tigers and many evil spirits.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a story that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Long ago, a bodhisattva who was a sea-captain went to sea to gather jewels. He lost his way and arrived at Siṃhaladvīpa, a city of demonesses. The fearsome demonesses caught him and kept him there as a husband. A kind man secretly told him that this was, in truth, a city of demonesses. He said that the next day a horse would rise from the ocean, and anyone who wished to escape could ride on its back and reach the human world. The sea-captain rode that horse and escaped safely back to his own land.

This story shows us something important. If we guard our own mind from the afflictions and tame it, no fear will arise from tigers, lions, demonesses, or anything else.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when anger or fear arises in me, I will not look outward. Instead, I will tame only my own mind.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* We cannot tame every outer enemy and every fear one by one. If we tame only our own mind, every harm will calm down naturally. So taming our own mind is the best practice for the benefit of ourselves and others.

##### The Tale of the Bodhisattva-Prince Kalyāṇakārin

King Indraratnavarman had two sons: one of virtuous mind, named Kalyāṇakārin, and one of sinful mind, named Pāpakārin. Kalyāṇakārin, moved by great compassion, strove to relieve his subjects' poverty until he had exhausted his father's treasury, and so set out across the ocean to an island to obtain a wish-fulfilling jewel. Partway along the road, he crossed a place infested with tigers, lions, snakes, and rākṣasas that had carried off the jewel, and there obtained the wish-fulfilling jewel from a goddess. On the way back, his brother Pāpakārin, out of jealousy, pierced his eyes with thorns and seized the jewel. Although Kalyāṇakārin was blinded, he was tended by a cowherd and other kind-hearted people. In the end his sight was restored, and by the power of the wish-fulfilling jewel he benefited all beings; Pāpakārin too repented fully, and in the end all came to happiness. This story shows that every quality and every fault arises from the mind, and that a mind of compassion can overcome even the fear of tigers, lions, snakes, and the like.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The commentators agree that tigers, lions, and other wild beasts, together with enemies, are the dangers of this life, while the guardians of the hell-beings are the harm-doers of the next life's danger; since all these dangers arise from the fault of an unguarded mind, they hold that taming this single mind alone tames them.

**Key points.**

- **The harm-doers of this life's dangers.** Tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, bandits, and enemies and the like.
- **The harm-doers of the next life's dangers.** The guardians of the hell-beings, the henchmen of the Lord of Death, and the like.
- **The harm-doers threatening both this life and the next.** Sorcery cast through another's power, and rākṣasas that steal one's life-force and vitality, ghosts, tsen, gyalpo, and gongpo spirits, and the like.
- **The fault of an unguarded mind.** All these dangers arise from the fault of an unguarded mind; by binding and taming this single mind of one's own, all these harm-doers are tamed.
- **Clarified by the tale of Kalyāṇakārin.** A mind of compassion is shown to be able to overcome even the fear of fierce wild beasts such as tigers, lions, and snakes.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

The outer harm-doers — tigers, lions, snakes and the like; the guardians of the hells and evil destinies of the next life; and the beings called "sorcerers," that is, worldly flesh-eating dakinis, rākṣasas, and the like — every gathering of harm-doers is a varied form of harm arising as the fruit of bad karma accumulated through the power of an untamed mind. Ultimately, since the unbearable harms and fears of wild beasts and the like arise through the power of an untamed mind, if this single mind is bound, then, no differently from binding every outer harm-doer, harm cannot arise; and if this single mind of one's own, bound by mindfulness, is directed toward what is fitting so that a tamed mind arises, then one no longer accumulates the karma that leads to encountering adverse conditions and harm, and even karma already accumulated can be brought to transform. So he explains.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

When one holds this single mind of one's own with mindfulness and binds it firmly, all these harmful, malevolent forces become as if bound, and so cannot harm oneself. Through mindfulness and introspection, if one examines what lies ahead and behind and proceeds accordingly, one will not encounter tigers and the like; and since those endowed with mindfulness and introspection are protected and taken as their charge by the guardians of virtue, they do not meet with tigers and the like — and even if they do meet them, by praying to Padmasambhava they are not harmed, and so on. When this single mind of one's own is tamed through love, compassion, bodhicitta, or meditative absorption, all these dangers of this life and the next likewise become as if tamed and cause no harm. As proof of this principle, he cites the bodhisattva Asaṅga, before whom a sheep and a wolf played together in mutual affection, and the great adept Saraha, who rode upon a lion while using venomous snakes as ornaments, and the like. So he explains.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

It is fitting to bind the mind to a virtuous object. The harm of tigers, lions, elephants, bears, and venomous snakes — every kind of human enemy — as well as, in the next life, the guardians of the hell-beings, and the harm inflicted by sorcerers, dakinis and the like through mantric power, and by rākṣasas — all of this, once this single mind of one's own is bound by the rope of mindfulness to a virtuous object, becomes as if bound by reversing the very causes of fear, so that not the slightest harm can occur. And when this single mind of one's own is tamed through mindfulness and introspection so that it no longer engages in wrongdoing, all these harm-doers likewise become as if tamed. Therefore one should strive to tame the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, and every kind of enemy, together with the guardians of the hell-beings, sorcerers' curses, and rākṣasas — when this single mind of one's own is bound, all these harm-doers become bound; when this single mind is tamed, all of them become tamed, as clarified above by the passage from the *Jātakamālā*.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The harm-doers that threaten this life are tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, bandits, and every kind of enemy; the harm-doers that threaten the next life are the guardians of the hell-beings, the terrifying henchmen of the Lord of Death, and the like; the harm-doers that threaten both are sorcerers, dakinis, rākṣasas, and the like. When this single mind of one's own is held by mindfulness and bound, all these malevolent forces become as if bound and do no harm; and when this single mind of one's own, endowed with introspection, is tamed, all these malevolent forces likewise come under control as if tamed, and do no harm.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One might wonder: even if one guards the inner mind, since there are many outer harm-doers, how could all fears possibly vanish? Yet they do. All the many other enemies that bring fear in this life — tigers, lions, elephants, bears, venomous snakes, and human and non-human foes — and the guardians of the hell-beings and the henchmen of the Lord of Death that bring danger in the next life, together with sorcerers, dakinis, and rākṣasas of the preta realm that cause harm through the power of mantras — all these harm-doers, once this single mind of one's own is bound firmly to a virtuous object by the rope of mindfulness, which never forgets any point of what is to be practiced and what is to be abandoned by way of its antidotes, become — by reversing the very causes that give rise to every fear — as if all of them were bound or tied up, so that not the slightest harm or injury occurs. And when this single mind of one's own, by the strength of introspection, has its freely wandering engagement in wrongdoing tamed, all these outer harm-doers, such as tigers and lions, likewise come under one's own control as if entirely tamed, and cause no harm whatsoever. Therefore, he explains, one should bind the mind to a virtuous object and strive to train it. He also notes that the sea-captain, by guarding his mind, tamed the rākṣasī, and that accomplished masters ride upon tigers, lions, and the like, and hold venomous snakes in their hands, and so forth.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

By properly binding this single mind of one's own with mindfulness and introspection to a virtuous object, all the harms of this life and future lives become bound. By properly taming this single mind of one's own through love, compassion, and bodhicitta, all the fears and sufferings of this life and the next likewise become tamed. So he explains.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-5]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Binding This Single Mind Binds Every Harm-Doer; Taming It Tames Every Harm-Doer

All the commentaries explain this in agreement.

###### 2. Guarding the Mind with Mindfulness and Introspection Prevents Encounters with Danger, or Renders It Harmless

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that the guardians of virtue protect such a person, and that praying to Padmasambhava prevents harm even in an encounter.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** When this single mind of one's own is bound to a virtuous object by mindfulness and introspection, or tamed through love, compassion, and the meditation on emptiness, all outer harm-doers — tigers, lions, the guardians of the hells, sorcerers, rākṣasas, and the like — become as if bound and tamed, and cause not the slightest harm.

**Key points.**

- **If the mind is guarded, no harm-doer can cause harm.** When this single mind of one's own is bound firmly by the rope of mindfulness to a virtuous object and guarded through introspection, all outer harm-doers, by reversing the very causes that give rise to fear, become as if bound, and cause no harm.
- ⚑ **A difference in the method taught for taming the mind.** The interlinear gloss holds that the mind is tamed by meditating on emptiness — the fact that all phenomena lack inherent nature — while the other commentaries hold that it is tamed through love and compassion, bodhicitta, or through mindfulness and introspection that restrain wrongdoing.
- **All harm is the fruit of an untamed mind.** All the harm and fear inflicted by outer wild beasts, the guardians of the hells, sorcerers, and the like ultimately arise, His Holiness explains, as the fruit of bad karma accumulated through the power of an untamed mind.
- **The fruit illustrated by examples and stories.** The sea-captain tamed the rākṣasī by guarding his mind; before the bodhisattva Asaṅga, a wolf and a sheep played together in mutual affection; and the great adept Saraha rode a lion while holding venomous snakes in his hands — all illustrating the fruit of a tamed mind.
- **Atiśa's instruction.** Since the outer enemy is only a condition, while the inner enemy burns one's own being, if this inner mind is tamed, the outer enemy cannot cause harm.
