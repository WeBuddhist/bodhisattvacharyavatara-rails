## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 45 and 46 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Just as darkness has nowhere to hide when the sun rises, let us think together about how the afflictions in our mind, once removed, also have no way to return.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a point that is easy to understand and encouraging. An ordinary worldly enemy, even if driven out of a land, may grow strong again and return. But when the enemy of the afflictions is destroyed at the root by the wisdom that realizes selflessness, it has no place and no strength left to return, so it can never come back. In ultimate truth the afflictions have no true existence of their own; they arise only from confused thought, so they have no more solid core than a bubble. For example, when the sun rises inside a dark house, the darkness is cleared away at once and has nowhere left to go. In the same way, when we look at the afflictions with wisdom, they are naturally purified. So if the afflictions are still left in us, it is not because they have any power of their own by nature. It comes only from our own failure to make the effort to rely on the antidote.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when an affliction arises, I will examine it with wisdom and make the effort to let it go.

**Explanation:** *(Wisdom Practice)* When an affliction arises in our mind, if we examine it well with the eye of wisdom, we can see that it has no power of its own by nature. When we fail to let go of an affliction, it is because we have been unsteady in our effort or have grown lazy. Today, if we examine our own mind with wisdom and work at letting go of the afflictions, our mind will find peace.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Having, as just explained, contended with the enemy of affliction in every possible way, it is fitting and reasonable to confront it directly, because if one generates the wisdom that is its antidote and is able to destroy the seed of the affliction, then, since there is no cause for it to arise again, one must certainly generate courage. An ordinary enemy, even if defeated and driven from a land for the time being, may build up strength in another land and come to fight again at some point; but the way of the enemy of affliction is not like that — once the affliction has been defeated and the freedom of its abandonment attained a single time, there is no cause for it to arise again even when one meets with conditions later on, and it is in this great and distinctive difference that this is being said.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-45]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

By its own strength, an ordinary harmful enemy may be banished or driven, just once, from one's own land into another land; yet that same enemy may go to another, powerful land, settle there and take full possession of it, gather a large force of bandits and thieves and the like, and thereby build up and increase its strength and capacity — and then return to one's own land and do harm in return. But the way of the enemy of affliction is not like that ordinary enemy: once it has been abandoned through the noble path, there is no possibility of its returning again.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(This passage is trimmed of its trailing transitional sentence, which opens the next topic — the ultimate-truth style of meditating on the antidote.)*

As for abandoning the affliction because it has no basis on which to abide: if one thinks, "even though I can abandon this once, might its strength not grow again and it come back to harm me?" — ordinary worldly enemies are difficult to drive from their land, and even if one manages to drive them out once, they may settle in another land, take full possession of some strongly defended place that cannot be harmed by others, build up their might and strength there, and then return in order to take revenge and deal a decisive blow to the very enemy that first drove them out, and so do harm again. But the manner of the enemy of affliction is not like that: once it has arisen and been uprooted from the very root, seed and all, by the power of the antidote, what has been abandoned never returns, since there is no other place for it to take hold of and abide, and no possibility whatsoever of its strength growing and its counterattacking again. *(This is primarily how one meditates on the conventional antidote; the ultimate-truth style of meditation is set out in the following verse.)*

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If one asks: even if this affliction is abandoned once now, what is to be done should it return? — it cannot return. Were it like an ordinary worldly enemy, then, depending on the relative strength and numbers of the two sides at a given time, one might drive it out and expel it from the land without letting it settle — yet that enemy could go and settle in another land, take full possession of that region, gather many companions and allies of its own kind, and, once its capacity to harm one has grown, come back and, through vengeance, do great harm to one's life and possessions. But the way of the enemy of the afflictions such as attachment is not entirely like that: once it has been abandoned a single time by the antidote — the wisdom that realizes selflessness — there is, it is said, no possibility of its returning again.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Unlike an Ordinary Enemy, the Enemy of Affliction Has No Possibility of Returning Once Abandoned by the Noble Path

All the commentaries agree in explaining that, unlike an ordinary enemy who, driven from one land, may settle elsewhere, build up strength, and return to do harm, the enemy of affliction — once abandoned a single time — never returns.

###### 2. Gyaltsab, Sabzang, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Explain That Abandoning the Affliction from the Root Is the Work of the Noble Path or the Wisdom Realizing Selflessness

Since the affliction is destroyed from the root, there is no place left for it to abide, and no possibility whatsoever of its building up strength and counterattacking again.

###### 3. Kunzang Pelden, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Add Further Detail on How the Ordinary Enemy Returns

Kunzang Pelden explains that the ordinary enemy builds up strength by gathering a large force of bandits and thieves; Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that it takes possession of a strongly defended territory in order to take revenge and deal a decisive blow; Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that on its return it does great harm to one's life and possessions.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Notes That This Reasoning Is Primarily How One Meditates on the Conventional Antidote

The reasoning that the affliction can be abandoned because it has no basis on which to abide is primarily the manner of meditating on the conventional antidote; the manner of meditating on the ultimate antidote, he notes, is taught in the following verse.

###### 5. Sabzang Mati Panchen Notes That Even Driving Out an Ordinary Enemy Is Difficult

Since even ordinary worldly enemies are difficult to drive from their land in the first place, and such an enemy may yet return, this only underscores, by contrast, how much more thoroughgoing and stable the abandonment of the enemy of affliction by the noble path truly is.

###### 6. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Explains That This Verse Answers the Objection That the Enemy of Affliction Is, in Every Respect, Just Like an Ordinary Enemy

He explains that once the antidote, wisdom, has been generated and the seed of affliction destroyed, there is no cause for it to arise again — so one must certainly generate courage.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Although an ordinary worldly enemy, even once driven from a land, may settle in another land, build up strength, and return to do harm, all the commentaries agree that the enemy of affliction is not like that: once its seed has been uprooted from the root by the noble path or the power of the antidote, there is no possibility whatsoever of its returning again.

**Key points.**

- Once the seed of affliction is uprooted from the root by the noble path or the wisdom realizing selflessness, there is no other place for it to abide, and no possibility of its ever counterattacking again.
- An ordinary worldly enemy may take full possession of another, well-defended land, build up forces such as bands of thieves and bandits, and then return to take revenge on its former foe, doing great harm to life and possessions.
- Abandoning the afflictions through the reasoning that they have no basis on which to abide is primarily how one meditates on the conventional antidote.
- Even driving out an ordinary worldly enemy in the first place is extremely difficult.
- Once the antidote — wisdom — has been generated and the seed of affliction destroyed, there is no cause for its return even when one meets with conditions later on, so one must certainly generate courage.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

If one further asks how one could possibly manage to abandon it: the afflictions — arising merely from mistaken, improper conceptualization, hollow and without any essential core — are to be abandoned by looking at them with the eye of wisdom. #### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Although in Tibetan "affliction, affliction" is written as a single repeated term, in Sanskrit there are two separate words. The word "bharaka" carries the sense of austerity and weakness or faintness, while "kleśa" is used in the sense of an affliction that thoroughly disturbs one's peace. Since the two Sanskrit terms are distinct, the reduplicated form should be understood as "a faint, weak affliction." There are two ways of explaining this line — one based on the Tibetan, and one based on the Chinese. If one explains it as "abandon the affliction by the eye of wisdom that knows it to be an affliction," that follows the reading based on Tibetan; if one explains it as "abandon that faint, weak affliction by the eye of wisdom," that follows the reading based on Chinese. Since the Tibetan translation was made from the Chinese, the Chinese-based reading should be given priority. In this way, once the affliction is removed from one's own mind, there is nowhere else for it to go — it is like something that, once purified, simply dissolves of itself into the sphere of reality, its stains pacified. Nor is there any way for it, having settled somewhere, to gain strength again and come back to harm me at some later point — that possibility is closed off; it is only that one is unable to give rise to the noble wisdom in one's own mindstream because one's own mind is weak, and effort has therefore been lacking. Even though the affliction may seem to be something of such tremendous power, if one searches for its designated referent, nothing is to be found; and if one examines just what kind of nature this thing is that ruins us, one finds there is no independently established, self-sufficient mode of existence at all — "affliction" too is nothing more than a mere designation.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-46]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

How, then, is it to be abandoned? When examined and analyzed, afflictions such as attachment are easily destroyed — hence they are given the derogatory name "affliction," meaning something low and worthless — and they are to be abandoned by looking, with the eye of wisdom that knows they have no true nature, just as sunlight rising in a dark cavity dispels the darkness. Once removed from this mind of mine — which is like a temple — where would they go to some other place? There is no place for them to go; and having settled somewhere else and built up strength there, for what purpose would they come back to harm me again? There is no such coming back. So it is only that I, of weak and inferior wisdom, have made no effort in the past to abandon the afflictions — had I made such an effort, they would be easy to destroy, easier even than by some other enemy.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-46]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If the affliction is uprooted from the root a single time, there is no basis left for it to abide, because its root is mistaken conceptuality; so one must strive to abandon its very seed by the eye of wisdom that realizes emptiness. Once abandoned and cleared from this mind of mine, where would it go? And having settled somewhere, would it come back, having gained strength, to do me harm? That is impossible. And yet it is only my own weak mind that has, up to now, made no effort to uproot it from the root a single time — and this very lack of effort is precisely what does me harm.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-46]]

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination of the Text's Meaning)

The afflictions to be abandoned are to be examined and abandoned by the eye of wisdom, the antidote that knows the afflictions' nature to be empty — this is accomplished, by mundane wisdom, in the manner of suppressing the seed, and, by transcendent wisdom, in the manner of uprooting the seed from the root; and once the seed is abandoned, there is no cause for the affliction's manifest form ever to arise again. So once this enemy of affliction is cleared away from this mind of mine, where would it go to some other place? There is no place it can go; and having settled somewhere, would it come to harm me again? Since there is no one there to come and do so, it is only that I, of weak and inferior mind, have made no effort in the past to cultivate the antidote of discernment — had I made such effort, it would be easy to destroy, easier even than by some other enemy.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-46]]

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

As for this too: when the afflictions to be abandoned are examined as to their nature, they are easily destroyed, and are therefore indicated by the derogatory term "affliction." Such afflictions arise merely from mistaken conceptuality at the root and have no basis abiding by way of their own nature; so they are to be abandoned, whether by looking or examining with the eye of wisdom that realizes emptiness — the antidote — or simply by knowing their nature, through, in the case of mundane wisdom, suppressing their seed, and, in the case of transcendent wisdom, uprooting that seed from the very root. Once the seed is thus abandoned from the root, its manifest form can never again arise in one's mindstream — so, when such an object of abandonment is cleared by the antidote from this mind of mine, where would it go? There is no place for it to go; and having settled somewhere, would it gain strength and come to harm me again? There is no such possibility.

Even so, my mind's strength being weak, I wonder how I could possibly abandon these afflictions — becoming discouraged, I simply fail to make any effort; if instead I had the effort that delights in abandoning them, they would not fail to be abandoned, since they can be destroyed even more easily by other means as well. Or, alternatively: the antidote — the wisdom realizing selflessness — is itself weak in strength, so the effort needed to uproot the affliction once and for all, from the very root, has been lacking.

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam himself offers these two ways of construing "weak mind... effort has been lacking": either one's own resolve is weak and so one becomes discouraged and makes no effort, or the antidote — the wisdom realizing selflessness — is itself weak in force, hence the effort needed to uproot the affliction once and for all has been lacking; he states either reading is acceptable.)*

This way of explaining "affliction, affliction" follows the oral tradition of the venerable guru, and it relies on the great commentary's statement that "since this affliction has no root, it is to be abandoned merely by seeing reality itself — hence it is [called] affliction," and on the Detailed Exposition's statement, "these afflictions such as attachment..." — in other words, since merely looking at them with the eye of wisdom is enough to make them flee, they are given the derogatory name "affliction, affliction."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-46]]

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If one then thinks that these afflictions such as attachment have no antidote or means of abandoning them, that too is not so — there is indeed an antidote for what is to be abandoned. Since these afflictions such as attachment and aversion, when examined and analyzed, have no essence or nature of their own whatsoever, they are called "affliction"; being like a bubble, hollow and without any essential core, of the nature of mere conceptualization of the mind, they can be abandoned by looking at them with the eye of wisdom that realizes selflessness — just as, when the sun rises inside a dark house, all the darkness disappears without a trace, so, once the afflictions have been cleared from this very mind of mine, where would they go afterward? There is no place or object for them to go to. And as for "where would they dwell and come to harm me" — this means that there is no place where those afflictions could dwell and then come back to harm me again. It is only that, up until now, lacking wisdom, confused by delusion, and exceedingly weak, I have simply made no effort in the virtue that is the antidote for abandoning the afflictions — meaning that, were one to make the effort of that virtuous antidote, one would be able to abandon them.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Unlike the Ordinary Enemy, Once the Affliction Is Abandoned by the Eye of Wisdom a Single Time, There Is Nowhere for It to Come Back From

All the commentaries agree that, once one looks with the eye of wisdom and clears the affliction from one's own mind, it has nowhere to go and nowhere to abide from which it could come back and do harm again.

###### 2. Sabzang and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Explain the Distinction Between the Two Kinds of Wisdom

Mundane wisdom abandons the affliction by suppressing its seed, while transcendent wisdom abandons it by uprooting the seed from the root; once the seed is uprooted from the root, its manifest form can never arise again.

###### 3. The Derogatory Term "Affliction, Affliction" Shows That the Afflictions Have No True Nature and Are Easily Destroyed

When examined and analyzed, the afflictions are found to have no nature whatsoever, and are therefore named by this contemptuous, repeated term — a reading supported also by citations from the great commentary and the Detailed Exposition.

###### 4. Merely Looking with the Eye of Wisdom Dispels the Afflictions Without Remainder, Illustrated by the Simile of Sunlight Dispelling Darkness

Just as, when the sun rises inside a dark house, all the darkness disappears without a trace, so, the moment one looks with the eye of wisdom, the afflictions are explained to be abandoned.

###### 5. The Fault of Not Having Abandoned the Afflictions Lies Not in Any Power They Possess, but in a "Weak Mind"

Kunzang Pelden, Gyaltsab and Sabzang all explain that it is one's own weak mind that has, until now, made no effort; Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that, lacking wisdom and confused by delusion, one has simply made no effort in the antidote of virtue.

###### 6. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Gives Two Ways of Construing "Weak Mind... Effort Has Been Lacking"

Either one's own diligence is weak, leading to discouragement and no effort at all, or the antidote itself — the wisdom realizing selflessness — is weak in force, so that the effort needed to uproot the affliction once and for all from the root has been lacking; he holds either reading acceptable.

###### 7. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Concludes That Striving at the Antidote Makes Abandoning the Afflictions Possible

The reason the afflictions cannot be abandoned is not that they have any power, but that one has made no effort at the virtuous antidote — so if one strives at that antidote, one can indeed abandon them.

<!-- sub:divergences -->
#### ⚑ Divergences

- **kunpal, gyaltsab, sabzang, minyak-kunzang-sonam, khenpo-kunga:** "affliction, affliction" (the reduplicated word) is a single Tibetan term read as a term of contempt: since the afflictions have no essential nature when examined, calling them "affliction, [mere] affliction" belittles them as easily destroyed. ⚑
- **tenzin-gyatso:** the Tibetan reduplication in fact translates two distinct Sanskrit words — *bharaka* (weak/ascetic sense) and *kleśa* (violently disturbing sense) — giving two possible readings of the line: "know the affliction as affliction" (a Tibetan-based reading) versus "the faint/weak affliction" (the Sanskrit/Chinese-based reading); since the Tibetan translation follows the Chinese, His Holiness holds the latter reading should be given priority. ⚑

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Looking, with the eye of wisdom realizing emptiness or selflessness, at the afflictions — which have no true nature — and clearing them from one's own mind, they have nowhere to go and nowhere to abide from which to come back and harm one again; all the commentaries agree that the fault of not having abandoned the afflictions until now lies not in any power the afflictions possess, but in one's own lack of effort at the virtuous antidote.

**Key points.**

- ⚑ Kunzang Pelden, Gyaltsab and others hold that the afflictions, having no true nature, are given this belittling, repeated name because they are easily destroyed; His Holiness the Dalai Lama holds that the Sanskrit terms *bharaka* (weak) and *kleśa* (affliction) are in fact distinct, and that the reading "faint, weak affliction," following the Chinese-based explanation, should be given priority.
- Once the afflictions have been cleared from one's own mind by the eye of wisdom, there is no place for them to go, and no possibility of their settling elsewhere and coming back to harm one again.
- Just as, when the sun rises inside a dark house, all the darkness disappears without a trace, so, merely by looking with the eye of wisdom, the afflictions are said to be abandoned without remainder.
- Mundane wisdom abandons the afflictions by suppressing their seed; transcendent wisdom abandons them by uprooting the seed from the root.
- Not having abandoned the afflictions is due not to any great power they possess, but to one's own weak wisdom and lack of effort at the virtuous antidote.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains "weak mind... effort has been lacking" as meaning either that one's own diligence is weak, leading to discouragement, or that the antidote itself — the wisdom realizing selflessness — is weak in force; he holds either reading acceptable.
