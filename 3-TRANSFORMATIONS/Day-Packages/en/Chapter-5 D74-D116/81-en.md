## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the nineteenth and twentieth verses of the fifth chapter. Just as we carefully protect a wound on our body from being touched by others in a crowd, this teaching shows that we should protect our own mind out of fear of far greater suffering.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

For example, if we have a wound on our body while walking through a big crowd, we protect it carefully. We are afraid that someone will touch it or bump into it. In the same way, when we live among bad company, we must always guard the wound of our mind with mindfulness and awareness.

Someone who kills a louse caught between their nails will be reborn in the Crushing Hell, where the body must endure being pressed like sesame oil between two iron mountains. So if people carefully guard a small wound on the body out of fear of its small pain, then out of fear of the terrible suffering of hells like the Crushing Hell, we must guard the wound of the mind, the seed of the afflictions, all the more.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when I am among difficult people, I will guard the wound of my mind with mindfulness and awareness.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the Mind)* When we live among difficult people who stir up the afflictions, we must guard the wound of our mind, the seeds of the afflictions, with mindfulness and awareness, just as we guard a wound on the body. If we protect our mind this way and keep its wound from being touched, we will be freed from the great suffering of the lower realms. Our own mind will become calm and tamed.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Having understood in this way that everything depends on the mind, this states an example for making the mind steadfast: when one dwells amid an unruly, undisciplined crowd — people wild and unrestrained in the three doors of body, speech, and mind — just as one takes the utmost care to guard a wound, so too, even while dwelling among bad people who are a condition for the mind's distraction, this wound of the mind must always be guarded.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Ma-drang drolwa" ("unruly and dissolute") means untamed and wild in conduct. In Butön Rinpoche's detailed exposition it is said that although this term corresponds to a single Sanskrit expression, two different Tibetan renderings of it have appeared: the Omniscient Butön explains that it is rendered as both "ma-drang" (uncounted — i.e., lacking equanimity) and, since the mind is scattered, as "drolwa" (loosened, wandering off). In any case, when one dwells amid a rowdy, tumultuous crowd whose minds are not settled in equanimity and are distracted, one takes great care to guard a wound — for example, when going into a great marketplace, if one has a wound on one's arm or leg, one thinks, "Might someone touch it from this side, or bump against it from that side?" and so takes careful precaution. In just the same way, even while dwelling among bad people, this wound of the mind must always be held by mindfulness and awareness, and guarded.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-19]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

When one dwells amid a great, rowdy crowd whose minds are scattered outward and, lacking mindfulness and awareness, are unrestrained — that is, whose discipline of self-control has been broken and loosened — one strives with effort and takes careful precaution for fear that a wound on one's own body might be injured. In just the same way, even while dwelling among bad people — such as a place where anger arises amid savage, harsh people, or amid women where desire arises — and even while wishing to guard one's training, if this wound of the mind, which produces great suffering when injured, is not carefully guarded at all times, it is unacceptable. For example, just as a bodily wound, when it meets with adverse conditions, becomes damaged and festers, so too this mind of ours, when it meets with desirable or undesirable objects, comes to be corrupted by them, giving rise to desire and hatred; therefore, at all times and in every way, one must not dwell amid conditions that give rise to desire and hatred.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-19]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

"Unruly and dissolute" means: just as, when people who have a wound on the body dwell amid an undisciplined crowd of wild, unrestrained people, they take great care to guard the wound, so too, even while dwelling among bad people who are a condition for the arising of the afflictions, this wound of the mind must always be guarded. For if it is not guarded, that condition will cut off the life-force of higher rebirth and of liberation.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-19]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For example, when some people who have a wound dwell amid a great, undisciplined, unrestrained crowd — people not guided by the noble and whose conduct is not well restrained — they strive with effort and, fearing that the wound might be damaged, take careful precaution in order to protect it. In just the same way, even while dwelling amid bad people who engage in various improper acts at a place that is a support for the arising of the afflictions, and even while wishing to guard one's training, one must take careful precaution to guard, at all times, this wound of the mind, which produces great suffering when it is damaged.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-19]]

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-19]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Ma-drang" (uncounted, unruly) refers to children who, their minds scattered and lacking mindfulness and awareness, have not yet been counted among refined or elderly people — that is, the naturally thoughtless young children; "drolwa" (loosened, dissolute) refers to their conduct, unrestrained by any discipline, running freely at will. If, amid such a rowdy crowd, there is present an elderly person whose body carries a wound, a sore, or the like, and who is therefore at grave risk of severe injury, that person takes the utmost care and precaution, lest their wound be bumped or torn open by those unruly children's bad behavior. In just the same way, a person who dwells amid an extremely bad crowd that becomes a condition for the arising of desire and hatred must likewise at all times guard this wound of their own mind — that is, the ground of desire, anger, jealousy, and rivalry.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Since Everything Depends on the Mind, Its Wound Must Be Guarded Like a Bodily Wound Among a Rough Crowd

All the commentaries explain consonantly that just as one who has a wound on the body guards it carefully amid a rowdy, unrestrained crowd, so, when dwelling among bad people who produce the afflictions, one must always guard the wound of the mind.

###### 2. The Wound of the Mind Is the Seed of the Afflictions, and Leaving It Unguarded Cuts Off Higher Rebirth and Liberation

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain that the wound of the mind is the ground of desire, hatred, jealousy, and rivalry; if it is not guarded, that condition cuts off the life-force of higher rebirth and of liberation.

###### 3. Butön's Explanation of the Two Tibetan Renderings of "Ma-Drang Drolwa"

His Holiness the Dalai Lama, drawing on the Omniscient Butön's exposition, explains that a single Sanskrit expression came to be rendered by two different Tibetan terms — "ma-drang" (lacking equanimity) and "drolwa" (loosened through distraction) — both pointing to a wild, disorderly crowd.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Just as one who has a wound on the body carefully guards it while moving through an unruly, unrestrained crowd, so a bodhisattva dwelling among people who are a condition for the afflictions must always guard, with mindfulness and awareness, this wound of the mind.

**Key points.**

- **Guard the mind's wound as one guards a bodily wound.** Just as one who has a wound on the body guards it with the utmost care while amid a crowd of unrestrained conduct, so too, while dwelling among bad people who are a condition for the arising of the afflictions, one must always guard this wound of the mind with mindfulness and awareness.
- **What the wound of the mind is.** "The wound of the mind" refers to the ground of the afflictions — desire, hatred, jealousy, rivalry, and the like. Just as when a bodily wound is struck, pain and pus arise, so, when the mind meets with pleasant or unpleasant objects, desire and hatred increase.
- **The stakes of not guarding it.** If this wound of the mind is not guarded and one falls under the power of the afflictions, this severs the very life-force of higher rebirth and of liberation.
- **A textual note, flagged for review. ⚑** According to Butön Rinchen Drub's exposition, although "ma-drang drolwa" has two differing Tibetan renderings — "ma-drang" (lacking equanimity, hence unpleasant) and "drolwa" (the mind wandering off through distraction) — both must be understood as referring to a wild, disorderly crowd; for example, just as, when going into a great marketplace, one takes careful precaution for fear that someone else might strike the wound on one's arm or leg.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

By the mere small suffering of a bodily wound, one who is afraid takes care because of that wound; so those who are afraid of the mountains of the Crushing Hell crushing the body — how could they not guard this wound of the mind? It must be guarded.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

If, out of fear of even a minor wound on the body, one relies on careful precaution to protect it, then, when one recalls the terrors of the lower realms — such as being crushed by the Crushing Hell — there is no need even to mention that the wound of the mind must be guarded; it must certainly be guarded.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The proof of this is as follows: if, being afraid because the small suffering of a damaged bodily wound harms only this present life, one takes careful precaution for the sake of guarding the wound, then those who are afraid of the great suffering of having the body crushed and so forth amid the mountains of the Crushing Hell in the next life's hell — how could they not, through mindfulness and awareness, guard this wound of their own mind right now, preventing it from leaking outward? They must guard it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If it is fitting to take careful precaution to guard a wound out of fear of the small suffering of a bodily wound, then, being afraid of the crushing suffering of hells such as the Crushing Hell that comes about when the mind is damaged, how could one not guard this wound of the mind — that is, misdeeds and downfalls and the like? Reflecting on this great danger, one must guard it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Just as, when dwelling amid an unruly, dissolute crowd whose minds are distracted and whose conduct is unrestrained, one must strive with effort to take careful precaution for a wound, so too, even while dwelling among bad people who are a condition for the arising of the afflictions, if this wound of the mind, which produces suffering, is not guarded, it must always be guarded. For if, out of fear that the small suffering of a wound might cause harm, one takes precaution, then, being afraid of the body being crushed by the great mountains of the Crushing Hell — how could one not guard this wound of the mind that produces such suffering? It must be guarded.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

If, being afraid because the small suffering of an ordinary bodily wound's damage harms only this present life, one takes careful precaution for the sake of guarding the wound, then those who are afraid of the great suffering — such as being crushed amid the mountains of the Crushing Hell — in the hell of a future life, how could they not guard, through mindfulness and introspection, this wound of their own mind right now? It is fitting that they must guard it in every way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If, being frightened and afraid because leaving an ordinary wound unguarded produces small suffering when it is damaged, one takes careful precaution for the sake of guarding that wound, then — if the wound of the mind is not guarded, the afflictions arise, and under their power misdeeds and downfalls and the like occur, causing it to be damaged — in a future life one will go to the abode of the Crushing Hell, where great mountains bearing the heads of various creatures come from either side and crush the body between them: those who are afraid of these many thousands of severe sufferings must, relying on mindfulness and awareness, guard this wound of the mind by every possible means. For if it is not guarded and becomes damaged, this produces a danger far greater than the former one. On this point, the master Gélha explains it with the example of ram-shaped mountains coming from two directions and crushing lice and the like as they are pressed between them — how, he asks, could one not guard this wound of the mind? — while Vairocana states that, because one has crushed and killed a louse between one's fingernails, one is reborn in the Crushing Hell and must experience the suffering of being pressed between two iron mountains.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If, out of fear of the small suffering of a bodily wound in this life — such as the pain that comes from festering, swelling, and the like, by way of fearing that it might be bumped — one must take the utmost care and precaution regarding the wound, then a person who must be afraid of being reborn, in a future life, in the abode of the Crushing Hell — where yaks, sheep, pigs, and other creatures whose heads bear the likeness of whatever animals one has, in a past life, killed, come with their heads ablaze with fire, and one's own body as a hell-being is crushed and ground like sesame-oil pulp between them — how could such a person not guard, in this very life, against the danger of the wound of desire and hatred in their own mind? They ought to guard it in every way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-20]]

#### Metaphors and Examples (the small wound vs. the great crushing-hell wound)

In substance, this extends to its furthest point the same wound-metaphor as verse 5-19: if people, out of fear of even the small suffering of a damaged bodily wound, guard that wound carefully, then, being afraid of the crushing suffering of the body in the great mountains of the hell called the Crushing Hell (saṃghāta) in a future life, this becomes an *a fortiori* reason why the wound of the mind — that is, the seed of the afflictions — should be guarded. #### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. If a Small Bodily Wound Is Guarded Out of Fear, How Much More Must the Mind's Wound Be Guarded Out of Fear of Hell

All the commentaries explain consonantly, with an *a fortiori* reasoning, that if people carefully guard even a small bodily wound out of fear of its small suffering, then out of fear of the intense suffering of hells such as the Crushing Hell, they must guard the wound of the mind all the more.

###### 2. Leaving the Mind's Wound Unguarded Leads, Through the Afflictions and Downfalls, to Rebirth in the Crushing Hell

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain that if the wound of the mind is not guarded, the afflictions arise and, through them, misdeeds and downfalls occur; this is a grave danger, for it leads to rebirth in the Crushing Hell.

###### 3. Two Traditions Explain Why the Hell Is Called "Crushing"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites two explanatory traditions for the name "Crushing Hell": the master Gélha explains it by the example of ram-shaped mountains crushing a louse, while Vairocana explains it as the suffering of the body being ground between two iron mountains as karmic result of having crushed a louse between one's fingernails.

###### 4. A Further Note from Butön on the Two Tibetan Renderings of "Ma-Drang Drolwa"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam also brings in a further statement from Butön on the same point, already raised at 5-19, about the two differing Tibetan translations of the single term "ma-drang drolwa."

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** If, in this life, one is careful to guard even the small suffering of a bodily wound out of fear, then, out of fear of the great suffering of the body being destroyed in the Crushing Hell in a future life, one must guard this wound of the mind through mindfulness and awareness by every possible means.

**Key points.**

- **The a fortiori reasoning.** If, in this life, one is afraid of even the small suffering of a bodily wound and so avoids and guards the wound, then, being afraid of the great suffering of the body being crushed and so forth in the abode of the Crushing Hell in a future life, would one not guard this wound of the mind through mindfulness and awareness? One must guard it by every possible means.
- **How the wound of the mind leads to that rebirth.** If the wound of the mind is not guarded, the afflictions arise, downfalls occur, and one accumulates the evil karma of having killed sentient beings such as yaks, sheep, and pigs; as a result, in a future life one comes to experience, in the abode of the Crushing Hell, the suffering of being crushed like sesame-oil pulp between blazing mountains bearing the likenesses of the heads of the animals one killed in the past.
- **Two explanations of the hell's name. ⚑** Two differing explanations of the name "Crushing Hell" are cited: the master Gélha explains it as being crushed between two ram-shaped mountains, just as a louse is crushed; while Vairocana explains that, because one has previously crushed and killed a louse between one's fingernails, that karma causes one to be crushed, in a future life, between two iron mountains.
- **The conclusion.** If one avoids and guards even a small bodily wound, then it is established that there is no need even to mention that one must guard the wound of the mind, which protects against the terrors of the lower realms such as the Crushing Hell.
