## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 42 and 43 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. We spend most of our time on this life's living, profit, reputation, and comfort, and along the way we gather all kinds of harmful actions, some deliberately and much of it without noticing. Today's verses remind us how short and unstable this life is, and how much fear the results of bad actions bring at the time of death. They give us the resolve to confess what we have done and to stop trading it away for this life's small returns.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

Notice who Shantideva is speaking to. He calls them protectors. He already knew who they were and what they offered, and he went the careless way regardless. The confession is not that he lacked information. It is that having it changed nothing.

The phrase worth slowing down for is "such a danger," because Khenpo Kunzang Palden does not leave it vague. He separates it into three, in order. First, the terror as life is cut off at death. Second, after death, the terror of the bardo, the state between this life and the next. Third, in the next life, the terror of the lower realms. So this is not one shapeless dread. It is a sequence, and each stage has its own character.

Set that against what the misdeeds were actually for: subduing enemies, looking after our own people, food, money, things to enjoy. A little livelihood for one short life.

Then the verse stops arguing and shows us the man being led out to be maimed — the dry mouth, the fixed eyes, the colour gone from his face. If a human punishment does that, the commentaries ask, what should our own actions do? Therefore, feel regret. Regret is the part that is available today.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will not lie or mislead anyone for my own small advantage. I will stay straight with people.

**Explanation:** *(Avoiding wrongdoing)* Piling up wrongdoing for the sake of this life's profit and comfort is exactly the carelessness these verses confess, and it usually shows up in small forms rather than large ones. A convenient untruth to get out of something. Shading a fact so a deal goes our way. Letting someone believe what suits us. Today I will hold my ground in those moments instead. When the easy false answer is right there, I will give the true one, even if it costs me something. These small deceptions harm the other person and quietly harm us, and declining them once makes the next one easier to decline.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Calling out "O protectors!" to the objects of refuge, the Tathāgatas together with their heirs: as for me, on the strength of things that have no substance in them at all — my own means and wealth, my youth, my standing and so on, which merely look like good reasons — I have been heedless, not knowing what is to be taken up and what given up. … With a thought like "I am somebody," never taking an honest measure of myself, in a kind of dream-like daze, I have in any case fallen under the sway of heedlessness, with no protector and no refuge. That a terror like this would come — a dreadful situation in which there is nothing at all one can do — I did not know; and for the sake of the comforts of this life alone, which is impermanent by nature, I held wrongdoing to be of no account and committed a great deal of it. So, "how wretched I am" — he speaks in words of regret.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Crying out in anguish, "O protectors!", to the victorious ones together with their heirs, who possess great compassion: I, of evil karma — confused about what is to be taken up and what given up, and without the faith that places confidence in cause and effect — have fallen under the power of heedlessness in all three doors. ##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Calling out "O protectors!" to the objects of refuge: I, who have fallen under the power of a heedlessness that gets everything backwards — what to take up and what to turn away from, in all the conduct of the three doors — did not know and did not understand that great terrors like these existed, coming one after another in an unbroken chain: the suffering of life being severed at the point of death, the suffering of being seized and led away by the messengers of the Lord of Death, the suffering of falling into the lower realms after death, and the rest. And so, in the course of this short life, which is impermanent, does not last long and has not the least substance, merely to secure a livelihood — food, clothing, enjoyments and so on — and on account of enemies and relatives and the rest, I committed and accumulated a great many wrongdoings that were not needed at all and that carry great danger. <!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Moreover, being utterly terrified of one's own wrongdoing, one calls out "O protectors!" to the buddhas and bodhisattvas: "You who are the sublime objects of refuge, who give shelter and protection to the beings of the three realms of samsara from the sufferings of samsara — please look upon me, a person of wrongdoing." Having called out in this way: I, a person of wrongdoing who has fallen under the sway of heedlessness, did not know — or, before now, did not understand — that the results of wrongdoing have this nature and such as this: the time after death, the intermediate state, and the sufferings of the lower realms in the next life. And so, for the sake of this very life, impermanent and unstable, I actually carried out within my own being a great many wrongdoings — non-virtuous actions such as taking life, taking what is not given, and sexual misconduct. One should think: "That was a very grave fault indeed."

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Words of Regret: Though One Calls Out "O Protectors!" for Refuge, One Has Heaped Up Wrongdoing Out of Heedlessness

Zhenga's annotation commentary, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk make clear that even while calling out "O protectors!" to the victorious ones together with their heirs, one is confused about what is to be taken up and what given up, and has fallen under the power of heedlessness in all three doors.

###### 2. "Terrors Such as These" Means Three: The Final Severing at Death, the Delusive Appearances of the Intermediate State, and the Sufferings of the Lower Realms

Kunzang Pelden explains them as three distinct terrors: the terror of the final severing at the point of death, the terror of the intermediate state after death, and the terror of the lower realms in the next life. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and Minyak Kunzang Sonam likewise set out the same sequence — death and its aftermath, the intermediate state, and the sufferings of the lower realms.

###### 3. The Reason So Much Wrongdoing Was Accumulated: Merely for the Enjoyments of This Impermanent Life

Kunzang Pelden explains it as taking life and the like for the sake of subduing enemies, protecting kin, and food, wealth and enjoyments. Sabzang Mati Panchen likewise says it was done "merely to secure some small livelihood in this life."

###### 4. "So Much Wrongdoing" Is Identified as the Non-Virtuous Actions of Taking Life, Taking What Is Not Given, Sexual Misconduct and the Rest

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk glosses the meaning of "so much wrongdoing" as taking life, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct and so on.

###### 5. The Conclusion: Give Rise to Regret, and Strive at the Remedy, Virtue

Sabzang Mati Panchen concludes that, feeling intense regret at having accumulated wrongdoing, it is right to strive at the remedy, virtue. He cites the *Sutra of Advice to the King* to make clear that at the time of death there is no refuge other than the Dharma.

###### 6. A Closer Analysis: At the Root of Heedlessness Lies a Petty Conceit That Takes One's Own Passing Situation to Be Something Substantial

His Holiness the Dalai Lama analyses in detail the state of mind behind heedlessness — the thought "I am somebody" — a mind that treats a merely temporary standing as though it had real substance.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse is spoken in words of regret: although one calls out "O protectors!" to the victorious ones together with their heirs, one has fallen under the power of a heedlessness that is confused about what is to be taken up and what given up; and, not knowing that there are such terrors as the final severing at death, the delusive appearances of the intermediate state and the sufferings of the lower realms, one has heaped up a great many wrongdoings merely for the enjoyments of this impermanent life. All the commentaries explain it in agreement.

**Key points.**

- Even though one calls out "O protectors!" to the objects of refuge, the buddhas and bodhisattvas, one is confused about what is to be taken up and what given up, and has fallen under the power of heedlessness in all three doors.
- "Terrors such as these" is explained as three: the final severing at the point of death, the delusive appearances of the intermediate state, and the sufferings of the lower realms in the next life.
- The reason wrongdoing was accumulated is merely the enjoyments and livelihood of this impermanent life; and "so much wrongdoing" includes taking life, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct and the rest.
- As the *Sutra of Advice to the King* says — cited here by Sabzang Mati Panchen — at the time of death there is no other refuge, no other protector and no other dwelling than the Dharma.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama analyses in detail the mind that lies at the root of heedlessness: the thought "I am somebody," which takes a merely temporary standing to be something substantial.
- Sabzang Mati Panchen concludes that one should give rise to regret and strive at the remedy, virtue.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

When a man who has piled up offences is being led away to the place where limbs are cut and severed, panic and dread arise in him, and out of that a great fear and unease of mind is born. Under its power his whole appearance changes from what it was before — his mouth goes dry, his eyes are agitated; he cannot keep the composure he had earlier, and, gripped by dread, his body trembles and his skin turns pale and dull. And this, His Holiness explains, is no more than a punishment imposed within this life — it does not even part body from life — and yet that much panic comes over him. (Then, alongside this verse, he explains how the continuum of consciousness arises from earlier and earlier conditions, and sets out at length the direct reasoning that establishes past and future lives.)

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For example, when some grave offenders on whom the king's punishment has fallen are led away by men of like fortune to the place where merely a limb — a hand or a foot — is to be cut off, and that only today, they become utterly frightened and terrified and are struck with dread: their mouths hang open and go dry, the look in their eyes turns bad with a pale, fixed stare, their heads hang low, and so on. They are changed from what they were before, as though they were no longer the same men, and they appear utterly wretched.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Suppose someone says: what need is there to be so afraid and so anxious? It comes to everyone. — There is need. For when some wrongdoer on whom the king's punishment has fallen is led away today, by men of like fortune, to the place where limbs are cut off, then although what faces him has not even a fraction of the suffering of the lower realms, he becomes utterly terrified and struck with dread: he is tormented with thirst so that his mouth goes dry, the glow of his face fades, the look in his eyes turns bad — a wretched stare — his body grows dry and trembles, he cries out and laments, and so on; and he appears altogether changed in manner from the way he was before.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Suppose one thinks: however much wrongdoing one may have done, why should so much fear and terror arise? — It is fitting that fear and terror should arise. For example: in the world, when some person has been caught by the law of the king or the magistrate for having acted wrongly, and sentence has been passed that as punishment for the offence a limb — a hand or a foot — is to be cut off, and he is being led away to some other place where that cutting is to be done: that is the situation meant. Even in the moment when he is being led off to the place where limbs are cut, today, when they say the cutting is to happen right now, he himself becomes utterly dread-struck: dazed and terrified, his mouth goes dry, the look in his eyes turns very bad and he stares about in wretchedness, and so on. However brave and imposing a man he may have been before, now that the executioners have bound him with iron chains and ropes and treated him with contempt, in the moment when he is led off to the place where limbs are cut his courage fails him, he trembles, and he comes to be in a state of utter dread — that is how he appears. So, if a mere human sentence produces such a look of fear and terror as that…

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **a wrongdoer being led away to the place where limbs are cut off** (སྐྱེ་བོ་ཉེས་ཅན་ཡན་ལག་གཅོད་པའི་སར་ཁྲིད་པ) → an example of why fear and terror must arise on account of wrongful actions. They explain that even in the mere moment when the king's law leads an offender away to the place where limbs are cut off, great fear and terror arise — a dry mouth, a bad look in the eyes and the rest. Paired with the example of the messengers of the Lord of Death in verse 2-44, this shows the reason why one must be so afraid of the results of wrongdoing.

#### Scriptural Quotations

> First, once he has died and passed on, he is led away with no power of his own; and, suffering and ill at ease in mind, he enters a great and utter darkness, so that — like one being dragged off by savage men — he thinks in this way … and, further on: … and then he knows that a noose has been fastened around his own neck — and so forth.

— *Questions of Bhadrapāla* (བཟང་སྐྱོང་གིས་ཞུས་པ), cited by Minyak Kunzang Sonam as an illustration of the suffering of being dragged helplessly into darkness at the time of death

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Verse's Central Example — the Terror of a Wrongdoer Being Led Away to the Place Where Limbs Are Cut Off

All the commentaries agree in the end on the example given: even if an offender is merely being led away right now, by the king's law, to the place where limbs are cut off, he becomes utterly frightened and dread-struck.

###### 2. The Point of the Example — If a Merely Ordinary Punishment Causes That Much Fear, How Much More Must One Fear the Results of Karma

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk adds the link, "if a mere human sentence produces such a look of terror as that," joining it to the example of the punishment of the Lord of Death in verse 2-44.

###### 3. The Purpose It Serves — Giving Rise to Regret for Wrongdoing

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen states it directly at the close of his explanation of the two examples in verses 2-43 and 2-44: "Therefore one should feel regret for wrongdoing."

###### 4. The Bodily and Verbal Signs of Terror — a Dry Mouth, a Bad Look in the Eyes, the Glow Gone From the Face

Kunzang Pelden describes the signs in detail: the mouth hanging open and going dry, the look in the eyes turning bad with a pale, fixed stare, the head hanging low, and so on. His Holiness the Dalai Lama adds that the skin turns pale and dull.

###### 5. The *Questions of Bhadrapāla* Is Cited as an Illustration of the Suffering of Dying

Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes the *Questions of Bhadrapāla* on the suffering of being led off helplessly into darkness at death and of knowing that a noose has been fastened around one's own neck, setting it out so that example and meaning match.

###### 6. His Holiness the Dalai Lama's Addition — the Continuum of Consciousness and the Proof of Past and Future Lives

Taking this verse's example of terror as his starting point, he explains at length how the continuum of consciousness arises from earlier and earlier conditions, and the reasoning that establishes past and future lives, thereby setting out the reason why there is so much to fear at the time of death.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse gives the example of an offender under the king's law: if he were merely being led away right now to the place where limbs are cut off, great fear and terror would already arise in him — a dry mouth, a bad look in the eyes and the rest. Paired with the example of the punishment of the Lord of Death in verse 2-44, this shows the reason why one must be so afraid of the results of karma; and all the commentaries agree in the end on this explanation.

**Key points.**

- All the commentaries explain in one voice that even in the mere moment of being led away to the place where limbs are cut off, great terror arises — a dry mouth, a bad look in the eyes and so on.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk adds the link, "if a mere human sentence produces such terror as that," joining it to the example of the punishment of the Lord of Death in verse 2-44.
- Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen states the purpose of this example directly: "Therefore one should feel regret for wrongdoing."
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes the *Questions of Bhadrapāla* on the suffering of being helpless at the time of death, setting it out so that example and meaning match.
- Taking this verse's example of terror as his starting point, His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives an extended supplementary explanation of the continuum of consciousness and the reasoning that establishes past and future lives.
