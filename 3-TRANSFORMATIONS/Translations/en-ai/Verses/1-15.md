## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three commentators reach a strong consensus that the conventional mind of enlightenment (bodhicitta), when condensed by way of its essence, is divided into two distinct aspects: the aspiring mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-praṇidhi-citta* / *བྱང་ཆུབ་སྨོན་པའི་སེམས་*) and the engaging (or venturing) mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-prasthāna-citta* / *བྱང་ཆུབ་འཇུག་པ་*). This division corresponds to the progression from the initial aspiration to the actual practice of the Bodhisattva conduct.

### 1. Defining the Aspiring and Engaging Minds
*   **The Core Division:** The commentators establish clear definitions for these two minds based on the practitioner's internal orientation:
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** defines the general Mahayana generation of the mind as "a mind conjoined with the aspiration that seeks perfect Enlightenment for the sake of others." He notes that while bodhicitta can be divided into twenty-two types when conjoined with companions (following the *Abhisamayālaṅkāra*) or four types by way of boundaries (belief, pure exceptional resolve, etc.), the division into aspiring and engaging represents the most fundamental division of its conventional essence.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** defines the aspiring mind as having the essence of "the desire to attain great awakening," and the engaging mind as "actually engaging in the conduct of awakening motivated by that aspiration."
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly explains that the wishing attitude desiring to attain Buddhahood for the sake of others is the aspiring mind, whereas actually entering the path and wishing to practice that path is the engaging mind.
*   **The Analogy of the Traveler (Verse 1-16):**
    *   **Consensus Analogy:** To illustrate the distinction, all three commentators rely on the classical analogy of traveling: just as there is a clear difference in thought and physical action between a person who merely desires or yearns to travel to a specific destination (while remaining in bed or still at home) and a traveler who has actually entered the road and is actively journeying (lifting and placing down their feet), so too is the difference between the aspiring and engaging minds of enlightenment.
    *   **Conjoining with Practice:** Gyaltsab Je and Sazang Mati Panchen clarify that although both minds share the identical thought of wanting to attain the state of a Buddha for the sake of others, they are distinguished by whether they depend on being conjoined with the actual practice of the perfections, such as generosity.

### 2. Difference in Benefits and the Continuous Flow of Merit (Verses 1-17, 1-18)
*   **The Limit of the Aspiring Mind:** The commentators agree that although generating merely the aspiring mind toward awakening brings immense fruits within cyclic existence—such as obtaining the perfect splendor of gods and humans—it does not produce a continuous, uninterrupted flow of merit.
*   **The Power of the Engaging Mind:** From the moment a practitioner correctly takes up the engaging mind through the proper ritual with an irreversible resolve to completely liberate infinite, boundless realms of sentient beings, a vast, inexhaustible force of merit arises. This powerful stream of merit flows continuously and uninterruptedly, equal to the expanse of space, even when the practitioner is asleep, distracted, unmindful, faints, or becomes intoxicated.
*   **Scholastic Explanations:**
    *   **Cause vs. Fruit:** Gyaltsab Je explains this difference in benefits: the aspiring mind merely seeks the fruit (Enlightenment), whereas the engaging mind is conjoined with accomplishing the complete causes of Buddhahood (the practice of the perfections). Thus, only the latter generates continuous merit.
    *   **The Three Disciplines:** Sazang Mati Panchen explicitly attributes this continuous flow to the "three disciplines" (meaning the three moral disciplines of a Bodhisattva: restraining negative conduct, gathering virtuous dharmas, and accomplishing the benefit of sentient beings).

### 3. Rituals, Precepts, and Doctrinal Debates
*   **Ritual Requirements:**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** notes that a mere aspiring mind can arise without relying on a ritual, but the firm resolve to never abandon it until Buddhahood (grasped by the hook of commitment) is explained by Lord Atiśa to be adopted through a ritual. The engaging mind and its vows must definitely be taken through the proper ritual.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Ngulchu Thokme** confirm that taking up the engaging mind and its vows requires the proper ritual with an irreversible resolve.
*   **Precepts and Sources:**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** refers the reader to Je Tsongkhapa's *Great Stages of the Path of the Perfections* (*Lamrim Chenmo*) for the detailed precepts of the aspiring mind (such as training in the causes for not letting it decline, the four white deeds, and avoiding the four black deeds). For the engaging mind's precepts, he refers to Tsongkhapa's *Commentary on the Ethics Chapter of the Bodhisattva Grounds* (*Wati* / *Detailed Explanation of the Bodhisattva-bhūmi*).
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** similarly points to "the ethics chapter of the Bodhisattva Grounds" (the Bodhisattva vows) as the basis of the engaging mind's practice.
*   **Doctrinal Refutations (Gyaltsab Je):**
    *   **Mind Generation in a Buddha:** Gyaltsab Je strongly refutes the view that a Buddha lacks Great Vehicle mind generation. He argues that if a Buddha lacked the engaging mind, they would also lack the engaging vows (including Vinaya and Tantric vows), which would sever the continuity of vows in the Buddha's state.
    *   **Conventional vs. Ultimate:** Gyaltsab Je clarifies that conventional bodhicitta focuses on the conventional bodies of a Buddha (like the Form Body), while ultimate bodhicitta directly realizes the ultimate mode of existence (emptiness/suchness). Although ultimate bodhicitta is called "mind generation" in some texts, it is not the actual conventional Great Vehicle mind generation defined here.

### 4. Scriptural Proofs
*   **The Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra (Maitreya's Biography):**
    *   All three commentators cite the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* to support the division and the unique value of the aspiring mind.
    *   **The cracked diamond analogy:** Ngulchu Thokme and Gyaltsab Je cite Maitreya's famous analogy from the sūtra: just as a cracked diamond still outshines all golden ornaments and does not lose its name or fail to dispel poverty, the diamond of generating the mind of enlightenment (even when separated from actual practice) outshines all the golden qualities of Śrāvakas and Pratyekabuddhas, does not lose the name of a Bodhisattva, and dispels the poverty of samsara.
    *   **The Rareness of the Engaging Mind:** Ngulchu Thokme and Sazang Mati Panchen quote Maitreya's words: "Those who wish to attain perfect awakening are rare, but those who actually enter into the conduct of perfect awakening are even more rare."
*   **The Subāhuparipṛcchā Sūtra:**
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Ngulchu Thokme** cite the *Subāhuparipṛcchā Sūtra* (Sūtra of Subāhu's Questions) to logically establish the continuous, uninterrupted arising of merit for the engaging mind. The sūtra explains that for one who dons the armor of wanting to benefit infinite sentient beings, their virtuous roots grow and increase day and night, even when they are asleep or heedless.
