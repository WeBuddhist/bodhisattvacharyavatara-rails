## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the last two verses of the fourth chapter, verses 47 and 48. From this example of the afflictions being like an illusion, and a patient needing to take medicine, let us all join together today in actual practice.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a story that is easy to understand and encouraging. For example, a sick person may find a skilled and kind doctor and receive good medicine. But if that person does not drink the medicine and does not follow the right diet, the illness will not be cured. This is not the fault of the doctor or the medicine. It is the patient's own fault. In the same way, the Buddha is like the doctor, the holy Dharma is like the medicine, the afflictions such as craving and hatred are like the illness, and we are like the patient. The *Chandrapradipa Sutra* also says that even if a skilled doctor gives medicine, if the patient does not drink it, they cannot be freed from the illness. So merely hearing the Dharma does not help us. We must put it into actual practice.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will actually carry out in practice one piece of advice I have learned.

**Explanation:** *(Diligence Practice)* If a patient does not listen to the doctor's advice, medicine cannot cure the illness. In the same way, merely hearing the Dharma does not help. We must put it into actual practice. If we take this advice to heart today, it will greatly help clear away the afflictions in our own mind.

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^4-47`):*

> These emotions do not actually live out in the world, or in our senses, or anywhere in between, or anywhere else at all — so where exactly are they, that they can cause every being so much harm? They are like an illusion. So let go of the fear in your heart, and instead put your effort into understanding this clearly. Why would you let yourself suffer needlessly in the lower realms?

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

As for how one is to view this with the eye of wisdom: the afflictions do not dwell in attractive objects and the like, since even when the faculties perceive such an object, no affliction arises in those whose senses are restrained; and on careful analysis, not even the smallest particle of such an object is established, so the afflictions do not dwell in the object. Nor do they dwell in the sense faculties, since even while the faculties are present — as when one is reflecting on the Dharma and so on — no affliction arises; and since the faculties themselves have no inherent nature, the afflictions do not dwell in the gathering of the faculties either. Nor, in that same moment, does consciousness give rise to affliction, even though consciousness is present — and since consciousness itself lacks any essential nature, the afflictions do not dwell in between the object and the faculty either. And since they are not found anywhere else than these, one must ask: where, then, do the afflictions dwell, from which they go on to harm all beings? This is exactly like how, through the power of a magician's spell, things appear as if they were horses, elephants, and so forth. Therefore, one should let go of the fear in one's heart and rely on diligence in order to understand the true nature of the afflictions — for it would be utterly pointless to let oneself be harmed in the hells and the preta realm and so forth for no reason at all.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

*(Trimmed of a verbatim repetition of the root verse that precedes "for example.")*

Something like craving-attachment arises only from prior habituation, an observed condition, and discursive agitation of thought and the like — if one searches for what it is, taken as a designated referent, there is no way of abiding that could stand on its own. And on examination: it does not dwell in the object of attachment. Likewise it does not dwell in the sense faculty itself — the eye faculty and so on — which serves as its own supporting condition. Nor does it dwell in between, at the point where faculty and object meet. Nor, as I said, does it dwell "elsewhere" — yet it is not the case that it exists somewhere entirely disconnected from these either. It causes harm to all beings from wherever it dwells — yet when one searches, it is nowhere to be found. So this is exactly the situation: the way the afflicted enemies appear to the mind is one thing, but when one searches for the designated referent, one ought to find it — and yet one does not. Does that mean it is simply untrue, and therefore does not exist at all? No — since benefit and harm are, in actual fact, connected to it, it is not nonexistent. As for its manner of abiding: although it is not established from the side of the object, and exists merely through the power of naming, it is not the case that, in the way it appears, it appears merely through the power of naming — rather, when it appears as though established from the side of the object, that appearance, though untrue, like an illusion, exists through the power of conditions; it exists in the manner of dependent origination. That is why, when it meets with a conducive condition, it arises, and when it meets with an opposing condition, it ceases and passes away. However powerful an affliction may become through habituating oneself to what is discordant with it, it can still change and pass away through the power of conditions, and it can be overcome — so, being capable of abandoning it and yet failing to abandon it, and thereby, for no purpose or reason whatsoever, falling under the sway of the afflictions and inflicting on oneself the suffering of the hells and other lower realms — this, he says, is utterly unreasonable in every respect. On the strength of this, one must not become discouraged, but must rely on diligence, through knowing the actual nature of things, in order to abandon the afflictions. If one takes "searching for the designated referent" to refer to the abiding nature, or actual nature, of phenomena, then, as the venerable Milarepa said:

> If one takes ultimate truth as one's standard,
> then even buddhahood itself, along with obstructing spirits, does not exist.

This means: if one takes ultimate truth as one's standard, then not only obstructing spirits, but even buddhahood, and even the nature of reality itself, do not exist, as he says. There is a sense of "ultimate" that refers to what is to be negated within the ultimate, and a sense of "ultimate" that refers to the mind that cognizes the ultimate — here it is used in the former sense, referring to what is to be negated within the ultimate.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

As for how one should look, with the eye of wisdom, at the nature of the afflictions: whenever craving, aversion, and the like arise in one's own mindstream, rather than falling under their power, one must examine them in the following way. Afflictions such as craving and aversion do not dwell on the side of external objects — enemies, friends, and so on — since such afflictions can arise even without those objects being present. If they did dwell there, then whoever else happened to see that same object ought likewise to develop craving or aversion the way I do — but since that does not happen, the affliction is not there. Nor do they dwell internally, in one's own sense faculties or in the collection of consciousnesses, since even when the faculties and consciousnesses are present together, no affliction may arise. If it did dwell there, then affliction would have to arise toward absolutely everything one sees or hears — but since that does not happen, it is not there either. Nor do they dwell in between the object and the faculty, since that intervening space is simply empty like the sky — there is no object there at all in which anything could dwell. And since it is not possible for them to dwell or exist in anything else — whether in other sentient beings, or inside some container, or anywhere at all — one must ask: from what place, then, do these afflictions, which have been our enemies since beginningless time, cause harm to all beings, myself included, in this life and every life to come? On examination and analysis, this affliction appears but has no established inherent nature — like something arisen from conditions, appearing to one's own mind, yet lacking inherent nature: since these three characteristics are all present, its unreal appearance is exactly like an illusion. Being something that arises from conditions such as enemies and friends, that appears to one's own mind, and that lacks inherent nature — for this very reason, one should abandon the fear in one's heart that thinks the afflictions cannot be uprooted, and, rather than chasing after the object the way a dog chases the stone thrown at it, one should — like the lion who turns on the one who threw the stone — directly identify whatever thought of craving, aversion, or other affliction arises in one's own mind, and search into where it first came from, where it presently abides, and where in the end it goes; through such investigation of its origin, present location, and destination, one generates and sustains the diligence needed to understand that the afflictions lack inherent nature. Since they are, in this way, easy to abandon precisely because they lack inherent nature, failing to make such an effort and instead needlessly allowing oneself to be harmed with the suffering of the hells and other lower realms is truly senseless — do not let it happen, he says. In this connection, it is said in the noble Ratnakūṭa: "In future times there will arise monks, full of manifest pride, who resemble a dog chasing a stone. Kāśyapa, in what way do they resemble a dog chasing a stone? When frightened by that very stone, a dog chases after the stone itself, not after the one who threw it. Kāśyapa, thus it is that certain renunciants and brahmins apply themselves diligently to the individual analysis of forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tactile objects, and, though they may come to realize these as impermanent, deceptive, and perishable in nature, they do not know from what these things arise; so even if they go to a village, a city, an open settlement, a district, a country, or a royal palace, they will still be harmed by forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tactile objects. Even should they meet death while dwelling in solitude, those who abide by mere worldly restraint will be reborn into a heavenly world, and there too they will be harmed by the five qualities of sensory desire belonging to that realm. Having passed on from there, they still will not be completely freed from the lower realms — that is to say, from the hell realms, the animal realm, the world of the Lord of Death, and the realm of the asuras. In this way, such renunciants are like the dog chasing the stone. Kāśyapa, in later times, renunciant practitioners of yoga unlike the dog chasing the stone are, for example, like a lion at whom a stone is thrown: knowing where the stone came from, the lion does not chase the stone, but instead goes after the one who threw it, so that the stone will not be thrown again. In just the same way, a renunciant who practices yogic conduct, upon perceiving an external quality of sensory desire, recognizes at that very moment that it arises from the mind, examines the mind itself, and thereby comes to know it as without inherent existence, and so attains liberation."

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(This source's span for verse 4-47 runs, in the vault's segmentation, unusually long — continuing past its own chapter-summary heading, "Third, the summary," and further chapter-boundary material, all the way to the start of chapter 5, since this source stamps no separate transclusion marker for verse 4-48. Only the portion actually analyzing this verse — where the afflictions do or do not abide, concluding that they are illusion-like — is used here.)*

Suppose one objects: since the afflictions too arise from their own causes and are established by way of an inherent nature, then not even a single one of them can be abandoned. In response: the afflictions do not dwell, by way of inherent nature, in objects such as form and the rest — for if they did, then even arhats would have to give rise to affliction upon seeing form and so on, which is absurd. Nor do they dwell in that manner in the collection of the faculties, such as the eye, since even while the eye is present, at the time one is reflecting on the meaning of the abiding nature, no affliction is observed. Nor do they dwell in between these two, nor do they dwell anywhere else — so, once uprooted, where could these things — supposedly established by way of inherent nature — dwell, that they should go on to harm all beings? Since they are not found dwelling anywhere at all — this affliction, though empty of establishment by way of inherent nature, appears as though established by way of inherent nature, just like an illusion. At the time it so appears, one should abandon the fear in one's heart that thinks, "Since it is established by way of inherent nature, I will not be able to uproot it," and instead generate the wisdom that realizes emptiness, relying on diligence in order to abandon the afflictions — it is proper to act in just this way. Given that one is capable of this, it is unreasonable to needlessly allow oneself to be harmed in the hells and other lower realms by failing to make that effort. This point — that once the earlier afflictions are uprooted at the root, they no longer dwell in those places — has already been explained above as well.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Ngulchu Thokme folds verses 4-45, 4-46, and 4-47 together, quoting all three verses as one combined block and then giving a single unified explanation for all three; the same combined passage is also referenced on the pages for 4-45 and 4-46. In this source's segmentation, the quoted verse-block is stamped `^4-91` and the unified explanation `^4-93`; that stamped anchor is cited here in place of "`^4-47`".)*

An ordinary enemy, even once driven from one place, may still dwell in another place, and, having recovered there and built up strength again, may return and cause harm once more. The afflicted enemy is not like that: once uprooted for good, there is no possibility of its returning. How, then, is it to be abandoned? Since this affliction, on examination, is easily overcome, it is called "affliction" as a term of contempt. As the Great Commentary says: "since this affliction has no root, it is to be abandoned merely by seeing the ultimate truth, and for that reason it is called 'affliction,'" and as the Detailed Exposition says: "these afflictions, such as craving..." It is to be abandoned by looking at it with the eye of wisdom that realizes selflessness; and once it is cleared from one's own mind, where could it possibly go? There is nowhere for it to go. And from where could it come back, having recovered strength, to harm me again? There is no such place from which it could come. Therefore, it is only a person of weak resolve who, lacking diligence, fails to abandon it even though it is possible to do so. As for how one is to look with the eye of wisdom: the afflictions do not dwell in objects such as form; nor do they dwell in the collection of the eye faculty and so on, since even when these are present, the affliction may fail to arise. Nor do they dwell in between the object and the faculty, nor do they exist anywhere else, since they are not observed there. Therefore, one must ask where these afflictions dwell, from which they go on to harm all beings. On examination, they are not actually established as harm-doers, and yet they appear as harm-doers — so this is just like an illusion: though it appears, it lacks any true existence. Therefore, abandon the fear in your heart that thinks it cannot be overcome, and rely on diligence in order to generate wisdom. Failing to do so, and thereby needlessly allowing oneself to be harmed in the hells and other lower realms — this is unreasonable; so, either "do not act in this way," or simply, "do not do it," is the meaning.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

When properly examined and analyzed with reasoning, the afflictions, being without inherent nature, do not dwell in external objects such as form; nor do they dwell in the collection of internal faculties such as the eye; nor do they dwell in between the object and the faculty; and since they do not dwell anywhere other than these three, one must ask: where, then, do they dwell, such that they cause harm to all beings — the agent of harm itself being unestablished? Though not established ultimately, this affliction — which, at the level of mere conventional truth, harms all beings wherever it dwells — appears though it does not exist, just like an illusion. Therefore, abandon the fear in your heart that thinks it cannot be uprooted, and it is fitting to rely on diligence in order to generate the wisdom that knows the true nature, the actual antidote. Since one knows this truth — that it is not truly existent — it is unreasonable to allow oneself to be needlessly harmed by the suffering of the hells and other lower realms.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-47]]

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Suppose one thinks: even with diligence, merely looking with the eye of wisdom cannot abandon the afflictions. In response: when examined and analyzed with correct reasoning, the afflictions do not, by way of inherent nature, dwell on external objects such as form; nor do they, by way of inherent nature, dwell in the collection of internal faculties, such as the eye faculty; nor do they, by way of inherent nature, dwell in between the object and the faculty; and since they do not, by way of inherent nature, dwell anywhere other than these three, one must ask where these afflictions, or anything established by way of inherent nature, could dwell, such that they go on to harm all beings — since they do not dwell above, below, or anywhere else, and since the harmed and the harmer are, in every respect, unestablished by way of inherent nature. Once one understands and realizes, exactly as reasoning has determined, that they are in this way unestablished by way of inherent nature, one is able to abandon them then and there.

If the afflictions dwelt in objects such as form, then even arhats, upon seeing such objects, would have to give rise to craving or aversion depending on whether the object seemed attractive or unattractive — but since this does not happen, they do not dwell there. If they dwelt in the collection of the faculties, then even while meditating on the meaning of the true nature, when the eye and so forth are present, no affliction would be observed — so they do not dwell there either. By this demonstration that they do not dwell by way of inherent nature, one should also come to understand that, by way of inherent nature, there is no arising or ceasing. This accords with what is said in the Questions of Adhyāśaya, that one must abandon self-grasping affliction by meditating on the meaning of selflessness; and the master Śāntarakṣita likewise says:

> One who is skilled in the selflessness of phenomena,
> through habituation to the absence of inherent nature,
> abandons without obscuration
> the afflictions that arise from mistaken conception.

and:

> When the fault that comes from clinging to things as real —
> attachment, aversion, and the rest —
> the very enemies — are thoroughly overturned,
> how could they possibly arise again?

The meaning of this is: since the afflictions arise in dependence on the clinging to things as truly existent — that is, grasping at real existence — once one realizes the absence of true existence, they will cease of their own accord, just as smoke subsides once the fire is put out. And this clinging to things as truly existent, the object of that grasping, is to be abandoned by dispelling it through correct reasoning; how this is to be done will be explained below, in the context of the wisdom chapter. Here, the teaching that the afflictions do not dwell anywhere refers to their not dwelling anywhere ultimately, and the earlier explanation that they dwell in one's own mind is given from the standpoint of conventional, worldly usage — so there is no contradiction. These are to be meditated on as afflictions not established ultimately.

As for meditating on them, conventionally, as being like an illusion: these afflictions, although they appear as though established by way of inherent nature, are, since they are empty of establishment by way of inherent nature, false — like an illusion that appears through the power of mantra and substance and the like, though it does not exist. Therefore, having abandoned the fear in one's heart that thinks, "since this is truly established, how could I possibly abandon it," one should — or, having abandoned it, one should — rely on diligence in order to generate the wisdom that realizes the true nature capable of knowing this antidote's own nature, and strive accordingly. [The passage may also be construed as:] "or: it is fitting to rely on diligence in order to generate the wisdom that realizes the true nature, the antidote." Failing to act in this way, and, though capable of abandoning the afflictions, not abandoning them, and thereby, for no purpose or reason whatsoever, coming completely under the power of the afflictions, and needlessly allowing oneself to be harmed by the suffering of the hells and other lower realms — this, in every respect, is unreasonable. These points are the manner of practicing conscientiousness by reflecting on the afflictions to be abandoned; and this conscientiousness, relying on its antidote, functions to halt the activity of the afflictions to be abandoned, as is extensively taught in the *Tathāgatācintyaguhya Sūtra*:

> Furthermore, what is conscientiousness? It is: taming one's own mind, guarding the minds of others, dispelling delight in the afflictions, engaging, through delight in the Dharma, in what follows from it, having no activity of discursive thought toward desire, no activity of discursive thought toward malice, no activity of discursive thought toward harmfulness... having no activity of improper mental engagement, and having no activity whatsoever of any wrongdoing or unwholesome quality — this is called conscientiousness.

— and so on, as taught there at length.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

*(Trimmed of a verbatim repetition of the root verse preceding "This means:"; the final line's standalone repetition at the very end of the span, which carries no additional gloss, is likewise omitted.)*

If, examining and searching with the eye of wisdom for where the afflictions such as craving and aversion dwell, one asks whether they dwell among the five objects — form, sound, smell, taste, and tactile sensation — these afflictions do not dwell there, since that same object may also serve as an object for other persons or beings, who see it or hear of it, and yet affliction such as craving does not arise in all of them equally; so it does not dwell on the side of the object. If, then, one asks whether it dwells on top of one's own eye, ear, and other faculties, and the corresponding consciousnesses — the visual consciousness, auditory consciousness, and so on — that too is not the case, since affliction does not arise in every single instance in which these faculties and consciousnesses are present. If, then, one asks whether the afflictions dwell in the space in between the faculties (such as the eye) and the objects (such as form), that intervening space, being of the nature of empty space, provides nothing whatsoever that could be seen or felt as the dwelling place of these afflictions, so they are not there either. For example: at the moment some affliction such as craving or aversion has arisen, if one looks slowly at its very nature and asks, "Where exactly does it reside? Is it in my head, my body, my hands? In the outer skin? In the flesh within? In the bones, or the marrow within them? In the stomach's inner lining, or in the intestines and so on?" — searching and probing in this way, one will not find it. Therefore, since it does not dwell anywhere other than these either, one must ask: from what place do all these afflictions, such as craving, dwell and thereby cause harm to sentient beings across all times and in every way? If one actually pursues this to the end, one finds that they have no inherent existence, no place of dwelling, nowhere to come from, and nowhere to go — and being of just this character, these afflictions are exactly analogous to the illusory appearances of horses, elephants, armies, chariots and the like that are conjured through a magician's mantra, medicines, and minor auxiliary conditions. For this very reason, one should abandon every trace of the fear in one's heart, or the cowardice or dread, that thinks "I cannot abandon these" — and, in order to understand the true nature and manner of abiding of these afflictions such as craving, one should cultivate diligence in wholesome practices such as study and reflection, relying on diligence for that purpose.

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The illusion (སྒྱུ་མའི་དཔེ) — the afflictions explained as illusion-like** → How the afflictions, though not established by inherent nature, appear as harm-doers.
  Both Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk draw on the example of horses, elephants, armies, and chariots conjured through a magician's mantra (and medicines and other auxiliary conditions) to explain how mistaken appearances arise. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that for the afflictions to be illusion-like, three conditions must all be present: they arise from conditions, they appear to one's own mind, and they lack inherent nature. Both Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Sabzang Mati Panchen likewise explain that the afflictions, though empty of establishment by inherent nature, appear as though established by inherent nature — just like an illusion. Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains them as a falsehood that appears, though it does not exist, through the power of mantra and substance. His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that they exist through the power of conditions, in the manner of dependent origination, and are thus not merely nominal.

- **Smoke subsiding once the fire is put out** → How the afflictions cease of their own accord once one realizes the absence of true existence.
  Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains the meaning of Śāntarakṣita's verses: since the afflictions arise in dependence on grasping at things as truly existent, once true existence is seen to be absent, they cease of their own accord — just as smoke subsides once a fire has been put out.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Fourfold Analysis: The Afflictions Do Not Dwell in the Object, the Faculties, In Between, or Anywhere Else

All the commentaries agree in explaining that the afflictions do not dwell in external objects, nor in the internal collection of the sense faculties, nor in between the two, nor anywhere else at all; on this fourfold analysis, they are shown to lack inherent nature.

###### 2. Though Unestablished Anywhere, the Afflictions Appear as Harm-Doers — Hence They Are Illusion-Like

Although the afflictions are not established in any place of dwelling whatsoever, they appear to harm all beings — an appearance exactly like the illusory horses and elephants conjured by a magician's mantra. Sabzang Mati Panchen explains that the agent of harm itself is thereby shown to be unestablished.

###### 3. One Must Abandon the Fear of Being Unable to Overcome the Afflictions and Rely on Diligence to Generate the Wisdom That Knows Their True Nature

Having abandoned the terror that thinks the afflictions, being truly established, cannot be uprooted, one must rely on diligence to generate the wisdom that realizes their absence of inherent nature — all the commentaries agree on this point.

###### 4. Ngulchu Thogme Distinguishes the Afflicted Enemy from an Ordinary Enemy: Once Uprooted at the Root, It Cannot Return

Whereas an ordinary enemy, driven from one place, may recover strength elsewhere and return to cause harm, the afflicted enemy, once uprooted with the eye of wisdom, has nowhere it could dwell and hence no way to come back.

###### 5. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites Śāntarakṣita: The Afflictions Cease of Their Own Accord Once One Realizes the Absence of True Existence

Just as smoke subsides once a fire is put out, the afflictions, which arise in dependence on grasping at true existence, cease naturally once that grasping is dispelled through realizing selflessness.

###### 6. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Cites the Noble *Ratnakūṭa*: One Must Search Out the Origin of the Affliction, Not Chase the Object

Just as a dog chases the thrown stone while a lion turns on the one who threw it, one should, the moment an affliction arises, trace it to its origin, present location, and destination, rather than following after the external object.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The afflictions dwell nowhere — not in external objects, not in the inner collection of the faculties, not in between, and not anywhere else — and yet, though unestablished by inherent nature, they appear as harm-doers, exactly like an illusion. All the commentaries agree in teaching that one must therefore abandon the fear in one's heart and rely on diligence to generate the wisdom that realizes their absence of inherent nature.

**Key points.**

- Through a fourfold analysis, the afflictions are shown to dwell neither in the object, nor in the collection of the faculties, nor in between, nor anywhere else — establishing that they lack inherent nature.
- Although the afflictions are unestablished wherever one searches, they still appear as harm-doers, just as a magician's spell conjures the illusory appearance of horses and elephants. ⚑ His Holiness the Dalai Lama, while agreeing that the afflictions cannot be found when one searches for their designated referent, adds that they are not therefore nonexistent — they exist through the power of conditions, in the manner of dependent origination — thereby avoiding the extreme of nihilism.
- One must abandon the fear that the afflictions, being truly established, cannot be abandoned, and generate the wisdom that realizes their absence of inherent nature; failing to do so, though capable of abandoning them, needlessly subjects oneself to the suffering of the hells and other lower realms.
- Ngulchu Thogme explains that, unlike an ordinary enemy that can recover and return, the afflicted enemy, once uprooted by wisdom, has nowhere to dwell and so can never come back.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites Śāntarakṣita: once one realizes the absence of true existence, the afflictions cease of their own accord, like smoke subsiding once a fire is extinguished.
- Khenpo Kunzang Pelden cites the noble *Ratnakūṭa*: rather than chasing the external object like a dog chasing a thrown stone, one should, like the lion who turns on the thrower, trace the affliction itself to its very origin and thereby come to know it as without inherent existence.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: having reflected in this manifold way, one must accomplish, through determined effort, the bodhisattva's trainings as they have been taught, and the way in which they simply will not come about unless one actually puts them into practice is as follows. It is said, "make it a matter of earnest application, and think of it as the treatment of an illness" — if one applies oneself earnestly and puts this into practice, some genuine freedom from danger, together with its causes, will result. Just as one must be cured of illness by actually taking medicine, one must attain freedom of mind through actually being conscientious. Having thus counseled that one must apply oneself earnestly and put this into practice, he notes that not practicing conscientiousness and instead letting one's conduct run recklessly out of control is something the afflictions themselves bring about. Since the root from which conscientiousness arises is the need to fight the afflictions, this chapter has been taught with the manner of fighting the afflictions as its central concern. Where conscientiousness is present, introspective alertness will also arise — a mindfulness that knows the essential point of what to adopt and what to reject, thinking, "This kind of action is appropriate," and "That kind of action is not appropriate." Then, on the basis of conscientiousness, when the introspective alertness that constantly and repeatedly examines the conduct of one's own three doors performs its watch, and, sensing that one is on the verge of engaging in some basis for wrongdoing, thinks "there is danger here," it recognizes, through that very watchfulness born of introspective alertness, that this or that action is not to be done, and, the very moment mindfulness takes hold of it, one relies on the antidote and turns back from that action. In the same way, for whatever is to be accomplished, one trains in the bodhisattva's trainings having been seized by mindfulness and introspective alertness. In brief, whether walking, moving, lying down, or sitting — in any posture whatsoever — he teaches that one must, through mindfulness and introspective alertness accompanied by conscientiousness, cause the awakening mind one has generated to remain unimpaired and to increase further and further.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Having reflected carefully in this way on all these teachings on conscientiousness, as they have been explained in the scriptures and treatises, one must apply oneself with the greatest possible diligence to putting into actual practice the three trainings — the higher trainings of ethical discipline, meditative concentration, and wisdom — for the sake of accomplishing them. This is explained by way of a parallel drawn point for point: the Buddha is like the physician; the holy Dharma — study, reflection, meditation, and the rest — is like the medicine; the trainings or vows of ethical discipline, primarily, are like the diet and regimen; the afflictions, such as craving and aversion, are like the illness; and each of us is like the patient. In other words: if one fails to accomplish the ethical discipline one is meant to strive at — that is, if one fails to observe the diet, regimen, and so forth that the skilled physician's words prescribe, in the sense of "this must be given up, this must be taken up" — then, just as a patient who needs treatment for a condition like heat or cold, and yet does not properly follow the physician's instructions, will not be freed from or cured of that heat- or cold-related illness even by taking the finest medicines that serve as its remedy, so too the Dharma will fail to benefit such a person. But if one applies oneself properly and accomplishes it as one should, then, just as an illness is cured by properly relying on the medicine, one will attain the great bliss of complete freedom from all suffering. Therefore, one should apply oneself diligently and train in conscientiousness. As the *Suhṛllekha* (Letter to a Friend) says:

> Conscientiousness is the ground of the deathless nectar; heedlessness is the ground of death — thus has the Sage taught. Therefore, in order to increase your wholesome qualities, devotedly maintain conscientiousness at all times.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Having thus reflected carefully on what has been taught above, one must apply oneself with the greatest diligence to putting into actual practice the vast bodhisattva trainings, in accordance with what has been taught in the sutras and treatises. If one does not accomplish it as it has been taught, then — just as a patient who needs treatment, and does not follow a skilled physician's advice, will certainly not be cured even by the finest medicines, and so the medicines fail to help — so too here; but if one does accomplish it, then, just as relying on medicine brings about relief from illness, one will attain the great bliss of complete freedom from all suffering. <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Having thus reflected carefully, as explained above, on what has been taught in the sutras and treatises, or in the chapter on conscientiousness above — that is, the intention behind generating the awakening mind, and the vast trainings a bodhisattva ought to train in — one must, for the sake of accomplishing these trainings exactly as they are, earnestly abandon whatever is discordant with each of them, earnestly cultivate their respective antidotes, and take up the intention of conscientiousness diligently, thereby applying oneself to actual practice accordingly. If one does not train in this way, it is impossible for the illness of the afflictions in one's own mindstream to be cured — just as, if one does not properly listen to the words of a skilled physician, a patient who needs treatment with medicine will certainly not be cured, so too, if one fails to accomplish, exactly as taught in the Buddha's own scriptural word, what has been shown there, how could one possibly pass beyond the illness of suffering born from karma and the afflictions? There is no such possibility.

As for the fact that one must train properly in the trainings taught in the sutras and elsewhere, and that although a beginner cannot train extensively in this way, one must certainly train in the condensed essential points of the training: the Śikṣāsamuccaya says, "The Blessed One taught the bases of the bodhisattva's training in the sutras," and, as the noble *Ratnamegha* (Cloud of Jewels) says at length:

> Son of noble family, in what way are bodhisattvas restrained by the bodhisattva's vow of training? In this regard, the bodhisattva reflects as follows: by the personal-liberation vow alone I am not able to attain manifest, complete, and perfect awakening in unsurpassed awakening; therefore, whatever bodhisattva conduct and whatever bases of bodhisattva training the Tathāgata has taught, from whichever sutras they are taught, in all of that I shall train.

Therefore, since it is difficult for someone of weak intelligence, such as myself, to understand the bodhisattva's vow because it is taught so extensively, one might ask how, then, one should proceed: it is taught that one should train as will be explained a little below, beginning with "the essential points by which one will not fall into transgression." That if one does not train, having properly taken this to heart, one will be unable to abandon the afflictions in one's own mindstream is taught in the Candrapradīpa (Moon Lamp) Sūtra:

> Human bodies in this world are full of suffering; for many years, on occasion, they find no release. Being thus long afflicted by illness, one seeks out a physician also to heal the illnesses. Having sought again and again in this way, one finds a physician, skilled and learned; and he, having established compassion close at hand, gives the medicine, saying, "Take this." Having received the medicine — abundant, excellent, and precious — the patient does not drink the medicine that would restore health. The physician has not failed him, nor has the medicine failed him — it is the patient himself who is at fault. In just the same way, one who has gone forth into this teaching, endowed with the powers, the meditative absorptions, and all the faculties, if he does not apply himself diligently to meditation, then, without applying himself diligently, how could he possibly pass beyond suffering?

and, as the *Adhyāśayasaṃcodana* (Sūtra Inspiring Noble Intention) also says:

> Though I have taught the excellent Dharma, if, having heard it, you do not properly put it into practice, then, just as a patient carrying a sack of medicine cannot thereby cure his own illness, [neither will you be cured].

This is as it is taught. If one accomplishes it properly, then, just as listening to the physician's words and relying on the medicine cures the illness, one will attain liberation from all suffering and affliction. Therefore, one should accomplish it in accordance with the word of the great physician, the Buddha, and put it into actual practice.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

As for "having reflected in this way, I will make every effort to accomplish the training as it has been explained": this means one must apply oneself to study and reflection in order to understand the nature of the afflictions, and that failing to apply oneself in this way, letting these afflictions — for which no purpose or inherent nature whatsoever has actually been established — freely inflict on oneself, at one's own whim, the sufferings of the hells and so forth: that is, allowing oneself, driven by the afflictions, to accumulate unwholesome karma, and then, as the fruit of that karma, to experience the many sufferings of heat and cold, hunger and thirst, and the like in the hells, the preta realm, and the animal realm — engaging in that state of harm in every way whatsoever — is utterly unreasonable. Up until now, one has caused harm to oneself at one's own whim by failing to properly examine and by failing to see this actual state of the afflictions' true nature; but since the true nature of the afflictions is without inherent existence, it is not proper to allow the afflictions to inflict such pointless harm upon oneself — this is the meaning. In this way, this means that one must repeatedly reflect on the character or essence of each of the following: the manner of being conscientious by reflecting on the subject matter and purpose taught in the chapter on conscientiousness — that is, on the trainings — the manner of being conscientious by reflecting on the basis, one's freedoms and endowments, and the manner of being conscientious by reflecting on the afflictions to be abandoned; and that one must apply oneself, for the sake of accomplishing or practicing the trainings as they have been explained.

As for "if a patient does not listen to the doctor's words, how can they be cured by medicine that needs to treat them?": this means that one must engage in actual practice in accordance with this — mere understanding does not help; one must accomplish it, following understanding, by way of actual practice. For example: when a patient is afflicted by an illness of wind, bile, phlegm, or the like, and relies on a physician, then, just as the physician explains what foods, conduct, and medicines are or are not needed, the patient must listen to the physician's words and engage in the appropriate diet, conduct, and medicine. If one does not listen to the physician's words, how could that patient possibly recover? He certainly will not. In just the same way, one must put into actual practice, through genuine practical application, the advice taught in the chapter on conscientiousness — not merely have it explained by the venerable Śāntideva; merely being told does not help. Therefore, one must engage in actual practice in the proper way — this is the meaning.

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The physician, the medicine, and the patient (སྨན་པ། སྨན། ནད་པའི་དཔེ)** → Whether the bodhisattva's trainings, as explained above, are actually put into practice or not.
  Khenpo Kunzang Pelden lays out the parallel point for point: the Buddha is like the physician; the holy Dharma — study, reflection, and meditation — is like the medicine; the training in ethical discipline is like the diet and regimen; the afflictions such as craving and aversion are like the illness; and each of us is like the patient. All the other commentators agree that, in general, just as a patient who does not listen to a skilled physician's words cannot be cured even by fine medicine, so too if one does not put the Buddha's word into practice exactly as it has been taught, one will not be freed from the illness of the afflictions and suffering. Minyak Kunzang Sonam traces the example in detail through the Candrapradīpa Sūtra's account, explaining that when a physician compassionately gives good medicine and the patient does not take it, the fault lies with the patient, not with the physician or the medicine. His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that just as one must actually take the medicine in order to recover from illness, one must actually practice conscientiousness in order to attain freedom of mind.

- **The Candrapradīpa Sūtra's patient who will not drink the medicine** → Where the fault lies when one merely studies and reflects, without putting the teaching into practice.
  A patient, long tormented by illness, seeks out a skilled physician and, out of compassion, is given fine medicine — yet if that patient does not drink it, recovery does not follow, and the fault lies neither with the physician nor with the medicine, but with the patient. Applied to the Buddha's teaching: if one does not apply oneself diligently to meditation, as the fully-endowed powers and faculties allow, one will not pass beyond suffering.

> "Son of noble family, in what way are bodhisattvas restrained by the bodhisattva's vow of training? ... whatever bodhisattva conduct and whatever bases of bodhisattva training the Tathāgata has taught, from whichever sutras they are taught, in all of that I shall train."
> — the noble *Ratnamegha* (Cloud of Jewels), as quoted in the Śikṣāsamuccaya (adduced by Minyak Kunzang Sonam on the need to train in the bodhisattva's trainings as extensively taught)

> "Human bodies in this world are full of suffering; for many years, on occasion, they find no release. Being thus long afflicted by illness, one seeks out a physician also to heal the illnesses. Having sought again and again in this way, one finds a physician, skilled and learned; and he, having established compassion close at hand, gives the medicine, saying, 'Take this.' Having received the medicine — abundant, excellent, and precious — the patient does not drink the medicine that would restore health. The physician has not failed him, nor has the medicine failed him — it is the patient himself who is at fault. In just the same way, one who has gone forth into this teaching, endowed with the powers, the meditative absorptions, and all the faculties, if he does not apply himself diligently to meditation, then, without applying himself diligently, how could he possibly pass beyond suffering?"
> — the *Candrapradīpa* (Moon Lamp) Sūtra (adduced by Minyak Kunzang Sonam as the scriptural source of the physician/patient parable itself)

> "Though I have taught the excellent Dharma, if, having heard it, you do not properly put it into practice, then, just as a patient carrying a sack of medicine cannot thereby cure his own illness, [neither will you be cured]."
> — the *Adhyāśayasaṃcodana* Sūtra (Sūtra Inspiring Noble Intention) (adduced by Minyak Kunzang Sonam: hearing the teaching without practicing it is like a patient who carries a sack of medicine but is not thereby cured)

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Without Putting the Training into Actual Practice, One Will Not Be Freed from the Illness of the Afflictions and Suffering

Just as a patient who does not heed a skilled physician's words cannot be cured even by the finest medicine, so too, if one fails to accomplish the training the Buddha has taught exactly as it has been explained, one will never be freed from the suffering produced by the afflictions and karma — all the commentaries agree on this point.

###### 2. This Closing Verse Sums Up the Chapter, Counseling Reflection on Every Teaching Given So Far Before Meditating on Conscientiousness

Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary states outright that this verse serves "to gather up, without remainder, the entire meaning taught earlier." Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen likewise explains that one must apply oneself to meditating on conscientiousness for the sake of guarding the awakening mind and the training taught throughout the chapter. Minyak Kunzang Sonam similarly echoes the whole chapter, referring back to "the intention behind generating the awakening mind... taught above in the chapter on conscientiousness."

###### 3. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Maps the Physician/Medicine/Illness/Patient Simile onto the Path in Detail

He explains: the Buddha is like the physician; the holy Dharma — study, reflection, and meditation — is like the medicine; the training in ethical discipline is like the diet and regimen; the afflictions such as craving and aversion are like the illness; and each of us is like the patient.

###### 4. Mere Study and Reflection Do Not Help — One Must Put the Teaching into Actual Practice

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that mere understanding does not help; one must accomplish it, following understanding, through actual practical application. His Holiness the Dalai Lama adds, still more pointedly, that merely being taught it — even by the venerable Śāntideva himself — is of no benefit at all.

###### 5. Three Grounds for Repeated Reflection, Introduced by "Having Reflected in This Way"

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that one must repeatedly reflect on three things: the subject matter and trainings taught in the chapter on conscientiousness, the freedoms and endowments that form one's basis, and the afflictions to be abandoned.

###### 6. The Candrapradīpa Sūtra's Patient Who Will Not Drink the Medicine Shows Where the Fault of Not Practicing Truly Lies

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that although the physician compassionately gives fine medicine, if the patient does not take it, recovery does not follow — and the fault lies not with the physician or the medicine, but with the patient. Applied to the Buddha's teaching, failing to apply oneself diligently to meditation means one will not pass beyond suffering.

###### 7. The *Suhṛllekha*'s Teaching That Conscientiousness Is the Ground of the Deathless and Heedlessness the Ground of Death Is Cited to Urge Constant Conscientiousness

Both Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Sabzang Mati Panchen cite this same scripture directly, urging that, in order to increase one's wholesome qualities, one should devotedly maintain conscientiousness at all times.

###### 8. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Frames the Whole Chapter as a Manner of Fighting the Afflictions, to Be Carried into Every Posture of Daily Life

He explains that this chapter has been taught with the manner of fighting the afflictions as its central concern, and that whether walking, moving, lying down, or sitting, one must, through mindfulness and introspective alertness accompanied by conscientiousness, keep the awakening mind one has generated unimpaired and cause it to increase further and further.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This closing verse of the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* teaches that having carefully reflected on everything taught earlier — the awakening mind and every training — one must apply oneself to putting it into actual practice through conscientiousness; if one does not practice as the physician's words prescribe, one will not be freed from the illness of the afflictions and suffering, all the commentators agree.

**Key points.**

- Since this verse is the summarizing verse of the fourth chapter, it counsels reflecting on the awakening mind and every training taught above, and applying oneself to meditating on conscientiousness.
- Khenpo Kunzang Pelden maps out, point for point, that the Buddha is like the physician, the holy Dharma like the medicine, the discipline of the trainings like the diet and regimen, the afflictions like the illness, and each of us like the patient.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that mere understanding does not help; one must accomplish it, following understanding, through actual practical application — and one must repeatedly reflect on the trainings, one's freedoms and endowments, and the afflictions to be abandoned.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the Candrapradīpa Sūtra, shows that when a physician gives good medicine out of compassion and the patient does not take it, the fault lies with the patient, not with the physician or the medicine — so too, failing to practice what is taught means the fault for remaining unfreed lies with oneself.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama frames the whole chapter as fundamentally about fighting the afflictions, and teaches that mindfulness and introspective alertness, joined with conscientiousness, must be carried into every posture of daily life so that the awakening mind may remain unimpaired and continually increase.

*Thus ends Chapter Four: "Conscientiousness" (Vigilant Introspection).*
