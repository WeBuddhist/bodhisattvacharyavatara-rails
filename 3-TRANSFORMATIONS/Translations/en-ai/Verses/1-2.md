## Combined Commentary Summaries
The three commentators provide a highly consistent structural and psychological analysis of Verse 1-2, which marks the beginning of Śāntideva's expression of humility. They examine the verse along three main dimensions: the twofold gesture of humility, the renunciation of other-benefit, and the primary focus on self-cultivation.

### 1. The Twofold Gesture of Humility
All three commentators agree that Śāntideva's humility is grounded in a double denial: a denial of doctrinal originality and a denial of literary or poetic skill.
* **No Doctrinal Originality:** The commentators explain that the author has nothing new to say that has not already appeared in the pure sūtras and other canonical scriptures. Gyaltsab Je, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme all emphasize that the subject matter is strictly in accordance with previous scriptures.
* **No Poetic Skill:** All three commentators point out that the author denies possessing any exceptional skill in composition or the poetic phrasing of words. Gyaltsab Je describes this as lacking the skill in alternating heavy and light syllables, while Sazang Mati Panchen describes it as lacking the scholarship of poetry adorned with various ornaments and beautiful arrangements of letters.
* **Purpose of the Gesture:** The commentators agree that this double denial serves to systematically eliminate scholastic pride and intellectual arrogance. Ngulchu Thokme adds that expressing humility in this manner shows the author is entirely free from pride, which pre-emptively seeks forgiveness from his peers for any faults that might arise in the composition. Sazang Mati Panchen notes that by abandoning the pride of being skilled in words and meaning, the author is able to teach with genuine humility.

### 2. The Renunciation of "Other-Benefit"
Because the treatise contains no original doctrines and lacks literary embellishment, the commentators explain that Śāntideva has no primary expectation of benefiting others.
* **Logical Boundary of Audience:** Gyaltsab Je and Ngulchu Thokme establish a logical boundary regarding the target audience: since the profound and vast meaning of the Great Vehicle is already fully and perfectly expressed in the sūtras, those who possess sharp intelligence and can easily comprehend the scriptures directly do not need this treatise. Therefore, Śāntideva explicitly states that he has not composed this text for their sake.

### 3. Primary Motivation: Self-Familiarization and Mind Training
The commentators reach a strong consensus that the primary, immediate motivation for composing the treatise is self-benefit (*rang don*)—specifically, the continuous training, cultivation, and familiarization of the author's own mind with the qualities of bodhicitta and the Bodhisattva conduct.
* **Familiarization (*sgom pa* / *Vāsanā*):** Gyaltsab Je defines this as "familiarizing his own mind, so that what has already been understood will not decline but increase." Sazang Mati Panchen defines it as "cultivating and practicing the aspiring mind of enlightenment and the engagement of the six perfections in my own mind." Ngulchu Thokme similarly explains it as "familiarizing my own mind with the meditation on the mind of enlightenment."
* **Why Write a Treatise?** Gyaltsab Je and Ngulchu Thokme address a potential objection: if the author already understands these truths, why must he write a treatise rather than simply meditating on them in his mind? They explain that setting down these realizations in writing acts as a powerful support that prevents spiritual decline and causes his faith, wisdom, and compassion to continuously increase and develop. Sazang Mati Panchen notes that this physical record allows the author's force of faith (conviction, clarity, and aspiration) to temporarily increase more and more.
