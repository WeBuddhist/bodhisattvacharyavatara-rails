# Day 113 — Train in Every Situation, Dedicate Every Virtue: Acting Only for the Sake of Beings

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the ninety-ninth, one hundredth, and one hundred and first verses of the fifth chapter. Today, even every single step we take, we make into something for the sake of all beings.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable example, which is easy to understand and encouraging. The *Good Arm* gives this example of dedicating merit. Just as rivers that reach the ocean become one taste of salt, so the merit dedicated to unsurpassed awakening becomes one taste with the buddhas. Just as all the rivers that reach the ocean become one taste of salt, the merit dedicated to unsurpassed awakening becomes one taste with the merit of the buddhas.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will dedicate all the good I have done, so that all beings reach awakening.

**Explanation:** *(Doing good)* This teaches that everything we do must become only for the sake of beings. When we dedicate good to awakening in this way, it brings benefit to all beings. This makes a good cause for awakening, for ourselves and for others.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This says: whatever place, time, or situation one is in, whatever one does with the three doors, one must come to know whatever the Tathāgata taught in the sutras as the trainings of bodhisattvas, and strive to train in whatever is needed in that situation and must be put into practice.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whether one engages joyfully of one's own accord or is urged on by others; whether one is a beginner or one who has attained a ground, and so on — in whatever situation, whatever one does with the three doors, in each of those situations in which the pure scriptures teach that a bodhisattva should train, one should strive and train, with conviction.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The purpose: whether under the power of oneself or of others — in whatever situation, whether the situations of conduct such as walking and sitting, the situations of aspirational practice, or the situations of one who has attained a ground and so on, whatever one does with the three doors, whatever one engages in — in each of those situations in which the Great Vehicle's bodhisattva collection teaches that the bodhisattva heirs must train, one should strive at each of those trainings, with joy and respect, to take up and abandon in the proper way, and train without being stained by the stains of opposing factors. This is one way it is explained. Or it can be construed in this way: "under one's own power" as what is gathered in "gathering meditation," or "under another's power" as what is gathered in "acting for beings," or else, in whatever other situation of conduct, whatever one does with the three doors, whatever training it is and however it is taught to be trained.

To give just some examples of what is taught in the sutras: "A bodhisattva should not train in many dharmas; he should train in great compassion"; and again, "A bodhisattva should accomplish things in such a way that beings become glad the moment they see him"; and again, "A bodhisattva should guard his own mind above all"; and "A bodhisattva should not disparage even a single bodhisattva"; and "In all conduct, how one is to train: one should train just as it is taught in the *Completely Pure Domain of Conduct*." That this is the meaning is stated in the Detailed Explanation. The latter passage is from the *Cloud of Jewels*:

> When he enters a monastery, he generates the mind thus: "I will usher all beings into the city of nirvana." When he comes out, he generates the mind thus: "I will draw all beings out of the prison of samsara." Then, when he opens the door of his dwelling, he generates the mind thus: "With transcendent wisdom I will open the door to all the happy realms."

and so on — together with what is shown in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* where it speaks of other causes for increasing merit. In detail, he says one must train as in the *Sutra of the Completely Pure Domain of Conduct*. And in the *Ornament of the Sutras* too:

> However the buddhas' heirs conduct themselves,
> however they enter the varied domains of the senses,
> in just that way, with fitting and agreeable words,
> for the benefit of beings they bring that about.

So it says. As for the need to train in the many doors of training:

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: in short, whether one acts continually under one's own power or under the power of other beings — in whatever situation, whether walking, moving about, lying down, sitting, and so on, or teaching and listening to the Dharma and so on, or the activities of hearing, reflecting, and meditating and so on, whatever one does — the ways in which the Buddha taught in the sutras that bodhisattvas are to train, those that Shantideva gathered in accordance with the scriptural authority of the sutras, in each of those situations that are the subject matter of the *Way of the Bodhisattva*, one must strive, with conviction, to train in them or to accomplish them. #### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In whatever situation, place, or time, whatever one does, one must strive to train in what the Tathāgata taught in the sutras as the bodhisattva's training. Moreover, the purpose may be under one's own power or under the power of other beings, whichever it is.

**Key points.**

- Whether one engages joyfully of one's own accord, under one's own power, or is urged on by others and acts under the power of beings.
- In whatever situation — teaching and listening to the Dharma, hearing, reflecting, and meditating, or whatever one does with the three doors — one must train in the many doors of training.
- The trainings of the buddhas' heirs, gathered by Shantideva in accordance with the scriptural authority of the sutras, one trains in with effort.
- To give an example of what the sutras teach: the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* teaches trainings such as the causes for increasing merit.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Among all the fields of knowledge, there is none that the bodhisattva heirs, who strive to accomplish benefit and happiness for all beings, do not train in. For the wise one who thus abides well, unconfused about everything, there is none among all the trainings, as they are trained in, that does not become a cause for gathering merit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-100]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This says: when bodhisattva heirs work for the benefit of others, the beings who are their objects have a variety of constitutions and inclinations, and times and situations are limitless. So for the bodhisattva heirs there is hardly any "this is not to be done," once one looks at the place, the time, and the situation. For a bodhisattva who is skilled in such fields of practice, whatever he does all goes toward vast benefit for others, and there is nothing that does not go toward becoming a cause for easily completing the great accumulation of merit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-100]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Moreover, it is also taught in line with the *Sutra of the Completely Pure Domain of Conduct*: for the bodhisattva heirs there is no object of knowledge, no field of knowledge, that they do not train in — crafts and medicine, grammar and epistemology, and the inner science, that is, the five major fields of knowledge and the five minor fields, and so on. Having thus trained in the limitless objects of knowledge, and abiding in mindfulness and awareness, for that bodhisattva skilled in means, right down to habitual acts such as lifting and setting down and stretching and drawing in the hand, there is nothing that does not become a basis of merit and virtue. If even the non-virtuous becomes virtuous, what need is there to mention other things, such as the neutral?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-100]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

There is no object of knowledge that the bodhisattva heirs do not train in, because if one does not know any one of them, one cannot fully accomplish the welfare of others. As the *Ornament of the Sutras* says: "If one has not striven in the five fields of knowledge, even the supreme noble one will not become omniscient. Therefore, in order to curb and uphold others, and to know everything oneself, one strives in each of these." Thus, for the wise one who knows the objects of training well and abides in training, there is no action that does not become merit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-167]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

There is no object of knowledge, no field of knowledge, that the bodhisattva heirs, the bodhisattvas, do not train in. From the *Ornament*: *(Editorial note: no quotation text follows in the rail.)* — as it says. For the wise one who abides in that way, whose mind has been refined with regard to the limitless objects of knowledge, there is nothing that does not become merit, because he has no doubt about the places of adopting and abandoning and knows how to turn everything into virtue. From the sage Gyünshé's: *(Editorial note: no quotation text follows in the rail.)* — as it is explained.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-100]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Having ascertained the nature of the two truths as they are, and by way of fully grasping their meaning, having ripened their own mindstreams, the bodhisattva heirs then enter in order to ripen the mindstreams of others and accomplish a variety of benefits for others through many doors of skill in means. There is no object of knowledge, no field of knowledge, that they do not train in. Why? Because those great beings, who must fully accomplish the welfare of others, must, in accomplishing it, teach each disciple the very door of Dharma that accords with that disciple's own inclination and so fully satisfy their hopes and wishes; if they did not know even one of the means of taming, they could not accomplish it in that way. For that reason, at the earlier "whether independent or dependent," it was stated that the training to be trained in is not fixed. Thus, in the *Ornament of the Sutras*:

> If one has not striven in the five fields of knowledge,
> even the supreme noble one will not become omniscient.
> Therefore, in order to curb and uphold others,
> and to know everything oneself, one strives in each of these.

So it says. And the *Great Commentary* also says:

> Having fully understood the suchness of all things in every respect, the heirs of the buddhas, who strive for the sake of accomplishing benefit and happiness for all — whatever it is that they are not to train in, that does not exist even slightly; otherwise they would be unable to do everything that is to be done for the welfare of beings.

and so on, it says. Thus, relying on two wisdoms — the wisdom realizing the ultimate, the knowledge of the nature of things, and the wisdom realizing the conventional, the knowledge of the five fields — and through the various doors of skill in means for taming disciples, such as curbing and upholding, the wise bodhisattva who abides intent on accomplishing the ultimate fulfillment of the two aims has no doubt about whatever concerns bringing his own meditation to its conclusion or guiding others well in the Dharma, and has gained sound certainty, and is skilled in turning all the activities of the three doors toward the side of the white. Because of this, there is nothing that does not become a vast action of merit and virtue. Vibhūti says: "There is nothing that is not to be trained in by the Victor's heirs. Not grasping at 'I' and 'mine,' realizing the two truths, and striving for others' benefit — for one who conducts himself thus, there is nothing that is not merit." And in the Detailed Explanation:

> One should train in all five fields of knowledge in a manner embraced by the six supreme ones. With the intention of attaining great enlightenment for the sake of all beings, one should train in each of the five fields of knowledge in a way that is utterly pure in the three spheres, unstained by opposing factors, and in order to dedicate that root of virtue as well to great enlightenment; in sequence, the purpose, the basis, the object, skill in means, purity, and supreme dedication are to be known.

So it says, and it is also said that for that reason merit becomes vast. Moreover, as it says:

> For one skilled in proper means,
> there is nothing that does not become useful;
> for one who knows well how to combine,
> there is nothing that does not become medicine.

as the sage Gyünshé explains, some say.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-100]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: in general, the bodhisattva heirs of the Victor, in order to take care of fortunate disciples, train in two fields of knowledge, crafts and medicine; in order to refute wrong claims against the Buddha's teaching, in the two, grammar and epistemology; and in order to know everything themselves, in the inner knowledge, the three collections of the inner science — these being the five major fields of knowledge. In addition to these there are the five minor fields of knowledge, such as these: if one knows drama (ཟློས་གར), one is not confused about languages; if one knows prosody (སྡེབ་སྦྱོར), one is not confused about verse; if one knows lexicography (མངོན་བརྗོད), one is not confused about names; if one knows astrology (སྐར་རྩིས), one is not confused about numbers; if one knows poetics (སྙན་དངགས), one is not confused about ornaments of language. Thus, among these ten fields of knowledge, there is nothing at all that they do not train in; since all of them are needed for doing the welfare of others, they must be learned. For bodhisattvas, the wise who train and abide in this way, even such slight things as taking a step or moving the hands back and forth — there is nothing that does not become a cause of merit. Therefore one must train properly in all objects of knowledge and increase it from higher to higher.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** For the sake of accomplishing others' welfare, the bodhisattva heir must train in everything, with no object of knowledge that he does not train in. For one who is thus unconfused about everything and skilled in how to train, a vast accumulation of merit gathers easily, and there is nothing that does not become so.

**Key points.**

- In line with the *Sutra of the Completely Pure Domain of Conduct*, one must train in all that is to be trained in; since times and situations are limitless, one trains in every place and time.
- One who has refined the mind with regard to the limitless objects of knowledge is called wise; by ascertaining the nature of the two truths as they are, the knowledge of the nature of things becomes of great meaning.
- The inner science is for the sake of refuting wrong claims against the Buddha's teaching; and among the five sciences, if one knows drama, prosody, lexicography, astrology, and poetics, one is not confused, which is needed for working for the welfare of others.
- By training properly in all objects of knowledge, even the mere moving of the hands back and forth becomes merit.

### Verse 5-101

> **Rail source:** `2-RAILS/Verses/5-101-summary.md` &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Rail status:** `draft`

<!-- sub:root-verse -->
#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-101`):*

> Use body, speech, and mind for nothing except the welfare of beings, directly or indirectly; and dedicate everything to enlightenment for beings' sake alone.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

The summary of the basis of training: whether directly, through the giving of Dharma and material goods and so on, or indirectly, one does not act for anything other than the welfare of beings; and, doing others' welfare indirectly, for the sake of beings alone one dedicates all objects of virtue to perfect enlightenment.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This says: those who train in the path of generating a good heart do only what, directly or indirectly, brings benefit to others; there is nothing for them to do that is other than that. All the roots of virtue that accomplish others' welfare, together with their results — abandoning the thought of expecting anything in return or any ripened result, and cultivating the mind of giving, one must dedicate the roots of virtue together with their results for the sake of other beings alone.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-101]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whether one does others' welfare directly through Dharma and material goods, or indirectly — by accomplishing virtue such as one's own retreat and approach-and-accomplishment practice, dedicating it for others' welfare, making aspirations, and so on — one engages in nothing else, such as accomplishing one's own welfare, apart from accomplishing solely the welfare of all other beings. And the intended purpose being for the sake of beings alone, whatever roots of virtue, such as the six perfections, that oneself and others have accumulated in the three times, all of them, one must dedicate and make aspirations, saying, "I dedicate them in order to attain the state of perfect enlightenment."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-101]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

It says: whether directly or indirectly, one does not act for anything other than the welfare of beings, and for the sake of beings alone, dedicates all virtue for enlightenment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-170]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whether one directly performs giving and so on oneself, or indirectly has others do it, one does not act for anything other than the aim of accomplishing benefit and happiness for all beings; and the intended purpose too is for the sake of beings alone: all roots of virtue accumulated by oneself and others in the three times are to be fully dedicated and turned over for the sake of attaining great perfect enlightenment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-101]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

It is explained as: "Whether, with one's mindstream ripened, one trains in giving and so on by way of directly engaging in others' welfare; or else, first, one trains in giving and so on — the means of ripening one's own mindstream — and makes that very training indirectly a cause for accomplishing others' welfare, either way." Or it is also acceptable to explain it as: "Whether one trains in giving and so on directly oneself, or indirectly urges others to train in giving and so on, either way."

In any case, having all activities embraced solely by the thought of benefiting others, one does not oneself engage in anything other than the aim of accomplishing benefit and happiness for all other beings, nor does one have others engage in it. And — never mind great roots of virtue, even down to the smallest roots of virtue — for the sake of the intended purpose, beings alone, one gathers them together with the virtue accumulated by all, oneself and others, in the three times, and trains in dedicating all of it in order to attain unsurpassed great enlightenment. From the *Perfect Compendium of the Dharma*:

> In this way it is clearly and fully shown how bodhisattvas do nothing other than what is done for the welfare of others. Blessed One, of the bodhisattvas' *(text unclear: ཀྱིལས་)* whatever action, whatever action of speech and of mind, all of it proceeds by looking upon all beings. It comes under the power of great compassion. It is the cause of the basis of benefit for beings. It arises from the special intention of benefit and happiness for all beings. With such an intention to benefit, the perception comes to be thus: "Whatever accomplishing benefit and accomplishing happiness for all beings there is, that accomplishing I shall accomplish."

and so on, it says. And in the *Good Arm* as well:

> Just as, when rivers reach the ocean,
> they become of one taste with the taste of salt,
> so the merit dedicated to unsurpassed enlightenment
> becomes of one taste with that of the victors.

and so on, it says. And in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, in the section on protecting merit, having said, "The brief summary of protecting merit is this: it is what is dedicated to enlightenment," it establishes this with the example, from the scripture of Inexhaustible Intelligence cited earlier, of a drop of water falling into the ocean. Thus, through these two — training in many bases of training, and dedicating all such roots of virtue for others' welfare as a cause for perfect enlightenment — the way to train in the special causes for increasing merit is shown.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-101]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: in short, directly one may be able to benefit disciples, beings, by teaching them the Dharma and by giving material goods and so on; or else one oneself does, as much as one is able, such practices as hearing, reflecting, and meditating, teaching, debating, composing, and accomplishment and practice, together with dedicating all the roots of virtue of that for the sake of others; or indirectly — that is, in short, whether directly or indirectly, one does not engage in any conduct motivated by the thought of one's own welfare, other than the welfare of beings alone. And for the sake of other beings alone, one continually trains in the six perfections and the four ways of gathering, or in the three kinds of bodhisattva ethical discipline, or whatever virtues, such as hearing, reflecting, and meditating, one accomplishes — all of them, for the immediate and the long term, for the sake of others to attain enlightenment, with the three spheres taken as without any focus (དམིགས་པ་མེད་པ), one must dedicate again and again. Such is the meaning.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In all bases of training, whether directly or indirectly, one does not act for anything other than the welfare of beings. All roots of virtue that accomplish others' welfare, together with their results, are dedicated to enlightenment for the sake of beings alone.

**Key points.**

- Whether one does others' welfare directly oneself, through giving Dharma and material goods and so on, or indirectly.
- With one's mindstream ripened, one engages directly in others' welfare, and must put the thought of benefiting others first in all that one does.
- All roots of virtue from whatever one is able to do oneself — hearing, reflecting, and meditating, teaching, debating, composing, and accomplishment and practice — are dedicated for the welfare of others.
- One who trains in the path of generating a good heart has nothing to do that is not for the welfare of others.
