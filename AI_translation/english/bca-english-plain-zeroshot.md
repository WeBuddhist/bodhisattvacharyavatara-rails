# Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Way of Life ^0

## 0. Introduction ^I-0

In Sanskrit: Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra ^I-1

In Tibetan: Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Way of Life ^I-2

Homage to all Buddhas and Bodhisattvas! ^I-3

## 1. Chapter One: Explaining the Benefits of the Mind of Awakening ^1-0


I respectfully bow to the Enlightened Ones, who embody the ultimate truth, and their spiritual children,
and to all who are worthy of veneration.
I will briefly explain how to enter the vows of the spiritual children of the Enlightened Ones,
according to the scriptures. ^1-1


I do not present anything here that has not been said before,
nor do I possess skill in composition.
Therefore, I have no intention of benefiting others;
I composed this for the purpose of cultivating it in my own mind. ^1-2


To cultivate virtue, the strength of my faith
may temporarily increase through these words.
And if others who share my fortune
should see these words, may it be meaningful for them. ^1-3


This precious human life is very hard to find.
Having attained it, which can accomplish great purpose,
if one does not achieve what is beneficial with it,
how will such an opportunity arise again? ^1-4


Just as in the darkness of a cloudy night,
a flash of lightning briefly illuminates everything,
similarly, by the power of the Buddhas, rarely
do worldly merit and wisdom arise. ^1-5


Therefore, consider how weak ordinary virtues are,
and how powerful and unbearable negative actions are.
Without the perfect mind of awakening,
what other virtue can overcome them? ^1-6


The great sages, who have reflected for many eons,
have seen this very thing as beneficial.
By this, countless beings
can easily attain the supreme happiness. ^1-7


Whoever wishes to overcome hundreds of sufferings of existence,
and wishes to remove the unhappiness of beings,
and wishes to experience hundreds of joys,
should never abandon the mind of awakening. ^1-8


If the mind of awakening arises, in an instant,
those wretched ones bound in the prison of cyclic existence
are called children of the Enlightened Ones,
and become worthy of veneration by gods and humans. ^1-9


Like the supreme elixir that transforms base metals into gold,
it transforms this impure body into the precious body of a Buddha,
a body of immeasurable value.
Therefore, firmly hold onto this called the mind of awakening. ^1-10


The sole guide for beings, with immeasurable wisdom,
having thoroughly examined it, found it to be precious.
Therefore, those who wish to be free from the states of suffering,
should firmly hold onto this precious mind of awakening. ^1-11


All other virtues, like a plantain tree,
bear fruit and then perish.
But the wish-fulfilling tree of the mind of awakening, always
bears fruit and never diminishes, but grows. ^1-12


Even if one has committed extremely terrible negative actions,
like relying on a hero in great fear,
that which, when relied upon, quickly liberates,
why would the wise not rely on it? ^1-13


It burns away great negative actions, like the fire at the end of an eon,
surely consuming them in a single moment.
Its immeasurable benefits
were explained by the wise Maitreya. ^1-14


To summarize, the mind of awakening
should be known in two aspects:
the aspiring mind of awakening,
and the engaging mind of awakening. ^1-15


Just as one knows the difference
between wishing to go and actually going,
similarly, the wise should understand
the distinction between these two, in order. ^1-16


From the aspiring mind of awakening,
great results arise in cyclic existence,
but unlike the engaging mind,
merit does not continuously flow. ^1-17


From the moment one takes the vow
to liberate all countless beings,
with an irreversible resolve,
that mind is truly adopted. ^1-18


From that moment on, even while sleeping
or being careless, the power of merit
continuously and abundantly
arises, equal to space. ^1-19


This was reasonably explained
when requested by Good Hand,
by the Enlightened One himself, for the benefit of humble beings. ^1-20


If one merely thinks, "I will relieve
the headache of a single being,"
that thought of wishing to help
is endowed with immeasurable merit. ^1-21


What need is there to speak of one who wishes
to remove the immeasurable unhappiness of each and every being,
and to bring each one immeasurable qualities? ^1-22


Whether it be a father or a mother,
who has such a benevolent mind?
Whether it be gods or sages,
does even Brahma possess this? ^1-23


Those beings themselves, previously,
did not even dream of such a mind for their own benefit.
How then could it arise for the benefit of others? ^1-24


That mind which arises for the benefit of beings,
which others do not even possess for their own sake,
this extraordinary jewel of the mind,
is a wonder that has never been seen before. ^1-25


The cause of joy for all beings,
the elixir that cures the suffering of beings,
how can one measure the merit
of this precious mind? ^1-26


If merely thinking of benefiting others
is superior to making offerings to the Buddhas,
what need is there to speak of striving
for the happiness of all beings without exception? ^1-27


Though beings wish to abandon suffering,
they rush directly towards suffering itself.
Though they desire happiness, through ignorance,
they destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy. ^1-28


That which satisfies with all happiness
those who are deprived of happiness and full of suffering,
and cuts off all suffering, ^1-29


and also removes ignorance —
what virtue can compare to it?
What friend can be like it?
What merit can be like it? ^1-30


If one who repays a kindness
is temporarily worthy of praise,
what need is there to speak of a Bodhisattva
who spontaneously does good? ^1-31


People honor as virtuous one who offers a little ordinary food to a few beings,
giving them a momentary meal,
or who, with disdain, satisfies them for half a day. ^1-32


What need is there to speak of one who always gives
the supreme happiness of the Enlightened Ones
to countless beings for a long time, fulfilling all their wishes? ^1-33


If someone generates ill will towards such a spiritual child of the Buddhas,
that person, for as many eons as the number of ill thoughts generated,
will dwell in hell, so the Sage taught. ^1-34


However, if someone generates a pure mind,
the result will increase even more.
Even with great effort, no negative actions arise for the spiritual children of the Buddhas,
and virtues naturally increase. ^1-35


I bow to the body of one in whom
that supreme jewel of the mind has arisen.
Even if harm is done to them, happiness is connected to them.
I take refuge in that source of happiness. ^1-36


From *Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Way of Life*, this is the first chapter, called "Explaining the Benefits of the Mind of Awakening." ^1-a

## 2. Chapter Two: Confession of Wrongdoings ^2-0


To take hold of that precious mind,
I offer excellent homage to the Enlightened Ones, the sacred teachings,
The pure Three Jewels, and the Buddha's children—
All of them oceans of good qualities. ^2-1


All the flowers and fruits that exist,
All the various kinds of medicines,
All the jewels in the world,
And all the pure, delightful waters, ^2-2


Likewise, mountains of jewels,
Forests, secluded and pleasant places,
Trees adorned with flowers and decorations,
And trees whose branches bend with excellent fruits, ^2-3


Also, in the realms of gods and others, fragrances,
Incense, wish-granting trees, and jewel trees,
Various kinds of uncultivated crops,
And other ornaments worthy of offering— ^2-4


Lakes and ponds adorned with lotuses,
With swans whose calls are very sweet and delightful,
Reaching to the ends of the vast expanse of space,
All these things, which are not owned by anyone, ^2-5


I mentally take and offer them well to the Supreme Sage,
And to his children.
May these holy recipients of offerings, who are greatly compassionate,
Think of me with kindness and accept these offerings of mine. ^2-6


I am poor and lack good deeds;
I have no other wealth for offerings.
Therefore, O Protectors who think of the welfare of others,
For my sake, please accept these by your own power. ^2-7


To the Victorious Ones and their children, I
Permanently offer my entire body.
O Supreme Bodhisattvas, please accept me completely!
With reverence, I will become your servant. ^2-8


Since I am completely accepted by you,
I will be fearless in existence and bring benefit to all beings.
I will truly turn away from past wrongdoings,
And will commit no further wrongdoings in the future. ^2-9


In a bathing chamber, very fragrant,
With a crystal floor, clear and gleaming,
Adorned with delightful, blazing jewel pillars,
And canopies shimmering with pearls, ^2-10


To the Enlightened Ones and their children,
I offer many jewel vases filled with fragrant water,
Delightfully and perfectly filled, with songs
And various musical instruments, for their bathing. ^2-11


With unparalleled garments,
Pure and imbued with exquisite fragrance, I dry their bodies.
Then, I offer them excellent, dyed,
Very fragrant, sacred robes. ^2-12


With various fine, delicate, and soft garments,
And hundreds of supreme ornaments,
I also adorn the noble Samantabhadra, Manjushri,
Avalokiteshvara, and other Bodhisattvas. ^2-13


With supreme fragrances that fill the entire triple world,
I anoint the bodies of all the mighty Sages,
Who shine and blaze like refined, polished gold,
Making them radiant and brilliant. ^2-14


To the mighty Sages, the supreme objects of offering,
I offer delightful Mandarava flowers, lotuses,
Utpala flowers, and all other fragrant blossoms,
As well as beautiful and well-arranged garlands. ^2-15


I also offer them clouds of incense,
Whose captivating fragrance spreads everywhere.
And I offer them divine food,
Consisting of various delicious edibles and drinks. ^2-16


I also offer jewel lamps
Arranged in rows like golden lotuses.
On the ground, which is spread and anointed with incense,
I scatter delightful loose flowers. ^2-17


Immeasurable palaces, filled with delightful songs of praise,
Adorned with beautiful, blazing pearl and jewel hangings,
And those immeasurable ornaments of space—
I offer all these to those whose nature is compassion. ^2-18


Beautiful jewel parasols with golden handles,
Adorned with delightful fringes of various ornaments,
And those of excellent shape, lovely to behold, hoisted high—
I always offer these to the mighty Sages. ^2-19


Furthermore, may clouds of offerings,
With delightful, sweet musical sounds,
That satisfy the suffering of all beings,
Appear everywhere. ^2-20


May showers of jewels, flowers, and so forth
Continuously fall upon all the sacred teachings,
Upon all stupas and statues,
Without interruption. ^2-21


Just as Manjushri and others
Made offerings to the Victorious Ones,
So too do I offer to the Enlightened Ones,
The Protectors, together with their children. ^2-22


To these oceans of good qualities, I
Praise with oceans of verses of praise.
May these sweet clouds of praise
Definitely arise everywhere. ^2-23


To all the Buddhas of the three times,
And to the supreme Dharma and the supreme community,
I prostrate with bodies
As numerous as all the atoms in the universe. ^2-24


I prostrate to the foundations of the mind of enlightenment,
And to all stupas.
Likewise, to the abbots and teachers,
And to the supreme practitioners, I prostrate. ^2-25


Until I reach the mind of enlightenment,
I take refuge in the Buddhas.
Likewise, I take refuge in the Dharma
And in the community of Bodhisattvas. ^2-26


To the fully enlightened Buddhas
And Bodhisattvas residing in all directions,
All those endowed with great compassion,
I join my palms and make this request: ^2-27


From beginningless cyclic existence,
In this life or in other lives,
Whatever wrongdoings I have committed unknowingly,
Or caused others to commit, ^2-28


And whatever I have rejoiced in,
Overwhelmed by delusion and confusion—
Having seen these faults,
I confess them to the Protectors with all my heart. ^2-29


Whatever harm I have caused
To the Three Jewels, or to my parents, teachers, or others,
Through body, speech, and mind,
Driven by troubling emotions— ^2-30


Whatever extremely unbearable wrongdoings
I, a wrongdoer tainted by many faults,
Have committed—
I confess all of them to the Guides. ^2-31


I may die before my wrongdoings are purified.
Therefore, please protect me quickly,
So that I may certainly be freed from this,
In a swift manner. ^2-32


This Lord of Death is unreliable;
He does not wait for what is done or not done.
Therefore, all, whether sick or not,
Should not trust this fleeting life. ^2-33


Not knowing that I must leave everything behind,
I have committed various wrongdoings
For the sake of friends and enemies,
Because I did not understand this truth. ^2-34


My enemies will cease to exist,
And my friends will also cease to exist.
I too will cease to exist,
And likewise, everything will cease to exist. ^2-35


Just like experiences in a dream,
Whatever things were enjoyed,
Will become mere objects of memory.
Nothing from the past will be seen again. ^2-36


Even in this very lifetime,
Many friends and enemies have passed away.
The unbearable wrongdoings committed for their sake
Remain before me. ^2-37


Since I did not realize
That I am so fleeting,
Driven by delusion, attachment, and hatred,
I committed many kinds of wrongdoings. ^2-38


Day and night, without ceasing,
This life is constantly diminishing.
If there is no addition from elsewhere,
How can I not die? ^2-39


Even while I lie in bed,
Surrounded by all my relatives and friends,
The sensations of life departing
Will be experienced by me alone. ^2-40


When seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death,
What good are relatives? What good are friends?
At that time, only good deeds can protect,
But I have not relied on them. ^2-41


O Protectors, I, being heedless,
Did not realize such a great fear,
And committed many wrongdoings
For the sake of this impermanent life. ^2-42


If a person led today
To a place of execution would be terrified,
With dry mouth, distorted eyes, and so forth,
Appearing different from before, ^2-43


How much more for one seized by the terrifying messengers of the Lord of Death,
With their fearsome appearance,
And afflicted by the great illness of fear?
What need to speak of their utter wretchedness? ^2-44


"Who will protect me well
From this great fear?"
With eyes wide open in terror,
One searches for refuge in all four directions. ^2-45


Seeing no refuge in any direction,
One then becomes utterly despondent.
If there is no refuge in that situation,
What shall I do then? ^2-46


Therefore, to the Victorious Ones, the Protectors of beings,
Who strive to save beings,
The powerful ones who dispel all fears,
I take refuge from this very day. ^2-47


To the Dharma realized by them,
Which dispels the fears of cyclic existence,
And likewise, to the community of Bodhisattvas,
I truly take refuge. ^2-48


Terrified by fear, I
Offer myself to Samantabhadra.
I offer my body
To Manjushri. ^2-49


To the Protector Avalokiteshvara,
Whose compassionate actions are unfailing,
I cry out with a distressed voice, "Please protect me,
A wrongdoer!" ^2-50


To the noble Akashagarbha,
And to Kshitigarbha,
And to all the greatly compassionate Protectors,
I cry out from my heart, seeking refuge. ^2-51


I take refuge in Vajrapani,
Upon seeing whom, the messengers of the Lord of Death
And other enemies flee in terror in all directions,
Scattering in fear. ^2-52


Previously, I transgressed your commands.
Now, having seen this great fear,
I take refuge in you.
Please quickly dispel my fear! ^2-53


If one is afraid of an ordinary illness,
One must follow the doctor's words.
What need to speak of when one is constantly afflicted
By the illness of hundreds of faults like desire and so forth? ^2-54


Even one of these (faults) can destroy
All people dwelling in the world.
If no other medicine to cure them
Can be found anywhere, ^2-55


Then to not follow the words of the All-Knowing Physician,
Who extracts all suffering,
Is truly a sign of extreme delusion
And a cause for blame. ^2-56


If one must be careful
Even at a small, ordinary precipice,
What need to speak of a long fall
Into a chasm thousands of miles deep? ^2-57


It is not right to sit comfortably, thinking,
"I will not die today."
The time when I will cease to exist
Will certainly come. ^2-58


"Who will grant me fearlessness?
How can I certainly escape this?"
If I will inevitably cease to exist,
How can my mind be at ease? ^2-59


What remains for me from what I experienced and perished in the past?
Yet, clinging to that,
I transgressed the Guru's commands,
Being overly attached to it. ^2-60


Since I must leave this life,
And abandon all relatives and friends,
And go somewhere alone,
What good are friends or enemies? ^2-61


Suffering arises from unwholesome actions.
"How can I certainly escape from that?"
It is proper for me to constantly think
Only of this, day and night. ^2-62


Whatever wrongdoings I have committed,
Whether naturally harmful or prohibited by vows,
Due to my ignorance and delusion,
Any of these actions that I have done— ^2-63


Directly before the Protectors,
With palms joined, and with a mind fearful of suffering,
I prostrate again and again,
And confess all of them. ^2-64


O Guides, please consider my wrongdoings
As faults.
Since these are not good,
I will never commit them again in the future. ^2-65


From "Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Way of Life," this is the second chapter, "Confession of Wrongdoings." ^2-a

## 3. Chapter Three: Taking Hold of the Mind of Awakening ^3-0


I rejoice with joy
in the good deeds that bring rest from the suffering
of all beings in states of suffering,
and in the happiness of those who suffer. ^3-1


I rejoice in the good deeds accumulated
that lead to awakening.
I rejoice in the definite liberation
of all beings from the suffering of the cycle of existence.
I rejoice in the awakening of the Protectors,
and in the spiritual paths of the Children of the Buddhas. ^3-2


I rejoice with joy
in the ocean of virtuous aspirations
that bring happiness to all beings,
and in the actions that benefit beings. ^3-3


I pray, with palms joined,
to the Buddhas in all directions:
Please light the lamp of the sacred teachings
for beings bewildered by the darkness of suffering. ^3-4


I pray, with palms joined,
to the Buddhas who wish to pass beyond suffering:
Please do not leave these beings blind,
but remain for countless ages. ^3-5


Having done all these things,
whatever good deeds I have accumulated,
may they remove all the suffering
of all beings. ^3-6


As long as there are sick beings,
until they are cured of their illness,
may I become the medicine, the doctor,
and their nurse. ^3-7


May I rain down food and drink,
removing the suffering of hunger and thirst.
During times of famine between ages,
may I become food and drink. ^3-8


For beings who are destitute and poor,
may I become an inexhaustible treasure,
and may all kinds of necessities
appear before them. ^3-9


My body, as well as my possessions,
and all my good deeds of past, present, and future,
I shall give away without reservation
to accomplish the welfare of all beings. ^3-10


By giving up everything, one passes beyond suffering.
I will accomplish passing beyond suffering.
Giving up everything is for the sake of all,
and giving to beings is supreme. ^3-11


I have given this body
to all beings to do with as they please.
Therefore, they may always kill, insult,
beat, or do whatever they wish. ^3-12


Whether they make my body a plaything,
or a cause for mockery and jest,
since I have given this body away,
what use is it to argue about it? ^3-13


Let them do whatever actions
will not harm them.
May no one ever be without benefit
through me. ^3-14


If, with regard to me,
anger or faith arises in anyone,
may that always become the cause
for all their aims to be accomplished. ^3-15


Whoever speaks ill of me,
or harms others,
or likewise slanders me,
may all of them be destined for awakening. ^3-16


May I be a protector for the unprotected,
a guide for those on the path,
and for those who wish to cross,
a boat, a raft, and a bridge. ^3-17


For those seeking an island, may I be an island,
for those desiring a resting place, a resting place.
May I become a servant
to all beings who wish for one. ^3-18


May I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, a good treasure vase,
an effective spell, a great medicine,
a wish-granting tree,
and a cow of plenty for all beings. ^3-19


Like the great elements,
and like space, may I always
become the basis for the diverse sustenance
of countless beings. ^3-20


Likewise, for the realm of beings
that reaches to the ends of space,
may I always be the cause of their sustenance
until all have passed beyond suffering. ^3-21


Just as the Buddhas of the past
generated the mind of awakening,
and likewise dwelt in the trainings
of an awakening hero, ^3-22


So too, for the benefit of beings,
I shall generate the mind of awakening,
and likewise, I shall train
in those trainings in due order. ^3-23


Thus, having taken hold of
the excellent mind of awakening with wisdom,
and in order to make it flourish to the end,
I shall strengthen my resolve in this way: ^3-24


Today my life has borne fruit;
I have gained this excellent human life.
Today I am born into the Buddha's spiritual family;
today I have become a child of the Buddhas. ^3-25


Now, by all means, I shall undertake
actions befitting this spiritual family,
and I shall ensure that this pure and noble family
is not defiled. ^3-26


Just as a blind person might
find a jewel in a pile of rubbish,
so too, by some chance,
this mind of awakening has arisen in me. ^3-27


This is the supreme nectar
that conquers death itself for beings.
This is also the inexhaustible treasure
that removes the poverty of beings. ^3-28


This is also the supreme medicine
that completely cures the illnesses of beings.
For beings who wander and grow weary on the path through existence,
it is a resting tree. ^3-29


It is a bridge that delivers
all beings from states of suffering.
It is the rising moon of the mind
that dispels the torment of beings' troubles. ^3-30


It is the great sun that completely removes
the ignorance and confusion of beings.
It is the essence of butter that has emerged
from churning the milk of the sacred teachings. ^3-31


For the travelers of existence who wish
to enjoy the delights of happiness,
this is the supreme source of happiness,
that satisfies the great guests, all beings. ^3-32


Today, before all the Protectors, I have invited
all beings to become Buddhas,
and to happiness in the interim.
May the gods and other beings rejoice! ^3-33


From "Engaging in the Way of Life of an Awakening Hero," this is the third chapter, called "Taking Hold of the Mind of Awakening." ^3-a

## 4. Chapter Four: The Teaching on Carefulness ^4-0


Thus, having firmly taken hold
Of the excellent resolve to awaken,
Those on the path should always strive
Diligently, never straying from the training. ^4-1


Whatever is undertaken rashly,
Or not well examined,
Even if it has been vowed,
It is proper to examine whether to do it or abandon it. ^4-2


What the Buddhas and their spiritual children
Have examined with great wisdom,
And what I myself have also examined
Again and again—what reason is there to turn back from that? ^4-3


If, having made such a vow,
I do not accomplish it through action,
Having deceived all those beings,
What will become of me? ^4-4


Even for a small, ordinary thing,
If one thinks in their mind to give it,
But then does not give it,
It is said that they will become a hungry ghost. ^4-5


Having sincerely invited all beings
To the unsurpassed happiness (of awakening),
If one then deceives all beings,
How can one go to happy realms of existence? ^4-6


Even if a person abandons the resolve to awaken,
Yet still works to free others,
The way of karma is inconceivable;
Only the Omniscient One knows. ^4-7


For one on the path to awakening,
This is the heaviest of downfalls,
For if this occurs,
It diminishes the welfare of all beings. ^4-8


Whoever, even for a moment,
Obstructs the merit of this (resolve),
Thereby diminishing the welfare of beings,
Their unfortunate realms of existence will be endless. ^4-9


If one destroys the happiness of even a single being,
One's own self is harmed;
How much more so if one destroys the happiness
Of all beings, limitless as space? ^4-10


Thus, if powerful downfalls
And the powerful resolve to awaken
Alternate in cyclic existence,
It will take a long time to attain the stages of spiritual development. ^4-11


Therefore, just as I have vowed,
I must accomplish it with diligence.
If I do not strive from now on,
I will go lower and lower. ^4-12


Countless Buddhas, who benefit all beings,
Have appeared and passed away,
But due to my own faults,
I have not been within the reach of their healing. ^4-13


If I continue to act in the same way,
Again and again, it will be the same;
In unfortunate realms, I will experience
Illness, bondage, cutting, piercing, and so forth. ^4-14


The appearance of a Buddha,
Faith, and obtaining a human body,
And the ability to practice virtue—such things
Are rare; when will I obtain them again? ^4-15


Even if one is free from illness, like this day,
With food and no harm,
Life is deceptive, fleeting;
The body is like a temporary loan. ^4-16


With such conduct of mine,
I will not even obtain a human body.
If I do not obtain a human body,
There will be only evil, no virtue. ^4-17


When I have the opportunity to practice virtue,
If I do not perform virtuous deeds,
Bewildered by all the suffering of unfortunate realms,
What will I be able to do then? ^4-18


If I do not perform virtuous deeds,
And instead accumulate evil deeds,
Then for hundreds of millions of eons,
I will not even hear the sound of happy realms. ^4-19


For this very reason, the Blessed One
Said that being human is extremely rare,
Like a turtle putting its neck through a yoke
Floating in a vast ocean. ^4-20


If even a single moment of evil action
Can lead to dwelling in the lowest hell for an eon,
Then with the evil accumulated in beginningless cyclic existence,
What need is there to speak of not going to happy realms? ^4-21


Even after experiencing that much,
One is not freed;
For while experiencing that,
Other evil deeds arise. ^4-22


Having obtained such a precious human existence,
If I do not practice virtue,
There is no greater deception than this,
And no greater foolishness than this. ^4-23


If, having understood this,
I still become lazy due to foolishness,
Then at the time of death,
Great sorrow will arise. ^4-24


If the unbearable fires of hell
Burn my body for a long time,
Then the unbearable fire of regret
Will surely torment my mind. ^4-25


Having somehow obtained this extremely rare
And beneficial ground by chance,
While being endowed with understanding,
If I am again led to hell... ^4-26


It is as if I have been deluded by a spell.
My mind here is utterly absent.
I do not even know what deludes me;
What is within me? ^4-27


These enemies—hatred, craving, and so forth—
Have no hands or feet, and so on.
They are not brave or wise, yet how
Have they made me their slave? ^4-28


While dwelling in my own mind,
They harm me with delight.
Not to be angry at them, but to be patient,
Is patience that is inappropriate and worthy of scorn. ^4-29


Even if all gods and demigods
Were to rise up as my enemies,
They could not lead me
Into the fires of the lowest hell. ^4-30


But these powerful enemies, the defilements,
If one encounters them, even Mount Meru
Would not remain as ash.
They can cast me there in a single moment. ^4-31


My enemies, the defilements,
Have existed for a long time, without beginning or end.
All other enemies, however,
Do not last for such a long time. ^4-32


If one serves allies appropriately,
They all bring benefit and happiness.
But if one relies on the defilements,
They bring suffering and harm again and again. ^4-33


These enemies, who have been my continuous foes for so long,
The sole cause for the increase of all harm,
If they are firmly established in my heart,
How can I be fearless and happy in cyclic existence? ^4-34


These guardians of the prison of cyclic existence,
Who become executioners in hell and so forth,
If they remain in the net of attachment that dwells in my mind,
How can I have happiness? ^4-35


As long as I have not clearly vanquished these enemies,
I will not abandon my efforts here.
Even those who are enraged by a small harm
And are full of pride, will not sleep until they have vanquished it. ^4-36


Those who desire to forcefully conquer the defilements,
Which are by nature suffering and mortal, when they face battle,
Disregard the suffering of being struck by arrows, spears, and weapons,
And do not retreat until their aim is achieved. ^4-37


For me, who strives to conquer these enemies,
Who are always the cause of all suffering and truly enemies by nature,
What need is there to speak of not becoming disheartened or lazy
By hundreds of causes of suffering? ^4-38


If wounds inflicted by meaningless enemies
Are cherished on the body like ornaments,
Then for me, who strives correctly to accomplish a great purpose,
Why should suffering be a hindrance? ^4-39


If fishermen, hunters, farmers, and so on,
Thinking only of their own livelihood,
Endure the harms of cold, heat, and so forth,
Why should I not endure for the happiness of all beings? ^4-40


When I vowed to free all beings
In the ten directions, as far as the limits of space, from defilements,
I myself was not yet freed
From the defilements. ^4-41


Not even knowing my own measure,
How is speaking like that not madness?
Therefore, in conquering the defilements,
I must never turn back. ^4-42


I must cling to this (resolve)
And wage war with resentment
Against these kinds of defilements,
Until they are destroyed. ^4-43


Even if I am burned and killed,
Or my head is cut off,
I will never surrender
To the enemy of defilements. ^4-44


Even if an ordinary enemy is driven from one's territory,
They may settle in another land, gather strength,
And then return.
The enemy of defilements is not like that. ^4-45


When the defilements are abandoned by the eye of wisdom,
Where will they go once removed from my mind?
Where will they dwell and come back to harm me?
My attachment to them is simply a lack of effort. ^4-46


The defilements do not dwell in objects, nor in the sense faculties, nor in between.
If they are not elsewhere either, then where do they dwell, these things that harm all beings?
They are like an illusion; therefore, abandon fear in your heart and rely on effort, knowing this.
Why should they needlessly harm me in hell and other realms? ^4-47


Having reflected thus, one should strive
To accomplish the training as it has been taught.
If a patient does not listen to the doctor's words,
How can they be cured by medicine? ^4-48

## 5. Chapter Five: Guarding Awareness ^5-0


Those who wish to keep their training
Must guard their minds with great effort.
If this mind is not guarded,
It is impossible to keep the training. ^5-1


Just as a mind-elephant, let loose,
Causes immeasurable harm,
An untamed, wild elephant
Does not cause such harm in this world. ^5-2


If the mind-elephant is firmly bound
By the rope of complete mindfulness,
All fears will cease,
And all virtues will come to hand. ^5-3


Tigers, lions, elephants, bears,
Snakes, and all enemies,
Guardians of the hell realms,
As well as sorcerers and demons— ^5-4


By binding this one mind,
All of them become bound.
By taming this one mind,
All of them become tamed. ^5-5


Thus, all fears
And immeasurable suffering,
Arise from the mind—
This is taught by the Perfectly Spoken One. ^5-6


Who intentionally created
The weapons of the hell beings?
Who made the burning iron ground?
From what did those masses of fire arise? ^5-7


The Sage declared that all such things
Are products of negative thoughts.
Thus, in the three realms of existence,
There is no fear other than from the mind. ^5-8


If giving away all beings
Were the perfection of generosity,
Then if there are still poor beings now,
How could the Protectors have perfected it before? ^5-9


All possessions are fruitful
When given to all beings with a mind of relinquishment.
This is taught as the perfection of generosity,
Therefore, it is the mind itself. ^5-10


Wherever fish and other creatures are,
They are driven away so they are not killed.
But the perfection of discipline is said to be
Achieved by gaining the mind of renunciation. ^5-11


Untamed beings are like space;
They cannot possibly be destroyed.
If this one mind of anger is overcome,
It is like overcoming all those enemies. ^5-12


To cover all this earth
With leather, how much would be enough?
But with just a piece of leather for a shoe sole,
It is like covering the entire earth. ^5-13


Similarly, external things
Cannot be turned away by me.
But I will turn away this mind of mine;
What need is there to turn away others? ^5-14


The result of generating a single clear thought,
Like becoming a Brahma god and so on,
Is not matched by the results of body and speech,
Because their actions are weaker. ^5-15


All recitations and ascetic practices,
Even if performed for a long time,
When done with a distracted mind,
Are declared by the wise to be meaningless. ^5-16


If one does not know this secret of the mind,
The supreme essence of the Dharma,
Even if one wishes to gain happiness and overcome suffering,
Such people wander aimlessly and fruitlessly. ^5-17


Therefore, this mind of mine
Must be well-held and well-guarded.
Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind,
What good are many other disciplines? ^5-18


Just as one who lives among fierce, wild people
Carefully guards a wound,
So too, one living among bad people
Should always guard this wound of the mind. ^5-19


If even those who fear a small pain from a wound
Guard that wound,
Why would one not guard the mind's wound
From the fears of crushing and dismemberment? ^5-20


If one lives with such conduct,
Whether among bad people,
Or even among women,
The steadfastness of one striving for discipline will not be harmed. ^5-21


My gains, respect,
Body, and livelihood may be lost;
Other virtues may also be lost,
But the mind should never be lost. ^5-22


To those who wish to guard their minds,
I implore you with folded hands:
Guard mindfulness and awareness
With all your effort! ^5-23


Just as people afflicted by illness
Are powerless in all their tasks,
Similarly, those whose minds are disturbed by delusion
Are powerless in all their tasks. ^5-24


Even hearing, contemplating, and meditating,
For one whose mind lacks awareness,
Will not remain in memory,
Like water in a leaky pot. ^5-25


Many who are learned, faithful,
And diligently strive,
Because they are afflicted by the fault of lacking awareness,
Become stained by downfalls. ^5-26


Thieves of unawareness,
Following the loss of mindfulness,
Even if one has accumulated merit,
Will lead one to the lower realms, like robbers stealing it away. ^5-27


This gang of afflictive thieves
Always seeks an opportunity.
Once they find an opening, they steal virtue,
And even destroy the life of fortunate rebirths. ^5-28


Therefore, never let mindfulness
Depart from the gate of the mind.
If it does, recall the harms of the lower realms,
And bring it back immediately. ^5-29


Through associating with spiritual teachers,
And following the instructions of masters,
Mindfulness easily arises
For those who are fortunate and respectful, due to fear. ^5-30


Buddhas and Bodhisattvas
Possess unobstructed vision everywhere.
Always consider that 'I am present
Before the eyes of all of them.' ^5-31


Thinking thus, one will feel shame,
Respect, and fear, and will be cautious.
Therefore, the recollection of the Buddhas
Will arise in one again and again. ^5-32


When mindfulness remains
At the gate of the mind for the purpose of guarding,
Then awareness will arise,
And even what has been lost will return. ^5-33


First, knowing that this mind
Is flawed, at that time,
I should remain like a piece of wood,
Holding firm. ^5-34


I should never look around
Aimlessly or distractedly.
Instead, with focused attention, always
Keep my gaze lowered. ^5-35


For the purpose of resting the gaze,
One may occasionally look around.
If someone appears in sight,
One should look and say, 'Welcome!' ^5-36


To check for dangers on the path and so on,
Look in the four directions again and again.
After resting, turn your head back
And look behind you. ^5-37


After checking ahead and behind,
One should go or come.
In all situations, therefore,
One should act with knowledge of what is appropriate. ^5-38


Having prepared to keep the body in a certain way,
Then, from time to time,
One should observe how this body
Is actually positioned. ^5-39


The wild elephant of the mind
Should be examined with all effort
So that it does not escape from the great pillar
Of focusing on the Dharma, to which it is tied. ^5-40


So that the striving for meditative concentration
Does not waver even for a moment,
One should examine one's mind individually:
'Where is my mind engaged?' ^5-41


If one is unable to do so due to fear, celebration, or similar circumstances,
Act as is convenient.
For example, during times of giving,
It is taught that discipline may be set aside. ^5-42


Whatever one has decided to do,
Do not think of anything else.
With a mind focused on that task,
One should accomplish it for the time being. ^5-43


If one does so, all will be well done;
Otherwise, neither will be accomplished.
If one acts with awareness, then
The minor afflictions will not increase. ^5-44


When engaging in various idle chatter
And many wondrous spectacles,
One should abandon attachment to them. ^5-45


If one finds oneself digging aimlessly, cutting grass,
Or drawing on the ground, and so on,
Recall the training of the Sugatas,
And abandon it immediately with fear. ^5-46


Whenever one wishes to move
Or wishes to speak,
First, examine one's own mind,
And then act with steady awareness. ^5-47


Whenever one's mind desires attachment
Or desires anger,
Do not act, do not speak,
But remain like a piece of wood. ^5-48


If one is prone to laughter or mockery,
Or if one is arrogant or conceited,
Or has the intention to expose faults,
Or has a deceitful or flattering mind, ^5-49


Whenever one is eager to praise oneself,
Or to criticize others,
Or is prone to scolding or regret,
At that time, remain like a piece of wood. ^5-50


If one desires gain, respect, or fame,
Or desires to seek attendants,
Or if one's mind desires service,
At that time, remain like a piece of wood. ^5-51


If one neglects the welfare of others,
Or desires to pursue one's own interests,
Or if a mind desiring to speak arises,
Then one should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-52


If impatience, laziness, fear,
Or recklessness, idle talk,
Or a mind clinging to one's own side arises,
At that time, remain like a piece of wood. ^5-53


Thus, having examined the mind for all afflictions
And for pointless striving,
At that time, the hero should firmly grasp it
With the antidote. ^5-54


With great certainty and strong faith,
Steadfastness, respect, and humility,
With a sense of shame and fear,
Strive to be calm and to please others. ^5-55


Do not be disheartened by the conflicting
Desires of foolish people.
Instead, with compassion, think: 'These minds
Are overcome by afflictions.' ^5-56


In blameless matters,
Exert control over oneself and other beings.
Always hold this mind as if it were an illusion,
Without a self. ^5-57


Having repeatedly reflected on the supreme leisure
Obtained after a long time,
Hold the mind like Mount Meru,
Completely unmoving. ^5-58


Even if vultures, clinging to flesh,
Drag it back and forth everywhere,
If you, mind, do not dislike it,
Why do you now make a fuss? ^5-59


Having taken this body as 'mine',
Why do you, mind, protect it?
If you and this body are separate,
What can it possibly do for you? ^5-60


Foolish mind, why do you not
Take a clean wooden image?
What good is it to protect this decaying machine
Of impure aggregates? ^5-61


First, separate this layer of skin
With your own intellect.
Then, separate the flesh from the network of bones
With the knife of wisdom. ^5-62


After separating the bones,
Examine down to the marrow.
Ask yourself, 'What essence is there in this?'
And investigate it yourself. ^5-63


If, after striving to search in this way,
You find no essence there,
Then why do you still, with attachment,
Protect this body? ^5-64


You cannot eat impurities,
Nor can you drink blood.
You cannot even lick intestines,
So what can this body do for you? ^5-65


Therefore, it is fitting for foxes and vultures
To protect this (body) for food.
But this human body
Is merely something to be used. ^5-66


Even if you protect it in this way,
If the merciless Lord of Death
Seizes it and gives it to birds and dogs,
What can you do then? ^5-67


If one does not give clothes and so on
To a servant who cannot be employed,
Then if this body, even when fed, goes elsewhere,
Why do you feed it so lavishly? ^5-68


Having given it its wages,
Now let it do its own work.
Without benefit, do not
Give everything to it. ^5-69


Regard the body merely as a boat
For going and coming.
In order to accomplish the welfare of beings,
Transform it into a wish-fulfilling body. ^5-70


Since you have such freedom,
Always keep a smiling face.
Completely abandon frowning and scowling,
And be a friend to beings, speaking gently. ^5-71


Do not carelessly throw down chairs and so on
With a loud noise.
Do not open doors forcefully,
But always be quiet and joyful. ^5-72


Just as a heron, a cat, or a thief
Creeps silently to accomplish their desired aim,
The Sage always acts in the same way. ^5-73


Be skilled at encouraging others,
And accept with respect, bowing your head,
Words that offer unsolicited help.
Always be a student to everyone. ^5-74


To all who speak well,
One should say, 'Well said!'
If one sees someone performing virtuous deeds,
Generate joy by praising them well. ^5-75


Praise virtues in secret,
And if virtues are praised, echo them.
If one's own virtues are praised,
Recognize it as an understanding of virtues. ^5-76


All efforts are for joy,
Which is rare even if bought with a price.
Therefore, experience the joy
Of others' virtues. ^5-77


There is no loss for oneself here,
And great happiness in the future.
But by wrongdoing, one is unhappy and suffers,
And there is great suffering in the future. ^5-78


When speaking, speak with conviction and relevance,
With clear meaning and pleasantness,
Having abandoned attachment and hatred,
Speak gently and appropriately. ^5-79


Even when looking at beings with one's eyes,
Think, 'Through these very beings, I
Shall become a Buddha.'
Look with sincerity and loving-kindness. ^5-80


Always be inspired by strong aspiration,
Or by the antidote itself.
Virtue and benefit, and
Great goodness for those who suffer, will arise. ^5-81


Being wise and energetic,
I should always perform tasks myself.
In all tasks, one should not
Rely on anyone else. ^5-82


Practice the perfection of generosity and so on,
Advancing higher and higher.
Do not abandon the great for the small,
But primarily consider the welfare of others. ^5-83


Having understood this, always
Strive for the welfare of others.
Those with great compassion, who see far,
Even permit what is forbidden in such cases. ^5-84


Distribute (food) to those who have fallen into error, the unprotected,
And those who uphold discipline.
Eat only a moderate amount,
And give away everything except the three Dharma robes. ^5-85


Do not harm this body, which practices the sacred Dharma,
For trivial reasons.
If one acts in this way, the aspirations of beings
Will quickly be fulfilled. ^5-86


Do not give up this body
Until the intention of compassion is pure.
Instead, use it as a cause to accomplish great aims
Both in this life and in future lives. ^5-87


Do not teach Dharma to those who lack respect,
Nor to those who wear head coverings while not ill,
Or carry umbrellas, staffs, or weapons,
Or have their heads covered. ^5-88


Do not teach profound and vast Dharma to those of lesser capacity,
Nor to women without a man present.
Practice all aspects of the Dharma, both lesser and supreme,
With equal respect. ^5-89


One who is a vessel for the vast Dharma
Should not be made to engage in lesser Dharma.
Do not completely abandon one's conduct,
And do not be misled by sutras and tantras. ^5-90


When discarding tooth-sticks and saliva,
One should cover them.
It is improper to discard urine and so on
Into water or on open ground that is used by others. ^5-91


Do not eat with a full mouth, or with noise,
Or with an open mouth.
Do not sit with legs outstretched,
And do not rub your hands together. ^5-92


On vehicles, beds, or other places,
Do not be alone with another woman.
All things that cause disrespect in the world,
Should be abandoned after observing and inquiring. ^5-93


Do not point with a finger.
Instead, with respect, use your right hand
To indicate the path in that manner. ^5-94


Do not move your hand excessively,
But move it slightly and make a sound,
Like snapping your fingers, to signal.
Otherwise, it will be unrestrained. ^5-95


Lie down on your preferred side,
Like the Protector who entered nirvana.
Firmly resolve from the very beginning
To rise quickly with awareness. ^5-96


Since the conduct of Bodhisattvas
Is taught to be immeasurable,
One should certainly practice the conduct
Of purifying the mind to that extent. ^5-97


Three times during the day and night,
Recite the three aggregates.
By relying on the Buddhas and the Bodhisattva mind,
Pacify the remaining downfalls with that. ^5-98


Whether under one's own control or another's,
Whatever one does in any situation,
One should diligently train in that situation
Which is taught as part of the training. ^5-99


There is nothing that the children of the Buddhas
Do not train in.
For a wise person who lives in that way,
There is no merit that will not arise. ^5-100


Whether directly or indirectly,
Do not engage in anything other than the welfare of beings.
For the sake of beings alone,
Dedicate everything towards enlightenment. ^5-101


Always, a virtuous spiritual friend
Who is skilled in the meaning of the Great Vehicle,
And the supreme discipline of a Bodhisattva,
Should not be abandoned even for the sake of one's life. ^5-102


Train according to the way of relying on a spiritual teacher,
As described in the biography of Paljung.
This and other teachings spoken by the Buddha
Should be understood by reading the sutras. ^5-103


The training is evident in the sutras;
Therefore, one should read the sutras.
The Sutra of Akasagarbha (Essence of Space)
Should be examined first of all. ^5-104


Because it extensively teaches
What is to be practiced constantly,
The Compendium of Trainings (Sikṣāsamuccaya)
Should also be certainly examined again and again. ^5-105


Alternatively, for now, examine the concise
Compendium of all Sutras.
Also, diligently examine the two works
Composed by the noble Nāgārjuna. ^5-106


Whatever is not forbidden
Should be practiced.
To guard the mind of the world,
Having seen the training, practice it correctly. ^5-107


Repeatedly examining
The states of body and mind,
This alone, in short,
Is the definition of guarding awareness. ^5-108


These things should be practiced with the body.
What is accomplished by merely speaking words?
Will merely reading medical texts
Benefit the sick? ^5-109

## 6. Chapter Six: The Teaching on Patience ^6-0

Accumulated over a thousand eons,
acts of generosity, offerings to enlightened ones, and so on—
all such good deeds,
are destroyed by a single moment of anger. ^6-1


There is no wrongdoing like hatred,
and no austerity like patience.
Therefore, diligently
cultivate patience in various ways. ^6-2


If you hold a mind pierced by the dart of hatred,
your mind will never experience peace.
You will not find joy or happiness,
you will not sleep, and you will become unstable. ^6-3


Even those who have been helped
with wealth and respect
will turn against a master
who is filled with hatred, even seeking to kill him. ^6-4


Friends become disheartened by it;
even if won over by generosity, they will not stay.
In short, no one
who is angry can live in peace. ^6-5


The enemy, anger, causes
such suffering.
Whoever strives to conquer anger
will be happy in this life and the next. ^6-6


When something undesirable happens,
or something desirable is hindered,
the mind finds the food of unhappiness,
and hatred grows, destroying oneself. ^6-7


Therefore, I must destroy
the food of that enemy (hatred).
For this enemy has no other function
than to harm me. ^6-8


Whatever happens, I must not
disturb my joyful mind.
Even if I become unhappy, my desires will not be fulfilled,
and my good deeds will be ruined. ^6-9


If something can be remedied,
what is the point of unhappiness?
If it cannot be remedied,
what good does unhappiness do? ^6-10


I do not wish for myself or my friends
to experience suffering, contempt, harsh words,
or unpleasant speech.
Why then would I wish the opposite for my enemies? ^6-11


Causes of happiness arise only sometimes,
but causes of suffering are very many.
Without suffering, there is no liberation.
Therefore, mind, be firm. ^6-12


Some endure the pain of hardship, faith,
and ascetic practices, such as burning and cutting,
for no purpose, or for the sake of liberation.
Why should I not endure them for a greater purpose? ^6-13


There is nothing that cannot become easy
through familiarity.
Therefore, by becoming accustomed to minor harm,
endure great harm. ^6-14


Have you not seen the meaningless suffering
of snakes, mosquitoes,
hunger, thirst, and itching,
and so on? ^6-15


I must not be delicate about
heat, cold, rain, wind,
illness, bondage, or beatings, and so on.
If I am, the harm will increase. ^6-16


Some, seeing their own blood,
become even more courageous.
Others, seeing the blood of others,
faint and collapse. ^6-17


This comes from the mind's steadfastness
and previous habits.
Therefore, disregard harm
and be untouched by suffering. ^6-18


Even if suffering arises, the wise
do not let their minds become agitated or disturbed.
When fighting the troubling emotions,
there is much harm during the battle. ^6-19


Disregarding all suffering,
those who conquer the enemy of hatred and so on
are true heroes.
The rest merely kill corpses. ^6-20


Furthermore, the benefits of suffering are:
it removes arrogance through weariness,
it generates compassion for those in the cycle of existence,
it makes one avoid wrongdoing and delight in good deeds. ^6-21


If you do not get angry at the great sources
of suffering like bile and so on,
why get angry at sentient beings?
All of them are driven by conditions. ^6-22


Just as illness arises
even though one does not wish for it,
so too do troubling emotions arise
even though one does not wish for them. ^6-23


Even without intending to be angry,
people become angry.
Even without intending to generate it,
anger arises in the same way. ^6-24


All faults and
various wrongdoings
arise from the power of conditions;
they are not independent. ^6-25


Those assembled conditions also
have no intention to produce anything.
And what is produced by them
has no intention, thinking, "I am produced." ^6-26


Whatever is desired as a primary cause,
or whatever is conceived as a self,
does not arise with the specific intention,
"I myself will arise." ^6-27


If it does not exist before it arises,
what then is its desire to arise?
And that which is constantly distracted by objects
cannot cease. ^6-28


If that self were permanent,
it would be seen as inactive, like space.
Even if it met with other conditions,
what could be done to that which does not change? ^6-29


If, even when acting, it remains as before,
what has the agent done to it?
What connection could there be,
saying, "This is its action"? ^6-30


Thus, everything is dependent on others.
And because of that dependence, it is not independent.
Knowing this, one will not get angry
at any phenomenon, just as one would not at a snake. ^6-31


If you say, "What should be stopped by what?
And stopping is not logical,"
then desiring the cessation of suffering
by relying on that (understanding) is not illogical. ^6-32


Therefore, whether it is an enemy or a friend,
if you see them acting improperly,
think, "This arose from such conditions,"
and thus maintain peace of mind. ^6-33


If things happened as one wished,
since no one desires suffering,
all living beings
would never experience suffering. ^6-34


Due to carelessness, I myself
inflict harm upon myself, such as thorns.
To obtain women and so on,
I become greedy and fast, among other things. ^6-35


Some hang themselves and jump into chasms,
eat poison and incompatible foods,
and through unwholesome actions,
inflict harm upon themselves. ^6-36


When under the power of troubling emotions,
one even kills oneself, who is dear.
How then could they not harm the bodies of others? ^6-37


If, when troubling emotions arise,
one engages in self-harm and so on,
and vast compassion does not arise,
what is the point of getting angry? ^6-38


If harming others
is the nature of foolish people,
it is not logical to be angry at them,
just as it is not logical to blame fire for its nature to burn. ^6-39


But if that fault is temporary,
and the nature of sentient beings is pure,
then it is still not logical to be angry,
just as it is rare for smoke to obscure the sky. ^6-40


If you get angry at the one who wields
a stick or other object to strike,
that person too is driven by hatred.
So it is more logical to be angry at hatred itself. ^6-41


In the past, I inflicted
similar harm upon sentient beings.
Therefore, it is fitting that this harm
now comes to me from those who harm sentient beings. ^6-42


His weapon and my body
are both causes of suffering.
If he wields the weapon and my body is exposed,
at whom should I be angry? ^6-43


A boil, like a human form,
unbearable to touch, full of suffering.
If I, full of craving, grasp it,
at whom should I be angry for the harm? ^6-44


Foolish people do not desire suffering,
yet they crave the causes of suffering.
If they are harmed by their own faults,
what is there to blame others for? ^6-45


Just as the guardians of hell
and forests of sword-leaves,
are created by one's own actions,
at whom should one be angry? ^6-46


Driven by my own actions,
those who harm me arise.
If these beings go to hell because of this,
have I not ruined them? ^6-47


By relying on these (harms),
I accumulate much wrongdoing, failing in patience.
By relying on me, those (harms)
will go to hell and suffer for a long time. ^6-48


I harm them,
but they benefit me.
Why then, perversely,
do you, unruly mind, get angry? ^6-49


If I had good intentions,
I would not go to hell.
If I protected myself,
what harm would come to them here? ^6-50


But if I return harm for harm,
they will not be protected,
and my own conduct will be ruined.
Thus, my austerity will be destroyed. ^6-51


The mind is not physical,
so no one can destroy it anywhere.
Because of strong attachment to the body,
the body is harmed by suffering. ^6-52


If contempt, harsh words,
and unpleasant speech
do not harm the body,
why, mind, do you get so angry? ^6-53


If others' dislike of me
does not consume me in this life or the next,
why should I not want it?
Why should I not want it? ^6-54


If I dislike this because it hinders gain,
then I should abandon my gain here,
but wrongdoing will remain firm.
My gain here will be lost, but wrongdoing will remain firm. ^6-55


It is better for me to die right now
than to live long by wrong livelihood.
Even if I were to live for a long time,
the suffering of death would still be the same. ^6-56


One who experiences happiness for a hundred years in a dream
and then awakens,
and another who experiences happiness for a moment
and then awakens— ^6-57


For both of them, once awakened,
that happiness is gone.
Both a long life and a short life
are the same at the time of death. ^6-58


Even if one obtains much gain
and enjoys happiness for a long time,
just like one stripped by robbers,
one goes naked and empty-handed. ^6-59


If you say, "I will live by gain,
exhaust wrongdoing, and perform good deeds,"
then if you become angry for the sake of gain,
will your good deeds not be exhausted and wrongdoing not arise? ^6-60


If the very purpose for which I live
is ruined,
what good is a life
that only commits wrongdoing? ^6-61


If you get angry at unpleasant speech
because it harms sentient beings,
why do you not get angry in the same way
at your own unpleasant speech towards others? ^6-62


If you tolerate those who lack faith,
because their lack of faith depends on others,
why do you not tolerate unpleasant speech,
since it depends on the arising of troubling emotions? ^6-63


Even towards those who mock and destroy
images, stupas, and the sacred teachings,
I should not feel hatred,
for the Buddhas and so on are not harmed. ^6-64


Even towards those who harm
gurus, relatives, and friends,
seeing that they act due to conditions,
turn away from anger. ^6-65


If harm is caused by both
sentient beings with minds and inanimate objects without minds,
why single out those with minds for blame?
Therefore, endure the harm. ^6-66


Some commit faults due to ignorance.
Others, ignorant, become angry.
Who among them is faultless?
And who among them is truly at fault? ^6-67


Why did I previously perform the actions
that cause others to harm me?
If everything depends on actions,
why should I blame this (person)? ^6-68


Seeing this, I must strive
to perform good deeds, so that all beings
may become mutually loving.
I must strive to perform good deeds. ^6-69


Just as when a house catches fire,
and the fire spreads to another house,
whatever dry grass and so on it spreads to,
that must be removed and discarded. ^6-70


Similarly, wherever the fire of hatred
spreads due to attachment,
fearing that good deeds will be burned,
one must immediately abandon that (attachment). ^6-71


If a person condemned to death has a hand cut off
and is thereby freed, how is that not good?
If by human suffering
one is freed from hell, how is that not good? ^6-72


If I cannot endure even this much present suffering,
then why do I not turn away from anger,
which is the cause of hellish suffering?
Why do I not turn away from anger? ^6-73


For the sake of desires, one experiences
thousands of burnings and so on in hell,
yet I have not accomplished my own purpose
or the purpose of others. ^6-74


This (present suffering) is not so harmful,
and great purposes can be accomplished by it.
Therefore, it is fitting to rejoice
in suffering that removes harm from beings. ^6-75


If, when others praise those with good qualities,
you obtain the happiness of joy,
why do you not also rejoice
by praising them yourself, mind? ^6-76


This happiness of your joy
is a blameless source of happiness.
It is approved by those with good qualities,
and it is also the supreme way to attract others. ^6-77


Furthermore, if you do not wish for this happiness (of your own joy),
even when others become happy,
then because you abandon giving rewards and so on,
both visible and invisible benefits will be lost. ^6-78


When your own good qualities are praised,
you wish for others to be happy too.
But when others' good qualities are praised,
you do not wish for your own happiness. ^6-79


Having generated the thought of awakening,
desiring all sentient beings to be happy,
if sentient beings find happiness on their own,
why then should you be angry? ^6-80


If you desire sentient beings to become Buddhas,
who are worshipped by the three worlds,
why do you grieve when you see them
receiving even a little respect? ^6-81


If someone you support
receives support from a relative,
would you not rejoice,
or would you get angry instead? ^6-82


If you do not wish for beings to prosper,
what is your desire for their awakening?
How can one who gets angry at others' prosperity
have the thought of awakening? ^6-83


If he obtains it from that (patron),
or if it remains in the patron's house,
and it is not yours in any case,
what does it matter whether it is given or not? ^6-84


Why do you abandon good deeds, faith,
and your own good qualities?
If one does not hold onto what is gained,
tell me, who would not get angry? ^6-85


End of the Second Section. ^6-86


Even if an enemy is unhappy,
what joy is there for you in that?
Your mere wish
will not be a cause of harm to them. ^6-87


Even if that suffering were to come about by your wish,
what joy would there be for you?
If you say, "I would be satisfied,"
what greater ruin could there be than that? ^6-88


This unbearable hook of troubling emotions,
cast by the fisherman of afflictions,
once caught, my mind will be cooked
by the guardians of hell in the pot of hell. ^6-89


Praise and fame
do not become good deeds, nor do they greatly increase them.
They do not increase my strength, nor do they make me free from illness.
Nor do they make my body happy. ^6-90


If I know my own purpose,
what self-interest is there in that (praise and fame)?
If I only desire mental happiness,
then I should rely on ornaments, alcohol, and so on. ^6-91


If for the sake of fame, wealth is lost,
and I even kill myself,
what good are mere words?
Who would be happy with that flesh? ^6-92


When a sandcastle collapses,
children cry out in distress.
Similarly, when praise and fame are lost,
my own mind is like a child's. ^6-93


Since sound has no mind,
it cannot have the intention to praise me.
Is it the fame that others are happy with me
that is the cause of my joy? ^6-94


Whether it is others or myself,
what good does others' happiness do for me?
That joy and happiness belong to them alone;
I do not receive any share of it. ^6-95


If I am happy because they are happy,
then I should do the same for everyone.
How can I rejoice in others' happiness,
and yet not be happy myself? ^6-96


Therefore, the joy that arises in me
when I think, "I am praised,"
is also not logical in that way,
and is merely childish behavior. ^6-97


Praise and so on distract me.
They also destroy weariness (of samsara).
They cause envy towards those with good qualities,
and destroy prosperity. ^6-98


Therefore, those who are present
to destroy my praise and so on—
have I not engaged with them
to protect myself from falling into lower realms? ^6-99


I seek liberation,
and do not need the bondage of gain and respect.
How can I be angry at those
who free me from bondage? ^6-100


I wish to enter suffering,
just as Buddhas bless.
How can I be angry at those
who have become the very gateway to not abandoning (beings)? ^6-101


It is not logical to be angry at them,
thinking, "This hinders my good deeds."
If there is no austerity equal to patience,
should I not rely on it (patience)? ^6-102


If, due to my own faults,
I do not practice patience towards this (harm),
then I am merely hindering
the very cause of good deeds that is present here. ^6-103


That without which something does not arise,
and which, when present, causes it to arise,
is indeed its cause.
How can that be called a hindrance? ^6-104


A beggar who arrives at the right time
is not a hindrance to generosity.
One who causes renunciation to arise
should not be called a hindrance to renunciation. ^6-105


In this world, there are many beggars,
but those who cause harm are rare.
For if one does not harm in return,
no one will cause harm. ^6-106


Therefore, just as a treasure appears
in a house without effort,
I should rejoice in my enemies,
for they are companions on the path to awakening. ^6-107


Since this (enemy) and I have accomplished (patience),
the fruit of patience
should first be offered to this (enemy).
For this (enemy) is the cause of patience. ^6-108


If you say, "This enemy should not be honored,
because they have no intention to cultivate patience,"
then why do you honor the sacred teachings,
which are also a cause for accomplishment? ^6-109


If you say, "This enemy should not be honored,
because they intend to cause harm,"
then if one strives to benefit like a doctor,
how can my patience be accomplished? ^6-110


Therefore, since patience arises
by relying on a mind full of hatred,
that (hatred) is indeed the cause of patience.
Thus, it is fitting to honor it like the sacred teachings. ^6-111


Therefore, the Buddha taught that sentient beings
are a field of merit, just like the field of the Victorious Ones.
Many, by pleasing these (beings),
have thus perfected the transcendent qualities. ^6-112


Since the Dharma of Buddhahood is accomplished
through both sentient beings and the Buddhas,
why should one not show respect to sentient beings
in the same way one shows respect to the Buddhas? ^6-113


The good qualities of intention are not inherent;
they come from the result. Similarly,
sentient beings also have good qualities.
Therefore, they are equal. ^6-114


The offering made to those with loving-kindness
is the greatness of sentient beings.
The good deeds of faith in the Buddhas
are also the greatness of the Buddhas. ^6-115


Since there is a part (of the cause) for the accomplishment of Buddhahood,
they are considered equal in that respect.
But no one is equal to the Buddhas,
whose ocean of good qualities is boundless. ^6-116


Even if a mere fraction of the supreme collection
of good qualities appears in someone,
to make an offering to them,
even offering the three realms would be too little. ^6-117


Since sentient beings possess a fraction
of the supreme Dharma that gives rise to Buddhahood,
it is fitting to honor sentient beings,
aligning with this much (quality). ^6-118


Furthermore, to those who have become unwavering friends
and performed immeasurable benefits,
what else could repay their kindness
apart from pleasing sentient beings? ^6-119


Since benefiting those who give up their bodies
and enter the lowest hell repays their kindness,
therefore, even if these (beings) cause great harm,
one should treat them with all goodness. ^6-120


Since my own masters
do not even care for their own bodies,
how can I, a foolish person,
be arrogant and not act as their servant? ^6-121


Those whose happiness makes the Buddhas rejoice,
and whose harm causes them displeasure—
by their happiness, all Buddhas rejoice;
by harming them, one harms the Buddhas. ^6-122


Just as one whose body is completely ablaze
cannot find mental peace through any desire,
similarly, if sentient beings are harmed,
there is no way for the Great Compassionate Ones to rejoice. ^6-123


Therefore, whatever displeasure
all the Great Compassionate Ones felt because I harmed beings,
I confess that wrongdoing individually today.
May the Buddhas forgive that displeasure. ^6-124


To please the Buddhas,
from today onwards, I go as a servant to the world.
Let the multitude of beings step on my head,
or kill me; I will not resist. May the Protectors of the world be pleased. ^6-125


There is no doubt that all these beings
are considered as one's own by the Great Compassionate Ones.
Why then should I not respect these very beings,
whom the Protectors see as their own essence? ^6-126


This (pleasing beings) is what pleases the Buddhas.
This is also the true accomplishment of one's own purpose.
This also removes the suffering of the world.
Therefore, I must always do this. ^6-127


For example, if a king's servant
harms many people,
those with foresight,
even if capable, will not harm him in return. ^6-128


For he is not alone;
the king's power is his power.
Similarly, one should not despise and kill
any seemingly weak person who causes harm. ^6-129


For the guardians of hell
and the Great Compassionate Ones are their allies.
Therefore, one should please sentient beings,
just as common people please a cruel king. ^6-130


Even if a king gets angry,
the suffering that one experiences
by displeasing sentient beings—
does that (suffering) cause the harm of hell? ^6-131


Even if a king is pleased,
what one obtains by pleasing sentient beings
cannot be given even by Buddhahood.
It is impossible for Buddhahood to bestow it. ^6-132


Let alone the future accomplishment of Buddhahood
that arises from pleasing sentient beings—
have you not seen great glory,
fame, and happiness in this very life? ^6-133


While in the cycle of existence, through patience, one obtains
beauty and so on, freedom from illness, and fame,
a very long life,
and the great happiness of a universal monarch. ^6-134


From "Engaging in the Conduct of a Bodhisattva," this is the sixth chapter, called "The Teaching on Patience." ^6-a

## 7. The Practice of Diligence ^7-0


Thus, with patience, cultivate diligence.
Enlightenment rests on such diligence.
Just as there is no movement without wind,
Merit does not arise without diligence. ^7-1


Diligence is enthusiasm for what is good.
Its opposing factors will now be explained:
Laziness, attachment to unwholesome things,
Despondency, and self-contempt. ^7-2


Indolence, tasting the flavor of comfort,
And craving that relies on sleep,
Not being weary of the suffering in cyclic existence—
These give rise to laziness. ^7-3


Entangled by the net of afflictions,
Caught in the net of rebirth,
Having fallen into the mouth of the Lord of Death—
Why do you still not know this? ^7-4


Haven't you seen your own kind
Being gradually killed?
Yet, relying on sleep,
You are like a wild beast or a buffalo. ^7-5


With all paths blocked,
And the Lord of Death watching,
How can you delight in eating,
And how can you delight in sleeping like this? ^7-6


Since you will die very soon,
Accumulate provisions while you can.
Even if you abandon laziness then,
What good will it do when it's too late? ^7-7


"This was not done, this was begun,
This was half-finished"—while you are in such a state,
The Lord of Death suddenly arrives,
And you will think, "Alas, I am undone!" ^7-8


With eyes swollen red from intense grief,
Tears streaming down your face,
Your relatives having lost all hope,
You will gaze upon the faces of Yama's messengers. ^7-9


Tormented by the memory of your own misdeeds,
And hearing the sounds of hell,
Terrified, your body soiled with impurities,
What will you do when you are overwhelmed? ^7-10


If in this life you are filled with such fear,
Like a live fish writhing,
What need is there to speak of the unbearable
Sufferings of hell for those who commit misdeeds? ^7-11


When touched by boiling water,
Even a young person feels intense heat.
Having committed actions that lead to hell,
Why do you remain so comfortably? ^7-12


Desiring results without diligence,
And prone to harm due to weakness,
Though seized by death, you are like a god—
Alas, you are overcome by suffering! ^7-13


Relying on the boat of human existence,
Cross the great river of suffering.
Since this boat is hard to find again,
Fool, do not sleep at this time! ^7-14


Abandoning the supreme joy of the Dharma,
Which is the cause of endless delight,
And being distracted by causes of suffering,
Why do you delight in frivolity and such? ^7-15


Non-despondency, gathering strength,
Taking on the burden, self-mastery,
Equating oneself with others,
And exchanging oneself with others—cultivate these. ^7-16


Do not become despondent, thinking,
"How can I attain enlightenment?"
For the Tathagata, who speaks the truth,
Has spoken this truth: ^7-17


Even a fly, a flesh-fly, a bee,
Or any kind of insect,
If it cultivates the power of diligence,
Can attain supreme, hard-to-reach enlightenment. ^7-18


I, born as a human of noble lineage,
And knowing what is beneficial and what is harmful,
If I do not abandon the conduct of a bodhisattva,
Why should I not attain enlightenment? ^7-19


But if I fear that I must
Give up my limbs and so forth,
Without weighing the heavy and the light,
My fear is merely due to ignorance. ^7-20


For countless eons,
My body has been cut many times,
Pierced, burned, and torn apart,
Yet I have not attained enlightenment. ^7-21


The suffering I endure to achieve enlightenment
Is limited and purposeful,
Like the pain of lancing a wound
To remove a deeply embedded thorn. ^7-22


All doctors, through their treatments,
Eliminate the discomfort of illness.
Therefore, to overcome many sufferings,
One should endure minor discomfort. ^7-23


Such ordinary treatments
Are not performed by the supreme physician.
With extremely gentle methods,
He cures immeasurable grave illnesses. ^7-24


The Guide first teaches giving
Of vegetables and such.
Having become accustomed to that, later,
One gradually gives even one's own flesh. ^7-25


When one develops the perception
Of one's own body as mere vegetables and such,
Then giving away one's flesh and so forth—
What difficulty is there in that? ^7-26


By abandoning misdeeds, there is no suffering.
By being wise, there is no unhappiness.
For wrong thoughts
And misdeeds harm the mind and body. ^7-27


With merit, the body is at ease;
With wisdom, the mind is at ease.
Even if they remain in cyclic existence for the sake of others,
How can those with compassion be weary? ^7-28


This power of the awakening mind
Exhausts previous misdeeds,
And gathers an ocean of merit.
Therefore, it is praised as supreme by the Hearers. ^7-29


Therefore, mounting the horse of the awakening mind,
Which dispels all weariness,
And proceeding from joy to joy,
Who with understanding would become despondent? ^7-30


To accomplish the welfare of sentient beings, the strengths are:
Aspiration, steadfastness, joy, and letting go.
Aspiration is generated by reflecting on the fear of suffering
And its benefits. ^7-31


Thus, having abandoned opposing factors,
And with aspiration, pride, joy, and letting go,
With the strength of taking on the burden and self-mastery,
One should strive to increase diligence. ^7-32


I must overcome my own and others' faults,
Which are immeasurable.
For each single fault
Can exhaust an ocean of eons. ^7-33


If I have not even seen a fraction
Of the effort to eliminate faults,
I am a vessel of immeasurable suffering—
Why should my heart not break? ^7-34


I must accomplish many qualities
For myself and others.
If each single quality
Requires an ocean of eons to cultivate, ^7-35


And I have never even begun to cultivate
A fraction of these qualities,
It is astonishing how I have wasted
The precious life I have obtained. ^7-36


I have not honored the Buddhas,
Nor offered great festivals of joy.
I have not worked for the Dharma,
Nor fulfilled the hopes of the poor. ^7-37


I have not given fearlessness to the fearful,
Nor comfort to the distressed.
I have only caused pain in my mother's womb,
And brought forth nothing but suffering. ^7-38


In the past and even now,
Lacking aspiration for the Dharma,
I have encountered such destitution.
Who would abandon aspiration for the Dharma? ^7-39


The Sage has said that aspiration
Is the root of all virtuous qualities.
Its root is always
Reflecting on the results of actions. ^7-40


Suffering, mental unhappiness,
Various kinds of fear,
And separation from desired things—
These arise from engaging in misdeeds. ^7-41


By performing virtues conceived in the mind,
Wherever one goes,
There, by that merit,
One is honored with excellent results. ^7-42


Though those who commit misdeeds desire happiness,
Wherever they go,
There, by those misdeeds,
They are utterly destroyed by the weapons of suffering. ^7-43


Residing in the heart of a vast, fragrant, cool lotus,
Nourished by the sweet speech of the Buddhas,
With a supreme body born from a lotus expanded by the Sage's light,
One will become a child of the Buddhas, dwelling before them, through virtue. ^7-44


By Yama's henchmen, utterly tormented, with all skin flayed,
Molten copper, melted by intensely hot fire, poured onto the body,
Stabbed by blazing swords and short spears, torn into a hundred pieces of flesh,
Falling onto an intensely blazing iron ground—this happens due to many non-virtues. ^7-45


Therefore, aspire to virtue,
And cultivate reverence.
By the ritual of the Vajra Banner,
Begin and cultivate confidence. ^7-46


First, having assessed your capacity,
Decide whether to undertake a task or not.
Not undertaking it is better
Than starting and then turning back. ^7-47


In other lives, this habit will persist,
And misdeeds and suffering will increase.
Even at the time of results for others,
It will be inferior and will not be accomplished. ^7-48


Pride in action, pride in afflictions, and pride in capacity—
These three kinds of confidence should be cultivated.
"I alone will do this!"—
This is the confidence in action. ^7-49


This world, powerless due to afflictions,
Cannot accomplish its own welfare.
Beings cannot do as I can,
Therefore, I will do this! ^7-50


If others perform inferior actions,
How can I remain idle?
One should not act out of ordinary arrogance,
For the absence of such arrogance is supreme. ^7-51


If it encounters a dead snake,
Even a crow acts like a garuda.
If one is weak,
Even a small fault can cause harm. ^7-52


If one abandons effort with despondency,
How can one escape destitution?
By cultivating confident effort,
Even great ones are hard to defeat. ^7-53


Therefore, with a steadfast mind,
One must overcome all downfalls.
If I am defeated by downfalls,
My desire to conquer the three realms will be a laughingstock. ^7-54


I must conquer all,
And let no one conquer me.
I, a child of the Victorious Lion,
Will abide in this confidence. ^7-55


Those beings who are overcome by arrogance
Are not truly confident in overcoming afflictions.
The confident do not fall under the sway of enemies;
Those others are overcome by the enemy of arrogance. ^7-56


Filled with the arrogance of afflictions,
One is led by arrogance to lower realms,
Deprived of human joys,
And becomes a servant, eating others' scraps. ^7-57


Foolish, unattractive, weak,
And despised by all.
If even an ascetic filled with arrogance
Is counted among the arrogant,
What then to say of the inferior? ^7-58


Whoever holds confidence to conquer the enemy of arrogance,
That one is truly confident and a victorious hero.
Whoever surely overcomes the rising enemy of arrogance,
Fulfills the desired results of victory for beings. ^7-59


Dwelling amidst the forces of afflictions,
One should stand firm in a thousand ways.
Like a lion among foxes and such,
One should not be overcome by the host of afflictions. ^7-60


Even if great danger arises,
Just as one protects one's eyes,
So too, even if danger arises,
One must not fall under the sway of afflictions. ^7-61


Just as one desires the joyful results of play,
Whatever task one undertakes,
One should be devoted to that task,
And delight in it, never growing weary. ^7-62


Even if one performs actions for the sake of happiness,
It is uncertain whether happiness will arise or not.
But if the action itself becomes happiness,
How can one be happy by not doing that action? ^7-63


If one is not satisfied by desires
That are like honey on a razor's edge,
How can one be satisfied by the peaceful
Happiness of karmic results and merit? ^7-64


Therefore, to complete a task,
Like an elephant tormented by the midday sun
That, upon finding a lake, plunges into it,
One should likewise engage in that task. ^7-65


If one's strength is exhausted,
One should abandon the task for the sake of resuming it later.
If it is well-completed, then with a desire for
Further and further tasks, one should abandon it. ^7-66


Like a warrior whose sword has fallen
In battle with an old enemy,
One should avoid the weapons of afflictions,
And rise up to destroy the enemies of afflictions. ^7-67


If one's sword falls in battle,
One quickly picks it up out of fear.
Likewise, if the weapon of mindfulness is lost,
One should quickly regain it, remembering the fear of hell. ^7-68


Just as poison, having entered the bloodstream,
Spreads throughout the body,
Likewise, if an opportunity is found,
Faults will spread throughout the mind. ^7-69


Carrying a vessel filled with oil,
With a sword-wielder standing nearby,
Fearing death if one spills it,
A practitioner should likewise be vigilant. ^7-70


Therefore, if a snake comes into one's lap,
Just as one quickly stands up,
Likewise, if sleep and indolence arise,
One should quickly ward them off. ^7-71


For each fault that arises,
One should reproach oneself and resolve,
"This will never happen to me again."
And reflect on this for a long time. ^7-72


"How can mindfulness be cultivated
In these circumstances?"—
One should desire to encounter the cause for this,
Or the appropriate action. ^7-73


Before undertaking any task,
One should consider that everything has power.
Remembering such words of caution,
One should make oneself light and ready to act. ^7-74


Just as the wind, by coming and going,
Controls a cotton ball,
Likewise, one should be controlled by enthusiasm,
And then one will succeed. ^7-75


From Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Conduct, this is the seventh chapter, called "The Practice of Diligence." ^7-a

## 8. Chapter Eight: The Practice of Meditation ^8-0

Having developed such effort,
Place your mind in meditation.
A person whose mind is distracted
Lives in the grip of disturbing emotions. ^8-1

By being alone in body and mind,
Distraction will not arise.
Therefore, one should abandon worldly things
And completely cast away all thoughts. ^8-2

Due to attachment and craving
For gain and so on, one does not abandon worldly things.
Therefore, completely give up these things,
And the wise should examine them in this way. ^8-3

Knowing that disturbing emotions are completely overcome
By insight accompanied by calm abiding,
One should first seek calm abiding, and this
Is achieved by being free from worldly attachments. ^8-4

Who among the impermanent
Would truly become attached to the impermanent?
Such a person will not see what is pleasant
For thousands of lifetimes. ^8-5

If one does not see it, one will not be happy.
The mind will not be settled.
Even if one sees it, one will not be satisfied,
And will be tormented by craving as before. ^8-6

If one becomes attached to living beings,
It completely obscures the truth.
It also destroys the feeling of weariness (with cyclic existence),
And ultimately leads to sorrow and torment. ^8-7

By merely thinking of them,
This life will pass meaninglessly.
With impermanent friends,
Even the eternal Dharma will be destroyed. ^8-8

If one associates with foolish people,
One will certainly go to lower realms.
If they lead one to what is not equal (to one's own path),
What is the point of relying on foolish people? ^8-9

In one moment they become friends,
And in an instant they become enemies.
They get angry at things that bring joy,
So it is difficult to please ordinary people. ^8-10

If you speak helpfully, they get angry.
They also turn away from what is beneficial to themselves.
If you do not listen to their words,
They will go to lower realms out of anger. ^8-11

They envy the high, compete with equals,
Are arrogant when praised by the low,
And get angry when unpleasant things are said.
When will one ever gain anything from foolish people? ^8-12

If one associates with foolish people,
Praising oneself, criticizing others,
And talking about things that delight in cyclic existence,
Unwholesome actions will certainly arise. ^8-13

In this way, relying on oneself and others
Only leads to ruin.
One has not accomplished one's own purpose,
And one will not accomplish their purpose either. ^8-14

One should keep far away from foolish people.
If you meet them, please them with joy,
But do not become too intimate.
It is best to remain like a stranger. ^8-15

Like a bee taking nectar from a flower,
Take only the essence of the Dharma.
And like one who has never seen anyone before,
Remain without intimacy with anyone. ^8-16

"I have much gain and respect,
Many people are fond of me."
If one harbors such pride,
Fear will arise after death. ^8-17

Therefore, the deluded mind,
Whatever it becomes attached to,
That very attachment, multiplied a thousandfold,
Will become suffering and arise. ^8-18

Therefore, the wise should not become attached.
Fear arises from attachment.
These things are by nature impermanent,
So be firm and understand them well. ^8-19

Even if much gain arises,
And fame and renown come,
With all the gain and fame,
There is no knowing where one will go. ^8-20

If others criticize me,
What joy is there for me in praise?
If others praise me,
What unhappiness is there for me in criticism? ^8-21

Living beings have various inclinations;
Even the Buddhas cannot please them all.
What need is there to speak of a lowly person like me?
Therefore, abandon worldly concerns. ^8-22

Living beings criticize those without gain,
And speak ill of those with gain.
How can joy arise from such difficult-to-please people? ^8-23

Because foolish people do not find joy
Without their own benefit,
The Buddhas have said that no foolish person
Is a true friend. ^8-24

In the forest, the deer, birds,
And trees do not speak ill.
When will I dwell together with them,
Who bring happiness when associated with? ^8-25

In a cave, a deserted temple,
Or under a tree, having made my dwelling,
When will I no longer look back,
And become free from attachment? ^8-26

When will I dwell freely and without attachment
In vast, naturally open spaces,
Where I have not claimed any land as my own? ^8-27

When will I be fearless,
Even without hiding this body,
Possessing only a begging bowl and other necessities,
And wearing clothes that are not needed by anyone else? ^8-28

Having gone to a charnel ground,
When will I equalize my own body
With the bones of others,
Recognizing them all as subject to decay? ^8-29

This very body of mine,
Whose stench even foxes
Will not approach,
Will become like that. ^8-30

Even this body, which arose alone,
Whose flesh and bones, born together,
Will decay and separate,
What need is there to speak of other friends? ^8-31

One is born alone,
And one dies alone.
If one does not take on the suffering of others,
What is the use of friends who cause obstacles? ^8-32

Just as travelers on a path
Take up lodging,
So too, those on the path of existence
Take up a place of birth. ^8-33

While the world is tormented,
Until four men
Carry one away from it,
Go to the forest. ^8-34

Having no friends and no enemies,
Dwell alone in solitude with this body.
Consider yourself as already dead,
So that when you die, there will be no mourning. ^8-35

No one sitting nearby
Will cause grief or harm,
And no one will distract you
From remembering the Buddha and so on. ^8-36

Therefore, in a very delightful
Forest grove, which is easy and pleasant,
And where all distractions are calmed,
I will dwell alone. ^8-37

Abandoning all other thoughts,
With only the thought of myself,
I will strive to settle my mind
And to tame it. ^8-38

In this world and the next,
Desires create trouble.
Here, there is killing, binding, and cutting.
In the next, there are hells and so on. ^8-39

Because of the many times
That messengers and women have previously pleaded,
And for whose sake one did not hesitate
To commit evil deeds or dishonorable acts, ^8-40

And for whom I entered into danger,
And exhausted my resources,
Those very ones, when embraced,
Bring supreme joy. ^8-41

If it is nothing but a skeleton,
Not independent and not oneself,
Why do you crave it so much and cling to it,
And not go to nirvana? ^8-42

At first, one makes an effort to lift it,
And even when pulled, one looks down in shame.
Whether seen before or not,
The face is covered with clothes. ^8-43

That face of yours, full of disturbing emotions,
Is now as if it were directly present.
Seeing it uncovered by vultures,
Why do you now flee? ^8-44

Even when others look with their eyes,
You protect it completely.
As long as it is eaten by them,
Why do you, miserly one, not protect it? ^8-45

Seeing this heap of flesh,
Vultures and others eat it.
Why do you adorn the food of others
With garlands of flowers, sandalwood, and ornaments? ^8-46

Seeing it as merely a bone,
If you are afraid even when it does not move,
Why would you not be terrified if someone
Moved it like a zombie? ^8-47

From the very same food,
Feces and saliva arise.
Why do you dislike feces,
But like saliva? ^8-48

Instead of delighting in a soft pillow
Made of cotton,
The lustful, deluded by impurity,
Say, "It doesn't smell bad." ^8-49

If one is attached to it even when covered,
Why not desire it when uncovered?
If there is no need for it,
Why embrace what is covered? ^8-50

If you are not attached to impurity,
Why do you embrace another,
A cage of bones bound by tendons,
Plastered with a paste of flesh? ^8-51

You yourself have much impurity,
And you constantly dwell in it.
Yet you crave other bags of impurity,
And desire impurity. ^8-52

"I like this flesh," you say,
And want to touch and see it.
But how can you desire flesh
Which is by nature without consciousness? ^8-53

The mind that desires
Cannot touch or see.
That which can, is not conscious.
What is the use of a meaningless embrace? ^8-54

It is not surprising that one does not understand
The impure nature of another's body.
But it is truly amazing that one does not understand
That one's own body is impure. ^8-55

Abandoning a young lotus,
Opened by the sun's rays, free from clouds,
Why does the mind, clinging to impurity,
Delight in a cage of impurity? ^8-56

If you do not want to touch
A place soiled with impurity,
How can you want to touch the body
From which that impurity came? ^8-57

If you are not attached to impurity,
Why do you embrace another,
Who came from an impure field,
And whose seed was produced by it? ^8-58

You do not desire even a tiny
Impure worm that comes from impurity,
Yet you desire a body that is by nature
Full of much impurity, and a person born of impurity. ^8-59

Not only do you not criticize
Your own impurity,
But you also crave other bags of impurity,
And desire impurity. ^8-60

Camphor and other pleasant things,
Or even rice and vegetables,
When put in the mouth and then spit out,
Even the earth becomes soiled and impure. ^8-61

If you still have doubts,
Even when such impurity is evident,
Then go to a charnel ground
And look at other impure bodies. ^8-62

Knowing that great fear arises
When the skin is peeled back from the body,
How can joy arise again
For that very thing? ^8-63

The fragrance applied to the body
Is sandalwood and not anything else.
Why do you become attached to others
Because of the fragrance of others? ^8-64

If you were not attached to it because of its natural bad smell,
Wouldn't that be good?
Why do those who needlessly burn the world
Apply sweet fragrances to it? ^8-65

What has sandalwood, a sweet fragrance,
Done to this body here?
Why do you become attached to others
Because of the fragrance of others? ^8-66

If the body's nature is terrifying when naked,
With long hair and nails,
Yellow teeth, and the smell of mud, ^8-67

Like a weapon that harms oneself,
Why do you strive to clean it?
By the deluded efforts of oneself,
This earth is completely disturbed by madness. ^8-68

If you are disgusted in a charnel ground
Just by seeing bones,
Are you happy in the charnel ground of a city,
Which is filled with moving bones? ^8-69

Such an impure thing
Is not obtained without cost.
Striving for it brings the suffering of
Being cut up, hell, and so on. ^8-70

Foolish people cannot increase wealth,
So what happiness is there in this when one is mature?
If one's life is spent on wealth and so on,
What is the use of desire when one is old? ^8-71

Some wretched people, driven by desire,
Are completely exhausted by work all day long.
They return home, their bodies worn out,
And lie down like corpses. ^8-72

Some suffer from the hardships of travel,
And the pain of being far away.
Though they desire children and spouses,
They will not even see them in their dreams. ^8-73

Deluded by the desire for self-benefit,
Even those for whom one sells oneself,
Are driven by the wind of others' meaningless work,
Without achieving their purpose. ^8-74

Some sell their own bodies,
And are used by others against their will.
Even their wives, when they have children,
Give birth under trees in deserted places. ^8-75

Foolish people, deceived by desire,
Say, "We want to live, we will live!"
They enter battle, fearing for their lives,
And become servants for profit. ^8-76

Some lustful people have their bodies cut,
Some are impaled on stakes,
Some are pierced with spears,
And some are even burned. ^8-77

Know that wealth brings endless trouble
Through the suffering of accumulating, protecting, and losing it.
Those who are distracted by attachment to wealth
Have no opportunity to be freed from the suffering of cyclic existence. ^8-78

For those with desires, there are many such
Faults, and little benefit.
It is like cattle pulling a cart,
Eating a few mouthfuls of grass. ^8-79

For the sake of that small benefit,
Which is not difficult to obtain even by cattle,
These precious human lives, so hard to find,
Are destroyed by those tormented by karma. ^8-80

Desires will certainly be destroyed,
And lead to hell and other lower realms.
Because they are not great, what is the use
Of the constant toil and effort they bring? ^8-81

If even a fraction of that effort
Could achieve Buddhahood,
Then those with desires suffer more than
Bodhisattvas, and do not achieve enlightenment. ^8-82

Considering the suffering of hell and so on,
Desires cannot even be compared to
Weapons, poison, fire, precipices,
Or enemies. ^8-83

Having thus become weary of desires,
Develop joy in solitude.
In peaceful forest groves,
Free from quarrels and disturbing emotions, ^8-84

In delightful, spacious stone mansions,
Cooled by the auspicious moonlight and sandalwood,
With the silent, peaceful forest breeze blowing,
One should wander, thinking of the benefit of others. ^8-85

In empty houses, under trees, or in caves,
One dwells as long as one wishes,
Free from the suffering of clinging and protecting,
And acts freely without attachment. ^8-86

To act freely and without attachment,
Without being connected to anyone,
The contentment and happiness that comes from this
Is difficult for even the powerful to find. ^8-87

In this way, having considered the qualities of solitude,
One should meditate on the calming of thoughts
And on the awakening mind. ^8-88

First, one should strive to meditate
On the equality of oneself and others.
Since happiness and suffering are equal,
All beings should be protected like oneself. ^8-89

Just as the body, though having many parts
Like hands and so on, is one in being protected,
So too, the various happiness and suffering of beings
Are all one in desiring happiness, like oneself. ^8-90

If my suffering
Does not harm another's body,
Even so, that suffering of mine
Is unbearable due to clinging to self. ^8-91

Similarly, the suffering of others
Will not fall upon me.
Even so, that suffering of theirs
Is difficult to bear due to clinging to self. ^8-92

I should remove the suffering of others,
Because it is suffering, like my own.
I should benefit others,
Because they are living beings, like my own body. ^8-93

When both myself and others
Are equal in desiring happiness,
What difference is there between me and them,
That I should strive only for my own happiness? ^8-94

When both myself and others
Are equal in not desiring suffering,
What difference is there between me and them,
That I should protect myself and not others? ^8-95

If you say that because their suffering
Does not harm me, I should not protect them,
Then why should you protect future suffering,
If it does not harm you now? ^8-96

The thought, "I will experience that,"
Is a mistaken notion.
For when one dies, it is another,
And when one is born, it is also another. ^8-97

If the suffering of a particular person
Is to be protected by that very person,
Then why should one protect the suffering of the foot
With the hand? ^8-98

If you say that even if it is not logical,
One acts here due to clinging to self,
Then what is illogical about self and other?
One should abandon it as much as possible. ^8-99

The terms "continuum" and "collection"
Are false, like a garland or an army.
There is no one who suffers,
So who will be empowered by this? ^8-100

Without a self that suffers,
All are without distinction.
Since it is suffering, it should be removed.
What is the use of certainty in that? ^8-101

Why should there be a dispute
About removing the suffering of all?
If it is to be removed, then remove all of it.
Otherwise, I am just like other beings. ^8-102

If you ask why compassion, which brings much suffering,
Should be diligently cultivated,
Consider the suffering of beings,
And how much suffering compassion brings. ^8-103

If one suffering
Can eliminate many sufferings,
Then a compassionate person should generate
That suffering for oneself and others. ^8-104

Therefore, just as Moon-Beautiful,
Even knowing the king's harm,
Did not remove her own suffering,
Because it would end the suffering of many. ^8-105

In this way, when the mind is accustomed,
Delighting in the calming of others' suffering,
Like a swan in a lotus pond,
One will enter even the lowest hell. ^8-106

When living beings are liberated,
Is the ocean of joy that arises
Not enough?
What is the use of desiring liberation for oneself? ^8-107

Therefore, even when acting for the benefit of others,
There is no pride or wonder.
Delighting solely in the benefit of others,
There is no hope for karmic results. ^8-108

Therefore, just as one protects oneself
From even the slightest unpleasantness,
So too, one should develop the mind of protection
And compassion for others. ^8-109

Through habit, just as one perceives
The semen and blood drops of others,
Which are not real, as "mine," ^8-110

So too, why not perceive
Another's body as "mine"?
It is not difficult to place
One's own body in others. ^8-111

Knowing oneself to be flawed, and others
To be an ocean of good qualities,
One should meditate on completely abandoning self-clinging
And taking on others. ^8-112

Just as one considers hands and so on
To be limbs of the body,
So too, why not consider living beings
To be limbs of the world? ^8-113

Just as the thought of "mine" arose
For this body, which has no self, through habit,
So too, why can't the thought of "mine" arise
For other living beings through habit? ^8-114

If that is the case, then even when acting for the benefit of others,
No wonder or pride will arise.
Just as one does not expect a reward
After eating one's own food. ^8-115

Therefore, just as one protects oneself
From even the slightest unpleasantness,
So too, one should cultivate the mind of protection
And compassion for beings. ^8-116

Therefore, the protector Avalokiteshvara,
With great compassion, to remove the fear
Of cyclic existence for beings,
Even blessed his own name. ^8-117

One should not turn back from hardship,
For by the power of habit,
Even the fear of hearing a name
Will turn into joy if that person is not there. ^8-118

Whoever wishes to quickly protect
Themselves and others,
Should practice the supreme secret
Of exchanging oneself and others. ^8-119

This body, to which I am attached,
Causes fear in small dangers.
Who would not hate this body,
Which causes fear, like an enemy? ^8-120

This body, which desires to perform rituals
To cure hunger, thirst, and other illnesses,
Causes the killing of birds, fish, and deer,
And lies in wait on the path. ^8-121

If, for the sake of profit and respect,
One kills one's father and mother,
And steals the property of the Three Jewels,
And is then burned in the lowest hell, ^8-122

What wise person would desire, protect,
Or worship such a body?
Who would not look at it like an enemy,
And despise it? ^8-123

"If I give, what will I use?"
This thought of self-interest is demonic.
"If I use, what will I give?"
This thought of others' interest is divine. ^8-124

If one harms others for one's own sake,
One will be tormented in hell and so on.
If one harms oneself for the sake of others,
One will attain all excellent qualities. ^8-125

Whoever desires to be high,
Will go to lower realms, become wretched and foolish.
If one transfers that desire to others,
One will attain happy realms and respect. ^8-126

If one uses others for one's own sake,
One will experience being a servant and so on.
If one uses oneself for the sake of others,
One will experience being a lord and so on. ^8-127

All the happiness in the world
Comes from desiring the happiness of others.
All the suffering in the world
Comes from desiring one's own happiness. ^8-128

What need is there for much explanation?
Look at the difference between
A foolish person acting for their own sake,
And a Buddha acting for the sake of others. ^8-129

If one does not truly exchange
One's own happiness for the suffering of others,
One will not attain Buddhahood,
Nor will there be happiness in cyclic existence. ^8-130

Let alone the next world,
Even in this life, the purpose of
A servant not working,
And a lord not giving rewards, will not be achieved. ^8-131

Abandoning all excellent happiness,
Both seen and unseen,
And through the cause of harming others,
The deluded take on unbearable suffering. ^8-132

All the harm in the world,
And all the fear and suffering that exist,
All arise from clinging to self.
What will that great demon do to me? ^8-133

If one does not completely abandon oneself,
One cannot abandon suffering.
Just as if one does not abandon fire,
One cannot avoid being burned. ^8-134

Therefore, to calm one's own harm,
And to calm the suffering of others,
One should give oneself to others,
And take others as oneself. ^8-135

"I am under the control of others,"
Mind, you must truly know this.
Now, except for the benefit of all beings,
You should not think of anything else. ^8-136

It is not appropriate for the eyes and so on,
Which are under the control of others, to act for their own sake.
It is not appropriate for the eyes and so on
To act wrongly towards that person for their own sake. ^8-137

Therefore, one should prioritize living beings.
Whatever one sees in one's own body,
One should take that and
Use it to benefit others. ^8-138

Having made oneself into the low and so on,
And others into oneself,
With a mind free from thoughts,
One should meditate on envy, competition, and pride. ^8-139

"This one is honored, I am not.
I have not obtained what this one has.
This one is praised, I am criticized.
This one is happy, I am suffering." ^8-140

"I am doing the work,
And this one is dwelling in happiness.
This one is great in the world,
And I am known as lowly and without qualities." ^8-141

"What is the use of being without qualities?
I am full of all qualities.
There are those who are lower than this one,
And there are those who are higher than me." ^8-142

Morality, views, and other things
Are not under my control due to the power of disturbing emotions.
I must heal myself as much as possible,
And I must accept harm. ^8-143

If I am not to be healed by this,
Why do I despise myself?
What good are this one's qualities to me?
I am the one with qualities. ^8-144

This one, dwelling in the mouth of lower realms,
Without compassion for beings,
Desires to overcome the wise
By external pride in qualities. ^8-145

Looking at those who are equal to oneself,
To make oneself superior,
One must certainly achieve one's own gain and respect
Even through conflict. ^8-146

"May my qualities
Be clear to all the world,
And may no one
Hear of this one's qualities." ^8-147

"My faults should be hidden,
And I should be worshipped, not this one.
I should obtain good gain now,
And I should be respected, not this one." ^8-148

"I will long delight in seeing
This one do wrong.
I will make all beings laugh at them,
And criticize each other." ^8-149

"This disturbing emotion also
Is said to compete with me.
Are these equal in hearing, wisdom,
Form, lineage, or wealth?" ^8-150

Having heard of my qualities,
Proclaimed to all,
One should fully enjoy the happiness
Of having goosebumps and joy. ^8-151

If this one has gain,
But works for me,
Give this one only enough to live,
And take it by my own power. ^8-152

"This one should be deprived of happiness,
And constantly afflicted with my harm.
This one has harmed me
For hundreds of lifetimes in cyclic existence." ^8-153

Mind, desiring your own benefit,
Even though countless eons have passed,
You have only created such great
Suffering through your efforts. ^8-154

Therefore, truly
Engage in the benefit of others.
The Buddha's words are unfailing,
And you will see its qualities later. ^8-155

If you had done this work
In earlier times,
You would not be in such a state
Of not being perfectly happy as a Buddha. ^8-156

Therefore, just as you have clung to "I"
In the semen and blood drops of others,
So too, habituate yourself to others. ^8-157

Having greatly considered others,
Whatever appears in your own body,
Take that very thing
And use it to benefit others. ^8-158

"I am happy, others are not.
I am high, others are low.
I am beneficial, others are not."
Why do you not envy yourself? ^8-159

"May I be free from happiness,
And may the suffering of others be joined to me."
When this happens, examine your own faults,
Saying, "What is this doing?" ^8-160

Even the faults committed by others
Should be transformed into your own faults.
Even if you commit a small fault,
Let it be known to many people. ^8-161

By speaking highly of others' fame,
Eclipse your own fame.
Use yourself like the lowest servant
For all purposes. ^8-162

"This one is by nature flawed,
And should not be praised for temporary qualities.
Ensure that no one
Ever knows of this one's qualities." ^8-163

In short, for the sake of my own benefit,
Whatever harm you have done to others,
Let that harm fall upon yourself
For the sake of living beings. ^8-164

This one should not be made
To become stubborn and strong.
Like a new bride,
Keep it restrained with shame and fear. ^8-165

"Do this, dwell like that,
And you should not do that.
This one should be controlled like that,
And if it transgresses, it should be punished." ^8-166

However, even if advised in this way,
If you, mind, do not do so,
Then by relying on all your faults,
You yourself must be punished. ^8-167

"The former time when you destroyed me
Was different.
I see you now, where will you go?
All your arrogance will be crushed." ^8-168

"Now, abandon the thought
That I still have my own purpose.
I have sold you to others.
Do not be sad, offer your milk and flesh." ^8-169

If, being careless, you
Do not give yourself to living beings,
Then you will certainly give me
To the guardians of hell. ^8-170

In this way, you have given me away,
And I have suffered for a long time.
Remembering my grievances,
I will crush your self-centered thoughts. ^8-171

If I desire happiness,
I should not make myself happy.
If I desire to be protected,
I should always protect others. ^8-172
The more this body is
Cared for and looked after,
The more it becomes
Demanding and difficult. ^8-173

Even if all the desires
Of such a demanding body
Could not be fulfilled by this entire world,
Who could possibly fulfill its desires? ^8-174

Unfulfilled desires lead to suffering
And disappointment.
But for someone who is free from all attachments,
Their happiness is endless. ^8-175

Therefore, do not give in to the body's desires
To let them grow.
What is not held dear
Is truly a good thing. ^8-176

This body will eventually turn to ashes,
It is still and moved by others,
It is an unpleasant form of impurity—
Why do I cling to it as "me"? ^8-177

Whether it lives or dies,
What good is this machine to me?
What difference is there between it and a log?
Alas, why does it not remove my pride? ^8-178

By taking care of this body,
I have accumulated pointless suffering.
What good is this body, which is like a tree,
To attachment and anger? ^8-179

Whether I care for it
Or vultures eat it,
If there is no attachment or hatred,
Why should I be attached to it? ^8-180

If someone gets angry when insulted,
And pleased when praised,
If that person has no understanding,
For whom have I made such an effort? ^8-181

If someone says, "I am a friend
To whoever desires this body,"
Since everyone desires their own body,
Why should I not be happy with that? ^8-182

Therefore, I will give up this body without attachment
To benefit all beings.
Even though it has many faults,
I will hold onto it as a tool for action. ^8-183

Therefore, enough of childish behavior!
I will follow the wise.
Remembering the words of carefulness,
I will overcome sleep and dullness. ^8-184

Therefore, to remove hindrances,
I will turn my mind away from wrong paths.
And I will always
Keep my mind steadily focused on the true object. ^8-185

This concludes the eighth chapter, called "Showing Meditation," from *Engaging in the Deeds of a Bodhisattva*. ^8-a

## 9. Chapter Nine: The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0


All these practices,
The Buddha taught for the sake of wisdom.
Therefore, those who wish for suffering
To cease should cultivate wisdom. ^9-1


Conventional and ultimate,
These are considered the two truths.
The ultimate is not an object for the mind;
The mind is said to be conventional. ^9-2


In this regard, two kinds of people are seen:
Spiritual practitioners and ordinary people.
Among these, ordinary people
Are refuted by the world of practitioners. ^9-3


Practitioners, too, due to differences in understanding,
Are refuted by those of higher and higher understanding.
Both (ordinary people and practitioners)
Do not analyze for the sake of the ultimate result. ^9-4


Ordinary people see things as real,
And conceive them as truly existing.
Since they are not like illusions, here
Practitioners and ordinary people dispute. ^9-5


Even direct perception of forms and so on
Is by common agreement, not by valid cognition.
Just as the common agreement of purity and so on
Regarding impure things is false. ^9-6


For the purpose of guiding ordinary beings,
The Buddha taught things as truly existing.
If they are not momentary,
Then even conventionally, there would be a contradiction. ^9-7


The practitioner's conventional truth is faultless,
Seeing things as they are, relative to ordinary people.
Otherwise, if one conceives women as impure,
That conception would be refuted by ordinary people. ^9-8


Merit from an illusory Buddha
Is just like that from a truly existing one.
If sentient beings are like illusions,
How can they be reborn after death? ^9-9


As long as the conditions are assembled,
So long will the illusion appear.
How can sentient beings be truly existent
Merely because they persist for a long time? ^9-10


If one kills an illusory person and so on,
There is no sin, because there is no mind.
But if an illusory being has a mind,
Then merit and sin arise. ^9-11


Since mantras and so on have no power
To create an illusory mind, such a mind does not arise.
The illusion that arises from various conditions
Is also of various kinds. ^9-12


A single condition cannot accomplish everything;
It is nowhere to be found.
If ultimate reality is nirvana,
And cyclic existence is conventional, then... ^9-13


Even a Buddha would be in cyclic existence,
So what is the use of Bodhisattva practices?
If the stream of conditions is not cut off,
Then even an illusion will not cease. ^9-14


But when the stream of conditions is cut off,
Then even conventionally, it will not arise.
When there is no delusion,
By whom will the illusion be perceived? ^9-15


When for you there is no illusion,
What is perceived at that time?
If something else truly exists,
That appearance is mind itself. ^9-16


When mind itself is an illusion,
Then what sees what, and by whom?
The Protector of the world (the Buddha) also
Taught that mind does not see mind. ^9-17


Just as the blade of a sword
Cannot cut itself, so too the mind.
If a lamp truly illuminates
Its own essence, then... ^9-18


A lamp does not need to illuminate itself,
For it is not obscured by darkness.
Just as a crystal's blueness (is blue by nature),
It does not depend on anything else. ^9-19


Similarly, some things are seen
To be dependent and some independent.
That which is not blue by nature
Does not make itself blue. ^9-20


If one says, "A lamp illuminates,"
Knowing it to be so,
By what knowledge does one say,
"The mind is inherently clear"? ^9-21


When nothing is seen by anyone,
Whether clear or unclear,
Like the graceful gait of a barren woman's daughter,
To speak of it is meaningless. ^9-22


If self-awareness does not exist,
How can consciousness be remembered?
It is remembered through connection with another's experience,
Like the memory of a mouse's poison (from its effect on others). ^9-23


If, because it is seen to be endowed with other conditions,
It is said to be self-illuminating,
Then just as seeing a pot by applying magical eye-salve
Does not make the eye-salve itself the pot. ^9-24


How seeing, hearing, and knowing
Are not to be refuted here.
Here, what is to be refuted
Is the conception of true existence, which is the cause of suffering. ^9-25


If illusion is not other than mind,
And if it is impermanent without being other,
Then how can it not be other if it truly exists?
If it is not other, then it does not truly exist. ^9-26


Just as an illusion, though not truly existent,
Is an object to be seen, so too is the perceiving mind.
If cyclic existence has a truly existent basis,
Otherwise it would be like space. ^9-27


How can non-existent things, relying on existent things,
Have activity?
Your mind, without a companion,
Would become solitary. ^9-28


When mind is free from grasping,
Then all are Buddhas.
Even so, what virtue is there
In merely conceiving of "mind only"? ^9-29


Even if one knows things to be like an illusion,
How will the afflictions cease?
For even the creator of an illusory woman
Can develop attachment to her. ^9-30


The creator has not abandoned
The habitual patterns of afflictions regarding objects of knowledge.
Therefore, when he sees it,
His habitual pattern of emptiness is weak. ^9-31


By habituating to the habitual pattern of emptiness,
One abandons the habitual pattern of (truly existing) things.
By habituating to "nothing exists,"
That too will later be abandoned. ^9-32


When the object to be investigated,
Which is "nothing exists," is not perceived,
At that time, how can the non-existent, without a basis,
Remain before the mind? ^9-33


When existence and non-existence
No longer remain before the mind,
At that time, with no other aspect remaining,
It utterly ceases, without an object of perception. ^9-34


Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-granting tree
Completely fulfills all hopes,
Similarly, due to the aspirations
Of those to be tamed, the Buddha appears in various forms. ^9-35


For example, a shrine to Garuda,
Even after it has been built and the builder has passed away,
And a long time has passed since their passing,
Still pacifies poisons and so on. ^9-36


Similarly, a shrine to the Buddha,
Built in accordance with Bodhisattva practices,
Even after the Bodhisattva has passed into nirvana,
Still accomplishes all purposes. ^9-37


How can making offerings to something without a mind
Yield results?
Because it is taught that whether present or passed into nirvana,
They are equally effective. ^9-38


Whether conventionally or ultimately,
It is appropriate, for the result is from scripture.
Just as with a truly existing Buddha,
So too are the results. ^9-39


One is liberated by seeing the truth,
So what is the use of seeing emptiness?
Because in the scriptures, it is taught
That without this path, there is no enlightenment. ^9-40


If the Mahayana is not established,
How are your scriptures established?
Because this is established for both traditions,
But for you, it is not established in the first place. ^9-41


Whatever reasons make you believe in your scriptures,
Those are equally applicable to the Mahayana.
If something is true because two others assert it,
Then the Vedas and so on would also be true. ^9-42


If you say the Mahayana is disputed,
Then your scriptures are disputed by non-Buddhists,
And other scriptures are disputed by your own and others.
Since they are disputed, they would have to be abandoned. ^9-43


If the root of the teaching is the state of being a monk,
Then the state of being a monk is also difficult to maintain.
For those whose minds are still grasping at objects,
Nirvana is also difficult to attain. ^9-44


If one is liberated by abandoning afflictions,
Then liberation would occur immediately.
Even without afflictions, for them,
The power of karma is still seen. ^9-45


If you assert that grasping craving
Definitely does not exist,
Then even if that craving is not an affliction,
Why should it not exist, just like delusion? ^9-46


Craving arises from conditions of feeling.
And feelings exist even for them (those without afflictions).
A mind that is still grasping at objects
Will remain for some. ^9-47


A mind that is devoid of emptiness
Will, after cessation, arise again,
Just like the meditative absorption without perception.
Therefore, one should meditate on emptiness. ^9-48


If you assert that any statement
Found in a sutra is spoken by the Buddha,
Then why don't you accept that most Mahayana sutras
Are equal to your sutras? ^9-49


If by one exception,
Everything becomes flawed,
Then why shouldn't everything be considered
Spoken by the Buddha, by one similar sutra? ^9-50


Whatever words even great Kashyapa
And others could not fathom,
Who would make them unacceptable
Just because you do not understand them? ^9-51


For the sake of those suffering from delusion,
Remaining in cyclic existence, liberated from the extremes of attachment and fear,
This is the result of emptiness. ^9-52


Thus, refuting the view of emptiness
Is not appropriate.
Therefore, without doubt,
One should meditate on emptiness. ^9-53


Emptiness is the antidote to the darkness
Of the veils of afflictions and objects of knowledge.
By those who quickly wish to know everything,
How can it not be meditated upon? ^9-54


If fear arises from that which
Causes suffering,
Then how can fear arise
From emptiness, which pacifies suffering? ^9-55


If there were any self,
It would fear anything.
But since no self truly exists,
Who is there to fear? ^9-56


Teeth, hair, and nails are not the self.
Bones and blood are not the self.
Mucus is not, phlegm is not,
Nor is lymph or pus. ^9-57


Fat and sweat are not the self.
Lungs and liver are not the self.
Other internal organs are also not the self.
Feces and urine are not the self. ^9-58


Flesh and skin are not the self.
Heat and wind (energy) are not the self.
The pores are not the self. In all aspects,
The six consciousnesses are also not the self. ^9-59


If the consciousness of sound were permanent,
Then it would perceive sound at all times.
If there is no object to be known, what knows?
By what is it called "knowledge"? ^9-60


If knowledge is without an object to be known,
Then wood would also become knowledge.
Therefore, it is certain that without
An object of knowledge present, there is no knowledge. ^9-61


If by that very (consciousness) one knows form,
Why would it not also hear sound at that time?
If it is because sound is not present,
Then its knowledge of sound would also not exist. ^9-62


How can that which is by nature a perceiver of sound
Become a perceiver of form?
One (thing) is conceived as father and son,
But this is not truly so. ^9-63


Just as the qualities of clarity, activity, and inertia
Are neither son nor father,
That (consciousness) is not seen
To be by nature a perceiver of sound. ^9-64


If it is seen in another aspect, like an actor,
Then it is not permanent.
If that other aspect is its true nature,
Then that single entity would be a new, previously non-existent one. ^9-65


If that other aspect is not truly existent,
Then state its own true nature.
If you say it is "mere knowledge," then
All beings would become one. ^9-66


Even sentient and insentient things
Would become one, because their existence is similar.
When distinctions are contradictory,
What is the basis for similarity at that time? ^9-67


Insentient things are also not the self,
Because they are insentient, like pots and so on.
But if you say it is because it is endowed with mind,
Then the non-knowing (insentient) would be destroyed. ^9-68


If the self is unchanging,
What has the mind done to it?
Thus, the non-knowing, actionless (self)
Would be made into space as the self. ^9-69


If the self does not exist,
Then the connection of karma and its results is illogical.
Since an action, once performed, ceases,
Whose karma would it be? ^9-70


The action and the basis of the result are distinct,
And there is no self as the agent.
Since this is established for both (sides),
Is there not a meaningless dispute here? ^9-71


That a cause possesses its result
Is not seen to be possible.
Based on a single continuum,
The agent and experiencer are taught. ^9-72


Past and future minds
Are not the self, because they do not exist.
But if the present mind is the self,
Then when it ceases, the self also ceases. ^9-73


Just as a plantain tree trunk,
When its parts are separated, nothing is found,
Similarly, when the self is sought
Through analysis, it is not truly existent. ^9-74


If sentient beings do not exist,
For whom should one feel compassion?
It is for those conceived by delusion,
Who are accepted for the sake of the result. ^9-75


If sentient beings do not exist, whose result is it?
True, but it is desired due to delusion.
For the purpose of pacifying suffering,
The delusion of results should not be abandoned. ^9-76


The pride that is the cause of suffering
Increases through the delusion of a self.
If you say there is no turning away from that,
Then meditating on selflessness is supreme. ^9-77


The body is not the feet or the calves.
The thighs and waist are also not the body.
The belly and back are not the body.
The chest and shoulders are also not the body. ^9-78


The ribs and arms are not the body.
The armpits and neck are also not the body.
The internal organs are also not it.
If the head and throat are also not the body,
Then what is the body here? ^9-79


If this body exists in all
Its parts individually,
Then the parts would reside in the parts.
But where does the body itself reside? ^9-80


If the body, as a whole,
Resides in the hands and so on,
Then there would be as many bodies
As there are hands and so on. ^9-81


If the body does not exist outside or inside,
How can the hands and so on have a body?
If there is nothing other than the hands and so on,
How can it exist? ^9-82


Therefore, on the bodyless hands and so on,
Delusion conceives a body.
Just as, due to a particular arrangement of shape,
A post is mistaken for a person. ^9-83


As long as the collection of conditions exists,
The body appears like a person.
Similarly, as long as the hands and so on exist,
So long does the body appear there. ^9-84


Similarly, since a hand is a collection of fingers,
What then is a hand?
And since a finger is a collection of joints,
And a joint is divided into its own parts... ^9-85


A part is divided into particles,
And a particle is divided by its directional parts.
Since directional parts are without parts,
Like space, therefore, particles also do not exist. ^9-86


Thus, to forms that are like a dream,
Who with discernment would be attached?
When the body is thus non-existent,
Then what is male and what is female? ^9-87


If suffering truly exists,
Why does it not harm those who are joyful?
If pleasure truly exists, why does it not delight
Those who are grieving, distressed, and so on, with delicious tastes and so on? ^9-88


If it is not experienced because it is overwhelmed by a stronger (feeling),
Then that which is not experienced,
And is not itself an experience,
How can that be a feeling? ^9-89


Suffering exists in a subtle form.
Is it not that its gross aspect has been removed?
If you say that is merely a different joy,
Then that subtle (suffering) is also a different (joy). ^9-90


If, due to the arising of an opposing condition,
Suffering does not arise,
Then is it not established that the conception of feeling
Is merely a strong attachment? ^9-91


For that very reason, this antidote
Of analytical wisdom should be cultivated.
The meditative absorption that arises from the realm of analysis
Is the food of practitioners. ^9-92


If sense faculties and objects have space between them,
Where would they meet?
If there is no space, they are one,
So what would meet what? ^9-93


A minute particle does not enter another minute particle,
For they are without gaps and are equal.
What does not enter cannot mix,
And what does not mix cannot meet. ^9-94


How can meeting be appropriate
For something without parts?
If you see meeting without parts,
Then demonstrate it. ^9-95


Meeting is not appropriate
For consciousness, which is bodiless.
And a collection (of parts) is also not truly existent,
As analyzed before. ^9-96


If contact thus does not exist,
From what will feeling arise?
What is the purpose of this effort?
What will harm what? ^9-97


When there is no experiencer,
And feeling also does not exist,
Having seen this state,
Why would craving not cease? ^9-98


Seeing or touching,
By their nature like a dream or illusion,
Arise simultaneously with the mind.
Therefore, feeling is not seen by that (mind). ^9-99


Even by what arises before or after,
It is remembered, but not experienced.
It does not experience itself,
Nor is it experienced by others. ^9-100


No experiencer exists,
Therefore, feeling is not truly existent.
Thus, to this collection (of aggregates) without a self,
What can this (suffering) do to harm it? ^9-101


The mind does not reside in the sense faculties,
Nor in forms and so on, nor in between.
The mind is not internal, nor external,
Nor is it found elsewhere. ^9-102


That which is not the body, not elsewhere,
Not mixed, and not separate in any way,
That is not anything at all. Therefore,
The nature of sentient beings is nirvana. ^9-103


If knowledge exists before the object of knowledge,
Then based on what does it arise?
If knowledge and the object of knowledge are simultaneous,
Then based on what does it arise? ^9-104


But if knowledge exists after the object of knowledge,
Then from what does knowledge arise at that time?
Thus, the arising of all phenomena
Cannot be understood. ^9-105


If conventional reality does not exist in that way,
Then how can there be two truths?
If it is by another conventional reality,
How can sentient beings pass into nirvana? ^9-106


This is a conceptualization of another's mind,
It is not one's own conventional reality.
If it is later ascertained, then it exists.
If not, then conventional reality does not exist. ^9-107


Conception and the object of conception
Are mutually dependent.
Just as, relying on common understanding,
All analysis is spoken of. ^9-108


When analysis
Is analyzed by analytical wisdom,
At that time, that analytical wisdom
Has no end, because it is analysis. ^9-109


When the object of analysis has been analyzed,
Analytical wisdom has no basis.
Since it has no basis, it does not arise,
And that is also called nirvana. ^9-110


For whom these two (truths) are truly existent,
That is very difficult to maintain.
If things are established by the power of knowledge,
What is the basis for the existence of knowledge? ^9-111


But if knowledge is established by the object of knowledge,
What is the basis for the existence of the object of knowledge?
If they exist by mutual dependence,
Then both would be non-existent. ^9-112


If there is no son, there is no father.
From what would sonship arise?
Without a son, there is no father.
Similarly, both (knowledge and object of knowledge) are non-existent. ^9-113


Just as a sprout arises from a seed,
And the seed is known by that (sprout),
Why is it not known that knowledge,
Which arises from the object of knowledge, exists? ^9-114


If the existence of a seed is known
By knowledge other than the sprout,
Then from what is the existence of knowledge
That knows the object of knowledge known? ^9-115


For now, by ordinary direct perception,
All causes are seen.
The distinctions of lotus stems and so on
Are produced by the distinctions of their causes. ^9-116


If you ask, "By what are the distinctions of causes made?"
It is from the distinctions of previous causes.
Why can a cause produce a result?
From the power of previous causes. ^9-117


If Ishvara is the cause of the world,
Then first, tell me who Ishvara is.
If you say it is the elements, then so be it,
But why bother with just a name? ^9-118


However, earth and so on are many,
Impermanent, not unmoving, not divine,
And are trampled upon and impure.
Therefore, they are not Ishvara. ^9-119


Ishvara is not space, because space is unmoving.
It is not the self, because that has been refuted before.
And what is the use of speaking of an inconceivable creator
By saying "inconceivable"? ^9-120


What is that which is desired to be created by it?
Is the self, that (Ishvara), earth and so on,
And the essence of Ishvara not permanent?
Knowledge arises from the object of knowledge, and... ^9-121


Beginningless pleasure and suffering are from karma.
Tell me what is created by that (Ishvara).
If the cause has no beginning,
How can the result have a beginning? ^9-122


Why does it not always create?
For it does not depend on others.
If there is nothing else not created by it,
On what would it depend? ^9-123


If it depends (on conditions), then a collection (of conditions)
Would be the cause, not Ishvara.
If it is a collection, it has no power to create,
And without it, there is no power to create. ^9-124


If Ishvara creates without wishing to,
Then it would be under the power of another.
If it wishes, it would depend on its wish.
If it creates, how can it be Ishvara? ^9-125


Those who assert that minute particles are permanent
Have also been refuted before.
The Samkhyas assert that the permanent Primal Substance
Is the cause of the world. ^9-126


The state of equilibrium of the qualities
Called sattva, rajas, and tamas (clarity, activity, and inertia)
Is called the Primal Substance.
The state of disequilibrium is called the world. ^9-127


For one (entity) to have three natures
Is illogical, therefore it does not exist.
Similarly, the qualities do not exist,
Because they are also each threefold. ^9-128


If the qualities do not exist, then sound and so on
Would be very far from existing.
And it is not possible for pleasure and so on
To exist in insentient things like clothes. ^9-129


If phenomena are of the nature of their cause,
Have phenomena not been analyzed?
Your cause is also pleasure and so on.
From that, cloth and so on do not arise. ^9-130


Pleasure and so on arise from cloth and so on.
Since that (cloth) does not exist, pleasure and so on do not exist.
And pleasure and so on, as permanent,
Are never perceived. ^9-131


If pleasure and so on are clearly existent,
Why are they not apprehended by experience?
If that itself becomes subtle,
How can it be both gross and subtle? ^9-132


By abandoning the gross and becoming subtle,
Those subtle and gross (states) are impermanent.
Similarly, why should all phenomena
Not be considered impermanent? ^9-133


If you assert that nothing non-existent
Can arise, because it is non-existent,
Then even if you do not wish for it to arise without being manifest,
It still exists. ^9-134


If the result resides in the cause,
Then one who eats food would be eating impurities.
With the price of cotton cloth,
Buy cotton seeds and wear them. ^9-135


If ordinary people, due to delusion, do not see it,
Then that truth is established by knowledge.
If that knowledge also exists for ordinary people,
Why do they not see it? ^9-136


If ordinary perception is not valid cognition,
Then seeing what is clear is also not true.
If valid cognition is not valid,
Then what is measured by it would not be false? ^9-137


In that case, meditating on emptiness
Would therefore be inappropriate.
Without touching the conceived existent,
Its non-existence cannot be apprehended. ^9-138


Therefore, whatever is a false existent,
Its non-existence is clearly false.
Therefore, regarding the death of a dream child,
The thought "it does not exist" ^9-139


Is an obstacle to the conceptualization of its existence,
And that (thought) is also false.
Therefore, by such analysis,
Nothing exists without a cause. ^9-140


It does not reside in individual
Or collected conditions.
It does not come from elsewhere,
Does not stay, and does not go. ^9-141


What is made truly existent by delusion,
How is it different from an illusion?
What is created by an illusionist,
And what is created by causes, ^9-142


Investigate from where it comes,
And where it goes.
That which is seen by proximity to something,
And is not there if that (something) is absent, ^9-143


Like a created reflection,
How can it have true existence?
If a thing already exists,
What need is there for a cause? ^9-144


But if it does not exist,
What need is there for a cause?
Even hundreds of millions of causes
Cannot transform a non-existent thing into an existent one. ^9-145


How can that state be existent?
And what else would become existent?
If existence is not possible when there is non-existence,
When will a thing become existent? ^9-146


Without a thing having arisen,
Its non-existence will not cease.
If it has not ceased from non-existence,
There is no occasion for a thing to exist. ^9-147


A thing also cannot become non-existent,
Because it would entail two natures.
Thus, cessation does not exist,
And things also do not exist. Therefore, ^9-148


All these beings (or phenomena)
Are eternally unborn and unceasing.
Beings are like a dream,
And when analyzed, like a plantain tree. ^9-149


Nirvana and non-nirvana
Are not different in reality.
Thus, for these empty phenomena,
What is there to gain, what is there to lose? ^9-150


What is there to be honored,
Or what is there to be completely disdained?
From what are pleasure and suffering?
What is there to dislike, what is there to like? ^9-151


If sought in reality,
Who craves, and for what?
When analyzed, in this living world,
Who is it that dies here? ^9-152


Who is yet to be born, and who has been born?
And who are relatives and friends?
All things are like space;
Let them be completely embraced by one like me. ^9-153


Those who desire their own happiness,
Through causes of conflict and joy,
Become greatly agitated and delighted.
They grieve, strive, dispute, and... ^9-154


Mutually cut and pierce each other,
And live with great difficulty due to their sins.
Having come repeatedly to fortunate realms,
And repeatedly experienced much happiness, ^9-155


After death, they fall into unbearable suffering
In lower realms for a long time.
In cyclic existence, there are many pitfalls.
There is nothing like this in reality. ^9-156


There, too, due to mutual contradictions,
There is nothing like this in cyclic existence in reality.
That too is an ocean of suffering,
Immeasurable and unbearable. ^9-157


There, one is so weak,
And life is also very short.
There, the efforts of staying alive and healthy,
And the weariness of hunger and thirst, and... ^9-158


Sleep, harm, and similarly,
Meaningless association with fools,
Life passes quickly and meaninglessly.
Analytical wisdom is extremely difficult to find. ^9-159


There, too, where distraction is habitual,
Where is the means to turn it back?
There, too, because one falls into the great lower realms,
Mara strives (to prevent liberation). ^9-160


There are many wrong paths,
And it is difficult to overcome doubt.
Again, leisure (precious human rebirth) is difficult to find,
And the appearance of a Buddha is extremely rare. ^9-161


The river of afflictions is difficult to abandon.
Alas, suffering will continue!
Even though they suffer so greatly,
Those who do not see their own suffering, ^9-162


These who dwell in the river of suffering,
Alas, they are worthy of lamentation!
For example, some repeatedly bathe,
And repeatedly enter fire. ^9-163


Similarly, even though they dwell in great suffering,
They imagine themselves to be happy.
Thus, those who live as if
There is no old age or death, ^9-164


Having been killed right at the beginning,
Will experience unbearable falls into lower realms.
To those tormented by the fire of suffering,
Born from the clouds of merit, ^9-165


By the rain of their own accumulated happiness,
When will I pacify them?
When, in a manner free from conceptualization,
Having respectfully accumulated collections of merit, ^9-166


Will I teach emptiness to those
Who are ruined by conceptualization? ^9-167


From "Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Conduct," this is the ninth chapter, "The Perfection of Wisdom." ^9-a

## 10. Chapter Ten: Dedication ^10-0


May all beings,
through the virtue of this Bodhisattva's way of life
that I have fully begun,
enter the Bodhisattva's way of life. ^10-1


May all those who are sick and suffering,
in body and mind, in all directions,
through my accumulated good deeds,
obtain an ocean of happiness and joy. ^10-2


May they never lose happiness
for as long as cyclic existence endures.
May all beings attain the unbroken happiness
of a Bodhisattva. ^10-3


May all the hell realms
that exist in the world,
become places where beings
rejoice in the happiness of the blissful. ^10-4


May those tormented by cold find warmth.
May those tormented by heat be cooled
by the endless rivers of water
that flow from the great clouds of Bodhisattvas. ^10-5


May the forests of sword-leaves
become dense sandalwood groves.
And may the Silk-cotton trees
become wish-fulfilling trees. ^10-6


May the hellish regions be adorned
with the sweet sounds of coots, cranes, swans,
and other melodious birds.
May they become delightful with lakes filled with fragrant lotuses. ^10-7


May the heaps of burning coals
turn into heaps of jewels.
May the scorched earth be covered with crystal ground.
May the crushing mountains become
immeasurable palaces filled with Buddhas. ^10-8


May the showers of burning embers, hot stones, and weapons
henceforth become showers of flowers.
And may the mutual striking with weapons
henceforth become the playful throwing of flowers. ^10-9


May those who sink in the dreadful, fire-pit-like rivers,
their flesh all dissolved, their bones the color of jasmine flowers,
through the power of my good deeds, obtain divine bodies,
and dwell with goddesses in gently flowing rivers. ^10-10


"Why are Yama's attendants, crows, and dreadful vultures fleeing in terror?
Whose mighty power is this, dispelling all darkness and bringing joy?"
Looking up, may they see the blazing Vajrapani in the sky,
and filled with great joy, be freed from evil, and accompany him. ^10-11


May they see showers of flowers mixed with fragrant water falling,
extinguishing the hellish flames with a hiss,
and thinking, "What is this sudden, overwhelming bliss?"
May the beings in hell see Avalokiteshvara. ^10-12


"Friends, cast off your fear and quickly come to us!
By whose power have all sufferings ceased, and joy arisen?
Who is this youthful, radiant one with a topknot,
who saves all beings, filled with the spirit of awakening and compassion, and makes us fearless?" ^10-13


"Look at him, whose lotus feet are honored by the crowns of a hundred gods,
whose compassionate eyes are moist, upon whose head fall showers of many flowers,
and who is surrounded by thousands of goddesses in delightful palaces, singing praises!"
May the beings in hell now cry out upon seeing such a Manjushri. ^10-14


Thus, through my roots of virtue,
may the beings in hell be overjoyed
upon seeing Bodhisattvas like Samantabhadra
raining down clouds of unobstructed, cool, and fragrant happiness. ^10-15


May animals be freed
from the fear of devouring each other.
And may hungry ghosts be happy,
like the people of the northern continent. ^10-16


May hungry ghosts be satisfied
by the streams of milk flowing from the hand
of the noble Lord Avalokiteshvara,
and always be cooled by bathing. ^10-17


May the blind see with their eyes,
and the deaf always hear sounds.
May pregnant women give birth without harm,
just like the goddess Mayadevi. ^10-18


May the naked obtain clothes,
the hungry obtain food,
the thirsty obtain water,
and delicious drinks. ^10-19


May the poor obtain wealth.
May the sorrowful and distressed obtain joy.
May the disheartened be cheered,
and may they achieve perfect steadfastness. ^10-20


May all sick beings
quickly be freed from illness.
May all diseases of beings
never arise again. ^10-21


May the frightened be fearless.
May the bound be freed.
May the powerless become powerful,
and may their minds be gentle towards each other. ^10-22


May all travelers
find happiness in all directions.
May their purposes for traveling
be accomplished without effort. ^10-23


May those on boats and great ships
have their wishes fulfilled,
reach the shore safely,
and rejoice with their relatives. ^10-24


May those who have strayed and wander on sorrowful paths
meet with fellow travelers,
and without fear of robbers, tigers, and so on,
travel easily and without weariness. ^10-25


May children, the elderly, and the helpless,
who are lost on dangerous, pathless wildernesses and so forth,
as well as those who are asleep, intoxicated, or deeply drunk,
be protected by the gods. ^10-26


May they be freed from all weariness,
and possess faith, wisdom, and compassion.
May they have abundant food and good conduct,
and always remember their past lives. ^10-27


May all beings have inexhaustible possessions,
like a treasury in the sky.
May they live freely,
without conflict or harm. ^10-28


May all beings who have little splendor
attain great splendor.
May those who are ascetics and have ugly forms
attain perfect beautiful forms. ^10-29


May all women in the world
become men.
May the lowly attain high status,
and may pride be overcome. ^10-30


Through these good deeds of mine,
may all beings, without exception,
abandon all evil deeds,
and always perform virtuous actions. ^10-31


May they never be separated from the spirit of awakening,
and be devoted to the Bodhisattva's way of life.
May they be embraced by the Buddhas,
and abandon all demonic actions. ^10-32


May all those beings
have immeasurably long lives.
May they always live happily,
and may the sound of death never be heard. ^10-33


May all directions be filled
with wish-fulfilling tree groves,
filled with Buddhas and Bodhisattvas,
proclaiming the sweet sound of the Dharma. ^10-34


May the earth everywhere
be free of pebbles and so forth,
smooth as a palm,
and soft, made of lapis lazuli. ^10-35


May many Bodhisattvas
adorn the earth with their excellence,
and dwell in all assemblies of followers. ^10-36


May all beings hear
the uninterrupted sound of the Dharma
from birds, trees, all rays of light,
and even from the sky. ^10-37


May they always meet with Buddhas
and Bodhisattvas.
May they worship the teachers of beings
with endless clouds of offerings. ^10-38


May the gods send rain in due season,
and may the harvests be abundant.
May kings rule righteously,
and may the world prosper. ^10-39


May medicines be potent,
and recitations of secret mantras be effective.
May dakinis, rakshasas, and so forth,
be endowed with compassionate minds. ^10-40


May no beings suffer,
be afraid, or be scorned.
May no one be unhappy. ^10-41


May monasteries be adorned with study
and recitation, and flourish.
May the monastic community always be in harmony,
and may the aims of the community be fulfilled. ^10-42


May monks who desire training
also attain solitude.
May they abandon all distractions,
and meditate with minds fit for practice. ^10-43


May nuns be endowed with gains,
and abandon quarrels and harm.
Similarly, may all renunciants
never lose their ethical conduct. ^10-44


May those who have broken their ethical conduct, feeling remorse,
always exhaust their misdeeds.
May they attain fortunate rebirths,
and there too, never lose their vows. ^10-45


May the wise be honored,
and also obtain alms.
May their minds be completely purified,
and may they be renowned in all directions. ^10-46


May they not experience the sufferings of the lower realms,
and without undergoing hardship,
may they quickly attain Buddhahood
with bodies superior to those of gods. ^10-47


May all beings repeatedly
worship all Buddhas,
and always be endowed with happiness
through the immeasurable joy of the Buddhas. ^10-48


May the Bodhisattvas
accomplish the welfare of beings as they intend.
May whatever the Protectors intend
be realized for all beings. ^10-49


Similarly, may Solitary Buddhas
and Disciples also be happy. ^10-50


Through the kindness of Manjushri,
until I attain the stage of great joy,
may I always remember my past lives,
and obtain renunciation. ^10-51


May I also live,
endowed with the taste of food.
May I attain perfect solitude
in all my lives. ^10-52


Whenever I wish to see him,
or to ask him anything,
may I see the Protector Manjushri himself,
without any hindrance. ^10-53


For the sake of accomplishing the welfare of all beings,
who fill the extent of space in the ten directions,
may my conduct be like that
of Manjushri. ^10-54


For as long as space endures,
and for as long as living beings remain,
may I too remain,
to dispel the sufferings of beings. ^10-55


Whatever sufferings beings have,
may all of them ripen upon me.
And may all beings enjoy happiness
through the assembly of Bodhisattvas. ^10-56


May the teachings, the sole medicine for the sufferings of beings,
the source of all happiness,
remain for a long time,
with gain and honor. ^10-57


I bow to Manjushri,
through whose kindness virtuous thoughts arise.
I also bow to my virtuous spiritual friend,
through whose kindness I have flourished. ^10-58


Thus ends the tenth chapter, "Dedication," from *Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Way of Life*. ^10-a

## Translator's Colophon ^b-0

The Indian master Sarvajñādeva and the chief revisor and translator, the monk Bande Peltsé, revised and established this translation from a Kashmiri manuscript. ^b-1

Later, the Indian master Dharmashrībhadra, and the chief revisor and translator, the monk Rinchen Zangpo, and Shakya Lodrö, corrected and revised it in accordance with manuscripts and commentaries from central India, and established it. ^b-2

Even later, the Indian master Sumatikīrti and the chief revisor and translator, the monk Lodën Sherab, thoroughly corrected and revised it, and perfectly established it. ^b-3
