## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 28 and 29 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Throughout our lives, knowingly and unknowingly, we gather faults in three ways: wrongdoing we commit ourselves, wrongdoing we get others to do, and wrongdoing we are quietly pleased about when others do it. These verses do not let us make excuses for any of the three. They give us the courage to name our mistakes honestly and to confess them from the heart rather than with the mouth alone.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

Shantideva has been confessing what he did. Here he widens it. Across beginningless samsara he did wrong himself, he got other people to do wrong, and when others did wrong he was quietly pleased about it.

Khenpo Kunzang Palden, an early commentator on this text, makes the point most of us would never reach on our own. There is no difference in weight between the three. Being glad about someone else's wrongdoing carries the same fault as doing it yourself. Rejoicing costs nothing, nobody sees it, and so nobody ever counts it against themselves.

The commentaries also say why we do it, and the answer is not cruelty. We do not see a misdeed as a misdeed, and we are confused about how actions lead to results. On that question, they say, we are no wiser than an animal: we follow whatever pleases us now and never look at where it leads.

This is why verse 29 puts seeing before speaking. Shantideva recognizes the mistake as a mistake first, and confesses after. Words on their own would not have been a confession.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when I see someone being criticized or harmed, I will not let myself feel pleased. I will recognize what is happening as a mistake.

**Explanation:** *(Avoiding Wrongdoing)* Most of us do not commit serious harm ourselves. But when someone else does something bad, or when a person we dislike meets defeat or trouble, a quiet satisfaction rises in us that we never admit to. Today's verses say that being glad about another person's wrongdoing carries the same fault as doing it ourselves. So today I will watch for that quiet satisfaction and set it down. When I hear that someone has failed or been caught out, I will name it inwardly as a mistake rather than as good news. Giving up this one habit closes a door through which we gather harm without ever noticing.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Main Body of the Fourth Branch, the Confession of Wrongdoing — the Opening of the Power of Repudiation (the Power of Regret)

Both Kunzang Pelden and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain that from this verse onward the first of the four powers that purify wrongdoing (the power of repudiation, the power of the support, the power of applying every remedy, and the power of restoration) — the power of repudiation, or the power of regret — is begun.

###### 2. The Starting Point in Time of the Wrongdoing — From Beginningless Samsara Down to the Present Life

The span of time covered by the confession of wrongdoing is explained as a measureless one: "from samsara, which has no beginning, in this present life or in other, earlier lives."

###### 3. The Cause of Wrongdoing — the Delusion of Not Knowing

The chief reason for doing wrong is not knowing, not understanding, the harm of one's actions; that is, being under the sway of ignorance and the afflictions. Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives this as an example: it is the force of a bewilderment about what to take up and give up in actions and their effects that makes one like an animal.

###### 4. The Two Ways Wrongdoing Is Carried Out — Doing It Oneself, Directly, and Making Another Do It

All the commentaries agree in the end that wrongful action is covered by two modes of carrying it out: doing it oneself, directly, through body, speech and mind, or else setting another to do it, that is, making them do it.

###### 5. Actual Examples of Wrongdoing — Killing, Lying, Covetousness

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk sets it out clearly, giving actual examples of the wrongdoing of the three doors: killing and the rest through the body, lying and the rest through speech, and covetousness and the rest with the mind.

###### 6. The Essence of Wrongdoing — Non-Virtue That Is by Nature Blameworthy

Sabzang Mati Panchen sets out clearly that the essence of wrongdoing is non-virtue by nature together with what is blameworthy.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse begins the main body of the fourth branch, the confession of wrongdoing — that is, the first of the four purifying powers, the power of repudiation (the power of regret) — and sets out clearly how, from beginningless samsara down to the present life, under the sway of an ignorance that did not know the harm involved, one either did wrong oneself, directly, or made another do it.

**Key points.**

- This is the opening of the main body of the branch of confessing wrongdoing: the first of the four purifying powers, the power of repudiation.
- The time of the wrongdoing is a measureless span, from beginningless samsara down to the present life.
- The chief cause of wrongdoing is the ignorance that does not know the harm of one's actions; Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives as an example that this is like the dullness of an animal.
- The carrying out of wrongdoing is divided in two: doing it oneself, directly, and making another do it.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk gives actual examples of the wrongdoing of body, speech and mind: killing, lying, and covetousness.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Three Kinds of Wrongdoing Must Be Confessed, With No Difference of Greater and Lesser

Kunzang Pelden sets out clearly that the three — doing it oneself, directly; setting another to do it; and whatever rejoicing, that is, gladness, one felt at the wrong that others did — do not differ in the weight of their wrongdoing, and so all of them must be confessed.

###### 2. Confession Has to Be Preceded by Seeing the Fault

Both Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain that "having come to see the fault as a fault," and not merely with the mouth, in mere words, means that recognizing the fault or misdeed for oneself must come first, before the confession.

###### 3. The Confession Must Come From the Depths of the Heart, Not Merely From the Mouth

Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and His Holiness the Dalai Lama all explain in agreement that "from the depths of my thought" means one must acknowledge and confess from the depths of the heart, from the very core, in the presence of the protectors.

###### 4. The Pledge Not to Do It Again Is Also Part of Confession

Both Kunzang Pelden and Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explain, following "and I will refrain from it from now on" and "I neither conceal it nor hide it," that at the close of confession the pledge not to do the wrong again must also be complete.

###### 5. The Wrongdoing of Rejoicing Arises Through Being Overborne by Delusion

Many commentaries explain in agreement how, overcome by the deluded confusion that is bewildered about what to take up and give up in actions and their effects, one becomes glad about and rejoices in the wrong that others do.

###### 6. The Objects of Such Rejoicing Include the Ten Non-Virtues and the Five Acts of Immediate Retribution

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk sets out clearly that to see, hear of, or come to know of whatever wrong others do — the ten non-virtues, the five acts of immediate retribution and the like — and then to rejoice in it, carries a fault no different from having done the deed oneself.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** On top of the previous verse's wrongdoing — what one did oneself and what one made others do — this verse adds the fault of having been overborne by deluded confusion and so rejoicing in, that is, being glad about, the wrong that others did; and it explains how all three kinds of wrongdoing, with no difference of greater and lesser, are recognized and seen as faults and then acknowledged and confessed to the protectors from the depths of the heart.

**Key points.**

- Wrongdoing done oneself, wrongdoing one made another do, and rejoicing in what others did are alike in weight, and all three must be confessed.
- The wrongdoing of rejoicing arises through being overborne by the delusion that is bewildered about actions and their effects.
- Before confessing, one must recognize and see the fault for oneself; and the confession itself must be made not merely with the mouth, in mere words, but from the depths of the heart, in the presence of the protectors.
- Both Kunzang Pelden and Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explain that the pledge not to do the wrong again must also be included at the close of the confession.
- ⚑ There is a disagreement about the sense of "I see": most commentaries explain it as recognizing and seeing the fault for oneself, whereas Minyak Kunzang Sonam, following the Great Commentary, explains it as seeing by force of the fruit of wrongful action ripening and doing harm.
