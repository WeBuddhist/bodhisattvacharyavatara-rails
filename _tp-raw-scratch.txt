256: <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
257: #### Main Teaching Points
258: 
259: 1. **Virtue is always feeble.** Because the precious human support, with its freedoms and advantages, is hard to find, a Dharma-minded attitude is rare to arise, and even when it arises it does not last long, the virtue that partakes of merit is like a flash of lightning — occurring only now and then — and so it remains at all times weak in strength.
260: 
261: 2. **Wrongdoing is powerful and utterly unbearable.** Because the causes of non-virtue are easily gathered, wrongdoing arises in a continuous stream with great power and force, like the darkness within thick clouds, and produces the utterly unbearable sufferings of the lower realms such as the hells.
262: 
263: 3. **Perfect bodhicitta alone overpowers it.** No ordinary virtue can overpower that great wrongdoing; only the mind of perfect enlightenment — whose nature is both aspiring and engaging bodhicitta, the wish to deliver all beings from the lower realms — is able to overpower it. Khenpo Kunga clarifies that both aspiring and engaging bodhicitta are gathered here.
264: 
265: 4. **The benefit of relative bodhicitta.** As both Gyaltsab and Minyak Kunzang Sonam make clear, what is chiefly taught here is the benefit of relative bodhicitta: it has the twofold power to purify the wrongdoing accumulated earlier — the cause of the lower realms — and to cut off the continuation of future non-virtue. Ultimate bodhicitta also has the power to eliminate the seed of the obscurations, but that is not the chief subject here.
266: 
267: 5. **The two readings of "always."** Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives two ways of explaining the word "always" in the verse: (1) applied to virtue, so that "virtue is always feeble"; and (2) applied to wrongdoing, so that "wrongdoing is always powerful and unbearable." Both accord with the meaning of the verse and may be taken up.
268: 
269: 6. **The power to overcome the discordant side in a single instant.** His Holiness the Dalai Lama teaches this with the example of a great weapon: just as, however powerful an enemy army decked out with countless weapons may be, a single substance of great force can destroy it in an instant, so bodhicitta too can overcome, in a single instant, the entire discordant side accumulated since beginningless time. He likewise explains the very meaning of the word "enlightenment" (byang chub) — byang = the purifying, without remainder, of the whole class of faults and misdeeds; chub = the attaining of the knowledge that sees the two truths simultaneously — in accord with the key point of this verse.

428: #### Main Teaching Points
429: 
430: 1. **Established by the mighty sages' vision and by scriptural authority.** The force of this line is not mere reasoning but rests on the valid cognition of scripture: the victors, the mighty sages, having examined and analyzed well over countless eons, arrived at the certainty that bodhicitta alone is the single cause of all benefit and happiness for boundless beings. Gyaltsab, Sabzang, and Khenpo Kunga all take "the mighty sages" in the plural to indicate all the buddhas, and clarify that, since this is scriptural authority established from all the sutras of the mighty sages and not dependent on any one, it cannot later be arbitrarily dismissed.
431: 
432: 2. **The single cause of all benefit and happiness.** Bodhicitta is explained as the sole cause of benefit and happiness. Ngulchu Thogme puts it with great clarity: "No other established cause of benefit and happiness is to be found apart from this very bodhicitta." This certainty — that it is established from this one thing alone, and no other cause — shows why one must give rise to bodhicitta without delay.
433: 
434: 3. **Boundless beings attain supreme bliss with ease.** Through bodhicitta, even boundless and immeasurable multitudes of beings attain the supreme bliss of perfect enlightenment both easily (without hardship) and quickly (swiftly). All the commentaries present these two qualities as the mark of bodhicitta being the supreme method.
435: 
436: 4. **The ultimate vision over many eons.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the phrase "for many eons" indicates the span of time over which the mighty sages examined and analyzed by way of the path, and that it should not be taken as a mere jātaka tale. The longer the period of analysis, the more it serves as a mark of a truly settled certainty.

596: #### Main Teaching Points
597: 
598: 1. **Never forsake bodhicitta.** The chief point is this: this very bodhicitta must never at any time (rtag tu) be forsaken from one's own mindstream. All six scholars agree here. Khenpo Kunga's explanation is especially clear: it is not merely a matter of not forsaking it for an instant, but of never casting it away from one's mindstream at all.
599: 
600: 2. **Overcoming the hundreds of sufferings.** This line has in mind the wish to overcome the hundreds of the sufferings of existence, and so it includes even the intention of the lesser person, who wishes to be freed from one's own suffering of samsara. For such a person too, bodhicitta is taught to be the supreme method, with no other method available.
601: 
602: 3. **Dispelling all the unhappiness of all beings.** This is the intention of the great person (skyes bu chen po): the wish to dispel, not merely one's own suffering, but every unhappiness of all beings without remainder. This too is an aim accomplished by bodhicitta.
603: 
604: 4. **Enjoying many happinesses.** Both Sabzang and Kunzang Pelden explain that this pervades both higher rebirth (mngon mtho) and definite goodness (nges par legs pa): both worldly happiness and the liberation of enlightenment are aims accomplished by bodhicitta.
605: 
606: 5. **Benefiting all three types of person.** Both Gyaltsab and Minyak read the three verbs of this line (gzhom 'dod / bsal 'dod / spyod 'dod — "wishing to overcome / to dispel / to enjoy") as marking, respectively, the wished-for aims of the lesser, middling, and great persons. They show that bodhicitta is established as the single method that accomplishes all the aims of all three types of person.

250: #### Main Teaching Points
251: 
252: 1. **In a single instant, the name changes.** The very moment bodhicitta is born in one's own mindstream, in a single instant the name changes and one comes to be spoken of as "an heir of the Blissful Ones" — with no regard whatever to the body being male or female, age old or young, or family good or bad, and with no need for any preliminary period of time.
253: 
254: 2. **The change of name and the change of standing — two.** Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen distinguishes two: the distinguished name, being spoken of as "heir of the Blissful Ones," and the distinguished standing, becoming worthy to receive the homage of the world with its gods and humans. He explains that this is accomplished the very moment bodhicitta is born, without regard to whether or not a bodhisattva ground has been attained.
255: 
256: 3. **The wretched being bound in the prison of samsara.** Before bodhicitta arose, one was a wretched being bound as if imprisoned by karma and the afflictions; the moment bodhicitta arises one becomes worthy of homage — illustrating the great reversal.
257: 
258: 4. **Worthy to receive the homage of the world with its gods and humans.** At the same time as the name changes, in terms of standing too one becomes an object of homage for all the gods, demigods, and humans of the world — as all the commentaries establish.
259: 
260: 5. **Bodhicitta is the teacher of all the buddhas.** Kunzang Pelden explains that because bodhicitta is the very teacher (source) of all the buddhas, it is worthy of homage even by the gods. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk cites the verse: "To whomever that supreme and precious mind is born — to the body of that person I pay homage."

430: #### Main Teaching Points
431: 
432: 1. **Bodhicitta, like the gold-making elixir.** Bodhicitta is like the supreme gold-making elixir: just as the supreme elixir, with a single measure, can turn a thousand measures of iron into gold, so bodhicitta takes up this impure, affliction-ridden body and can turn it into the Form Body of a victor. This transformation is shown in two aspects — in essence (from impure to pure) and in scale (from small to vast).
433: 
434: 2. **Transforming the impure body into the body of a victor.** All the commentaries explain the two aspects of the transformation alike: the impure body (impure by nature and veiled by karma and the afflictions) is turned into the body of a victor (the Form Body of a buddha). The *Noble Maitreya's Liberation* illustrates this in detail: by the essence of bodhicitta all the iron of karma and affliction is turned into the gold of omniscience.
435: 
436: 3. **Transformed beyond all price.** No conventional price whatever can be set on the body of a victor: it is a treasure beyond measure. Khenpo Zhenga's annotation renders it plainly — "precious and beyond all price."
437: 
438: 4. **Hold it utterly firm and unwavering.** The stanza's imperative ("hold it!") teaches that one must grasp bodhicitta and keep it utterly firm and unwavering. Just as, seeing such benefit, one guards a substance as precious as gold-making elixir, so all the commentaries alike explain that bodhicitta itself must be held firmly.
439: 
440: 5. **The three grades of gold-making elixir.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains three grades — the lowest (1:1), the middling (1:100), and the supreme (1:1000) — and shows that bodhicitta is that very supreme grade. The transformation of this impure body into a fruit of supreme value is no small thing, but of the very highest degree.
441: 
442: 6. **By what condition and means is it accomplished?** His Holiness the Dalai Lama poses directly the reason for this transformation, and answers that the cause is bodhicitta itself, which cherishes others more than oneself. Taken up upon this impure body, it accomplishes the priceless body of a victor adorned with the major and minor marks.
443: 
444: 7. **Kunzang Pelden explains the three stanzas together.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden comments on stanzas 1-10, 1-11, and 1-12 as a single unit: the gold-making simile (1-10), the precious-jewel simile (1-11), and the fruit-bearing-tree simile (1-12) are set out in sequence.

371: #### Main Teaching Points
372: 
373: 1. **The example of wishing to go and actually going.** The verse clarifies the aspiring and engaging minds as distinct by mapping them onto two cases: the mind wishing to go to a place, and actually setting out on the road and traveling. Though in both the intention "I shall attain buddhahood for the sake of others" is one and the same, they differ in whether or not one is engaged in the act of practice — generosity and the rest.
374:

376: 
377: 3. **Two methods of meditation: the exchange of self and other, and the sevenfold cause-and-effect instruction.** His Holiness the Dalai Lama teaches that, although there are two methods of cultivating bodhicitta — the pith instruction of exchanging self and other, and the sevenfold cause-and-effect instruction — here in the Bodhicaryāvatāra the method of exchanging self and other is taken as the main one. He advises that it is right to use whichever means suits the individual meditator's mental disposition; and he notes that scholars disagree on whether recognizing all beings as one's mother must be set as a preliminary to exchanging self and other, without giving a definite conclusion.
378: 
379: 4. **The aspiring mind outshines the qualities of the shravakas.** Even without having entered the path, and even when unable [to practice] the perfections, merely generating the aspiring mind means one does not lose the name "bodhisattva" and outshines the qualities of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas — as taught in the *Account of Maitreya's Liberation*.

237: #### Main Teaching Points
238: 
239: 1. **Great results arise in samsara from the aspiring mind.** — Even without the practice of conduct, merely giving rise to the aspiring mind can win, within samsara, the excellent sovereignty of a king of gods such as Brahmā or Indra, or of a human wheel-turning monarch; so it should not be disparaged.
240: 
241: 2. **The distinctive feature of a continuous stream of merit.** — Though the aspiring mind brings great results, it does not — as the engaging mind does, engaged with diligent effort in the perfections such as generosity — produce merit in a continuous, unbroken stream; therefore the engaging mind is supreme.
242: 
243: 3. **The vajra example.** — Just as a cracked vajra still outshines golden ornaments, so the aspiring mind, though it has not entered the path, outshines the qualities of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas and does not lose the name "bodhisattva."
244: 
245: 4. **The instruction not to disparage the aspiring mind.** — Even one who cannot train in the six perfections should by all means train in ripening the mind of awakening.

391: #### Main Teaching Points
392: 
393: 1. **"From whatever moment" — marking a point in time.** — With the words "from whatever moment," a definite point in time is set out: the moment when the mind of awakening is genuinely taken up. KKP's commentary sets out these five distinctions explicitly.
187: 
188: 2. **The point of the four kinds of giving (NKW).** — NKW explains clearly that the bodhisattva gives, unceasingly, all four kinds of giving (སྦྱིན་པ་རྣམ་པ་བཞི་): the giving of Dharma (ཆོས་), the giving of material goods (ཟང་ཟིང་), the giving of fearlessness / protection (མི་འཇིགས་པའི་སྐྱབས་), and the giving of loving-kindness (བྱམས་པ་) — four in all. These four kinds of giving encompass every form of benefit completely.
189: 
190: 3. **The point of the unsurpassed bliss of the Blissful Ones.** — The bodhisattva's gift is by its very nature without equal: it is not ordinary material goods such as food, but the very "unsurpassed bliss of the Blissful Ones" (བདེ་གཤེགས་ཀྱི་བདེ་བ་བླ་ན་མེད་). All four of KS, KKP, SMPLG, and NKW explain this distinction of the gift as the chief point that sets it apart from the ordinary giving of food in 1-32.

337: #### Main Teaching Points
338: 
339: 1. **The point of measuring each moment by an eon.** — This verse definitively shows a result in which each single moment of a negative mind aroused toward a bodhisattva equals one eon in the hells. This is not a mere estimate or a rhetorical warning, but a profound point in which the number of moments equals the number of eons — established as a scriptural fact by all the commentaries (KS, KKP, GDR, NTS, SMPLG, MKS, NKW), which directly cite the *Praśāntaviniścayaprātihārya Sūtra*.
340: 
341: 2. **The point of the ground — the heir of the victors, the patron of giving.** — What all the commentaries teach as the chief point is that the reason the fault of anger here is so extremely great depends on its ground: it is directed at a bodhisattva who has become the great patron of giving for all beings of the three realms. NKW distinguishes three kinds of wrong attitude — anger, contempt, and wrong faith. GDR too cites the Sutra of [Developing] Faith, [showing] that harming a bodhisattva is a far greater misdeed than destroying stupas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges.
342: 
343: 3. **The point that NTS pairs [1-34] with its opposite.** — NTS (and KS likewise) explains verse 1-34 together with the positive aspect at the start of 1-35 ("if one's mind turns bright and glad"), under a single outline heading, "that [the bodhisattva] is a special field," presenting the good and the bad as a linked pair: toward that very special ground, giving rise to a negative mind brings an extremely great fault, while giving rise to faith and respect brings extremely great merit.
344: 
345: 4. **The point that one must cultivate pure perception (His Holiness the Dalai Lama).** — Since we cannot know for certain whether bodhicitta is present in any given being's mind-stream (we do not know where a bodhisattva may be), TG draws out the practical upshot that we must cultivate pure perception toward everyone. Citing the Eight Verses of Mind Training by Langri Thangpa and the words of Dromtönpa, he explains that taking the lowest place oneself is a safeguard against generating, in advance, any contemptuous attitude toward a hidden bodhisattva.

246: #### Main Teaching Points
247: 
248: 1. **How adverse conditions turn into virtue.** This verse shows the reverse [counterpart] of the point of verse 1-34: just as, earlier, anger toward a bodhisattva increases one's wrongdoing over as many eons as the instants [of anger], so here a bright, faithful mind (first hemistich) increases one's merit over a like number of eons; and though great hardship befalls the buddhas' heirs (second hemistich), no wrongdoing arises, and moreover their virtue naturally increases. Khenpo Kunga's fire-and-wind image makes this vivid: whatever wind blows, it becomes a friend to the blaze.
249: 
250: 2. **The four means of gathering (KS).** Khenpo Zhenga explains the specific reason adverse conditions do not produce wrongdoing but produce virtue in a bodhisattva's continuum: relying on the four means of gathering (generosity, kind speech, teaching what accords [with each being], acting in accord [with what one teaches]), the bodhisattva benefits beings, and so, even amid great hardship, does not turn back from these but has their virtue naturally increase.
251: 
252: 3. **The distinction of strength (KKP).** Kunzang Pelden marks a distinction of bodhisattvas' strength with the images of the sun and moon and the lamp: the strong cannot be harmed by adversity; even a weak one, though a few harms may come, gives rise to no wrongdoing at all and has virtue increase, slowly, all the same. Kunzang Pelden brings in Thogme's verse — "when the world and its beings are filled with wrongdoing, transform adverse conditions into the path of awakening" — as the practice-instruction of this very point.
253: 
254: 4. **The firmness of steady courage (Tenzin Gyatso).** Tenzin Gyatso explains the reason behind "virtue naturally increases": because the buddhas' heirs are always meditating on the great courage of bodhicitta, even when they meet a great, urgent condition no fault of mind arises; rather, that very urgency becomes the condition by which patience and love for others grow. Even without full-fledged bodhicitta, a good mind concordant with it gives a firmness that whatever condition arises cannot shake — as the story of the compassionate ship-captain shows.

421: #### Main Teaching Points
422: 
423: 1. **The object of homage.** The object of homage in this verse is the precious bodhicitta itself: no matter in whom it arises — high, low, or middling, male or female, old or young, without distinction (KKP: "in whomever, high or low"; NKW: "any being whatsoever, high, low, or middling, male or female, old or young, without distinction") — [that being] becomes worthy of homage. With this verse, Chapter One — the explanation of bodhicitta's benefits — closes with Shantideva himself paying homage and going for refuge to the beings who possess bodhicitta.
424: 
425: 2. **How the doer of harm too is brought to happiness.** Even one who does harm to a bodhisattva is, in the end, connected with happiness temporary and ultimate; this is accomplished by the fourfold power of the bodhisattva's compassion, bodhicitta, skillful means, and aspiration. Khenpo Kunga sets this out directly with the Kṣāntivādin jātaka: the five attendants who cut off the sage's head and limbs were later reborn as the good group of five, Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya and the rest, the first to be trained at Vārāṇasī.
426: 
427: 3. **That 1-34 and 1-36 do not contradict.** Verse 1-34 (that anger toward a bodhisattva causes rebirth in hell for as many eons as the instants [of anger]) and verse 1-36 (that even doing harm connects one with happiness) may seem to contradict; but Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang, and Ngulchu Thogme all explain that there is no contradiction: the former is spoken, from the standpoint that karma and its fruit are unfailing, of the immediate [result] of a negative mind, birth in hell; while here the point is that the bodhisattva's compassion and aspiration take up the very doer of harm and, in the end, [make his] samsara have an end. So both are true at once.
428: 
429: 4. **Training in pure perception (KKP).** Kunzang Pelden gives an emphatic counsel at the chapter's close: recognizing that beings endowed with bodhicitta are objects of homage for the world with its gods, and future buddhas, one should confess and vow restraint for whatever faults of the three doors one has committed toward the buddhas' heirs before, train in pure perception, and not look at beings' faults. Shown by the image of the [waxing] moon: even if a fault appears in a bodhisattva, because it is compounded it is exhausted by the power of meditating on the path and [the being] becomes a buddha; so it is seeing-as-a-fault that is itself the fault — the bodhisattva has none.
