## 4. Chapter Four: Being Careful and Mindful ^4-0


Now that a child of the Buddha has taken hold of
bodhicitta so firmly,
they should never be careless.
They should always try hard never to break their training. ^4-1


Something started carelessly,
or something not thought through properly —
even if a promise was made about it,
it makes sense to think again about whether to do it or not. ^4-2


But something that the Buddhas and their children
have examined with their great wisdom,
and that I myself have also thought through carefully —
what reason could there be to turn back from that? ^4-3


If, after making such a promise,
I do not actually follow through with it,
then I have deceived every single being.
What kind of place will I end up going to? ^4-4


It is said that if someone even just thinks
about giving away some small, ordinary thing,
and then does not give it after all,
that person becomes a hungry ghost because of it. ^4-5


So if I sincerely invite every being
to the greatest happiness there is,
and then I let all of them down instead,
how could I possibly end up somewhere good? ^4-6


There is a way, beyond ordinary understanding,
that even helps free those
who let go of their own bodhicitta —
only a fully awakened Buddha truly understands how. ^4-7


For a bodhisattva,
this is the heaviest kind of mistake there is.
If it happens,
it means failing every single being. ^4-8


And anyone who, even for a single moment,
gets in the way of another's goodness,
because they have failed other beings,
faces an endless string of difficult rebirths. ^4-9


If destroying the happiness of even one single being
already harms me deeply,
then how much worse would it be
to destroy the happiness of beings without end, filling the whole of space? ^4-10


So when the power of wrongdoing
and the power of bodhicitta
take turns pulling someone through life after life,
reaching the higher levels takes a very long time. ^4-11


That is why I must carry out
what I have promised, with real respect.
If I do not make an effort starting today,
I will only keep sinking lower and lower. ^4-12


Countless Buddhas, who help every single being,
have already come and gone,
yet because of my own faults,
I was never healed by their care. ^4-13


If I keep acting the same way now,
again and again, the same thing will keep happening:
I will fall into hard rebirths, facing sickness,
being bound, cut, and wounded. ^4-14


A Buddha appearing in the world,
having faith, and gaining a human life,
along with the chance to practice goodness —
these are all so rare; when will I ever have them again? ^4-15


Even a day like today, free from sickness,
with enough food and no real trouble,
still slips by in an instant.
This life is as brief and fragile as a flash. ^4-16


If I keep behaving the way I do now,
I may never gain a human life again.
And without a human life,
there is only wrongdoing, no goodness at all. ^4-17


When I have the rare chance to practice goodness
and still do not do it,
then when confusion and the suffering of hard rebirths overwhelm me,
what will I possibly be able to do then? ^4-18


If I do no good
and instead keep piling up wrongdoing,
then for a hundred million ages,
I will not even hear the words "happy rebirth." ^4-19


That is exactly why the Buddha said
that gaining a human life is as rare
as a blind turtle happening to poke its neck
through a yoke floating on a vast ocean. ^4-20


If even a single moment of wrongdoing
can lead to an age spent in the deepest hell,
then how could the wrongdoing gathered
since beginningless time possibly lead to a happy rebirth? ^4-21


And even after experiencing the result of that wrongdoing,
it is still not the end of it —
because while experiencing it,
even more wrongdoing tends to arise. ^4-22


Having found such a rare opportunity as this,
if I still do not practice goodness,
there is no greater deception than this,
and no greater foolishness than this. ^4-23


And if, understanding this,
I still fall into laziness out of confusion,
then when the time of death arrives,
a terrible grief will rise up in me. ^4-24


If my body must burn for a long, unbearable time
in the fires of hell,
then surely, unbearable regret
will burn and torment my mind as well. ^4-25


Having found, by such rare luck,
this precious and beneficial opportunity,
if I, even while understanding all this clearly,
am led back into hell again — ^4-26


it would be as if I had been stupefied by a spell;
I would have no real mind of my own left in this.
I do not even know what confuses me so —
what is really going on inside me? ^4-27


Enemies like anger and craving
have no legs, no arms, and no other such things.
They are not even brave or clever.
So how is it that they have made me their servant? ^4-28


While living inside my own mind,
they cheerfully cause me harm,
and yet I do not get angry at them —
patience in the wrong place like this deserves only criticism. ^4-29


Even if every god and demigod in existence
rose up against me as enemies,
none of them could ever
drag me into the fires of the deepest hell. ^4-30


But this powerful enemy, the disturbing emotions,
can, the moment I meet it,
throw me into that very fire in a single instant,
reducing even Mount Meru to ash without a trace. ^4-31


No ordinary enemy anywhere
could last as long
as my enemy, the disturbing emotions,
which have been with me, without beginning or end, for so very long. ^4-32


If treated kindly and respectfully,
everyone else can bring benefit and happiness.
But the disturbing emotions, if given any place at all,
only turn around and cause more suffering and harm. ^4-33


So if this long-lasting, constant enemy,
the one true cause of ever-growing harm,
is allowed to settle firmly in my heart,
how could I ever feel safe or happy while wandering through life? ^4-34


The guards of saṃsāra's prison, the executioners
who kill in the hell realms and elsewhere —
if I keep letting my mind stay caught
in the net of attachment, how could I ever find happiness there? ^4-35


So until I have truly and completely defeated
this enemy right in front of me, I must never give up trying.
Even those whose pride flares up at some small harm
cannot fall asleep until they have dealt with it — how much more should I feel this way! ^4-36


When disturbing emotions, already painful by nature since they lead to death,
stand ready for battle, determined to be fought and destroyed,
one should not turn back and flee before the goal is reached,
even facing the pain of being struck by arrows and spears. ^4-37


So today, as I strive to defeat
these emotions, which by their very nature are certainly my enemies, always the cause of every suffering,
why would I ever feel discouraged or want to give up,
even if it means facing hundreds of kinds of suffering along the way? ^4-38


Warriors treat even meaningless scars from an enemy's blade
as if they were decorations on their bodies.
So when I am striving with true purpose for something so important,
why should any suffering along the way be able to harm me? ^4-39


Fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and others,
thinking only of their own small livelihood,
still bear the pain of heat and cold and other hardships.
So why should I not bear hardship for the happiness of every being? ^4-40


I have promised to free every being
throughout the ten directions and the endless reaches of space
from their disturbing emotions.
Yet I myself am still not free from my own. ^4-41


Not even knowing my own limits,
speaking like that — was that not a little crazy of me?
So I must never turn back
from defeating my disturbing emotions. ^4-42


I will hold onto this determination firmly,
treat these emotions as things to fight against with real resolve,
and battle them completely —
except, of course, for the kind of healthy determination that itself helps defeat disturbing emotions. ^4-43


Even if I were burned, or killed,
or even if my head were cut off,
I will never, in any way,
bow down to my enemy, the disturbing emotions. ^4-44


An ordinary enemy, even if driven out of one place,
simply settles somewhere else, gathers strength there,
and later comes back again.
But the enemy of disturbing emotions does not work that way at all. ^4-45


Once seen clearly with the eye of wisdom and abandoned,
a disturbing emotion cleared from my mind has nowhere left to go.
It has no place to hide and then come back from to harm me again.
It is only my own weak-mindedness that keeps me from making the effort. ^4-46


Disturbing emotions do not live out in the world, nor in the senses, nor in between,
nor anywhere else — so where do they actually live while harming every being everywhere?
They are simply illusion-like, nothing more; so let go of fear in your heart, and hold onto effort for the sake of understanding.
Why would I let myself be harmed pointlessly in hell and other such places? ^4-47


Having thought all of this through carefully, just as it has been explained,
I should make every effort to carry out this training.
If a doctor's advice is never followed,
how could a sick person who needs medicine ever get well? ^4-48


This is the end of the fourth chapter, called "Being Careful and Mindful," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^4-a
