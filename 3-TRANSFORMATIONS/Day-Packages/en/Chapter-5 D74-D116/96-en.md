## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the fifty-seventh, fifty-eighth, and fifty-ninth verses of the fifth chapter. This precious human life is short. Even so, what would it be like if our mind were as steady as a mountain that the wind cannot move?

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging. We must think again and again about this. We are free from the lower realms. We have left the eight states without freedom behind. We have found a human body that is hard to find. We must make it meaningful. For example, these three cannot make Mount Meru shake: dust, birds, and the ocean. In the same way, we must hold our own mind firm and unmoving, so that bad friends, the suffering of hardship, hearing profound Dharma, and so on cannot shake it.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will think about how hard this rare human life is to find. Then I will keep my mind steady and not be moved by distraction.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* If we think about how hard it is to find this precious human body, we will give up pointless distraction. If the mind is as unmoving as Mount Meru, bad conditions cannot overcome it. In this way, our daily actions turn toward doing good.

### Verse 5-57

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-57`):*

> acting in ways that cause no harm to myself or others, and holding this mind without a solid sense of self, like an illusion — I should always keep it so.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Regarding things that are inherently blameless (ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་མེད་པ — things without fault): having brought them under the power of oneself, with a sense of self-respect (ངོ་ཚ་ཤེས་པ), and under the power of beings, abiding in a sense of consideration for others (ཁྲེལ་ཡོད་པ), one should always hold, without letting it decline, this mind that knows everything to be like an emanation, without a self.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

In engaging in blameless things or actions, one must hold as the main consideration that benefit and happiness come to oneself and also to other beings — that is, one makes the engagement subject to that. And even at times of engaging in such conduct, which brings benefit and happiness to oneself and others, one should look upon oneself as without inherent nature, like an emanation, and, without giving rise to pride, conceit, and the like, should always hold this mind. *(Editorial note: His Holiness's transclusion tags for ^5-56 and ^5-57 appear together with no prose between them, followed by a single shared prose block; it splits cleanly by content into a 5-56 portion, reserved for that verse's page, and this 5-57 portion.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-57]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whatever one engages in regarding blameless things — under the power of oneself: listening to the Dharma, eating food, and so on; and under the power of other beings: teaching the Dharma, giving generously, and so on — one must engage in these; and when engaging in them, just as an emanation has no grasping at "I" and "mine" such as the thoughts "I did this," "I am doing it," "I will do it," so too one must always hold this mind without grasping at "I" and "mine."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-57]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Engaging in one's own blameless virtuous matters is done by bringing under one's power the purposes of not letting oneself decline and of benefiting all beings; and, being held by the wisdom that realizes emptiness, like an illusion or an emanation performing actions, without pride or conceit, one should always hold this mind of one's own.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-57]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

One engages in blameless things having brought them under the power of the aims of oneself and beings — or else, as the *Detailed Explanation* (རྣམ་བཤད) says, "having brought them under the power of oneself and beings, abiding in self-respect (ངོ་ཚ་ཤེས་པ) and consideration for others (ཁྲེལ་ཡོད་པ)." Engaging in this way, even while one performs actions like an emanation, one should always hold the mind without pride. *(Editorial note: Ngulchu Thogme's commentary treats verses 5-55 through 5-57 in one continuous quotation and explanation; this excerpt is the portion addressing verse 5-57 specifically.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-104]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Regarding inherently blameless things to be done, having brought them under the power of oneself and beings, and — for instance, like an emanation — without pride or conceit, one should always hold this mind of one's own together with mindful awareness (ཤེས་བཞིན).

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-57]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

When one engages in one's own blameless virtuous things, such as generosity, for the sake of accomplishing others' welfare, one must engage having brought under one's power both the aim of not letting oneself decline and the aim of accomplishing benefit and happiness for other beings too, both for the time being and ultimately; and these, held by the wisdom that realizes emptiness, are done like the actions of an emanated person — without the pride that thinks "I have done such a service for others" and without the conceit of clinging to it as real. One should always hold this very mind of one's own, having brought it under the power of the antidote.

One should understand that the special practices of bodhisattvas that serve as favorable conditions for accomplishing others' welfare in this way must be relied upon as antidotes to the things to be abandoned explained above. Since these three verses beginning with "Utterly certain" (ཤིན་ཏུ་ངེས) were spoken by condensing the meaning of the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*'s extensive explanation of the antidotes for abandoning fruitless conduct, or for guarding oneself, they also show, for the sake of guarding oneself, the means of guarding others. In short, they show that one must possess those qualities of the antidotes as the means by which other beings do not become ill-disposed toward oneself, and one does not become ill-disposed toward others.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-57]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Whenever one enters upon any virtuous path of action, it too is "blameless" — for "blameless" (ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ) is the name for sin and non-virtue of the kind that cannot bear being spoken of (ཁ་ན་གླེང་མི་ཕོད་པ). So regarding all virtuous things, or kinds of things to be done, that are free of such: "having brought under the power of oneself and beings" means, brought under one's own power — relying on a lama, a spiritual friend, and listening to the Dharma, reflecting on its meaning, and properly practicing and accomplishing it, and so on; and again, brought under the power of other beings — teaching the Dharma, giving generously, and so on. Engaging in whichever such activities, and applying oneself to whichever of them, at that time everything is like an emanation — like a reflection in a mirror, or like the illusion conjured by a magician. By being without "I" and "mine," without grasping and clinging, one should always hold this mind, placing it on the correct virtuous object of focus — such is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-57]]

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Engage in Blameless Deeds for Self and Others, Yet Hold the Mind Free of Pride, Like an Emanation

This verse sets out that one must engage in blameless things (generosity and the like) under the power of the aims of oneself and other beings, and that even while doing so one must always hold the mind without pride and conceit, as if everything were an emanation.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Explains the Word "Blameless"

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains the word "blameless" (ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ): it is the name for sin and non-virtue of the kind that cannot bear being spoken of.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Illustrates the Emanation Simile with a Mirror Reflection and a Magician's Illusion

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains this example of "like an emanation" by means of a reflection in a mirror and the illusion conjured by a magician.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Relates Verses 5-55 to 5-57 to the Śikṣāsamuccaya on Guarding Oneself and Others

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the three verses beginning with "Utterly certain" (5-55 to 5-57) were spoken in correspondence with the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*'s extensive teaching on the antidotes for guarding oneself, and that this means of guarding oneself also shows, separately, the means of guarding others.

#### Divergences Among Commentators

- ⚑ **Two ways of explaining "having brought oneself and beings under one's power":** The root words བདག་དང་སེམས་ཅན་དབང་བྱས are explained in two different ways. One explains them as "for the sake of the aims of oneself and others" — engaging in blameless things with oneself and beings as the beneficiaries. The other explains them as "having brought it under the power of oneself, abiding in self-respect (ངོ་ཚ་ཤེས་པ), and under the power of beings, abiding in consideration for others (ཁྲེལ་ཡོད་པ)." Ngulchu Thogme gives both, the second as a quotation from the *Detailed Explanation* (རྣམ་བཤད); the interlinear gloss follows the second.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that when engaging in blameless virtuous actions for the sake of oneself and others, one should always hold the mind — through the wisdom that realizes, like an emanation, the absence of inherent nature — without pride and without grasping at a self.

**Key points.**

- Blameless activity means engaging, without grasping at "I" and "mine," in things such as, for oneself, listening to the Dharma and eating, and, for others, teaching the Dharma and giving generously.
- When engaging in virtuous action, as with an emanated doer, one should hold the mind without pride and conceit, together with the wisdom that realizes emptiness.
- Like a reflection in a mirror or an emanation of a magician, one should keep the mind continuously placed on the correct object of focus, without grasping at "I" and "mine."
- As in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, the verse shows the antidote to pointless conduct and the means of guarding oneself, and, relying on guarding oneself, it shows the means of guarding others as well.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Even at times of striving in such virtuous actions, which accomplish benefit and happiness for oneself and others, one should not give rise to pride or conceit. And at the end, one should recall: "Having obtained such a good body-support of leisure, this is a most excellent opportunity to accomplish great goals, both temporary and ultimate" — the way leisure and opportunity are of great meaning and hard to find. Not letting this support be wasted pointlessly, but wanting to make it meaningful, one must hold such a mind unmoving, like Mount Meru — such is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-58]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Having thought again and again, "This supreme leisure that was not found for a long time — this precious human body, extremely hard to find — which I have now found or obtained, must be made meaningful," one must hold the mind that meditates on the difficulty of finding leisure and opportunity, and the like, firmly, completely unmoved from the aim of however one meditates by bad friends and the like, just as Mount Meru, the king of mountains, is unmoved by any condition.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-58]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Thinking, "After a long time, with great effort, I have obtained supreme leisure," and having thought again and again that leisure and opportunity are of great meaning and hard to find, one should hold firmly — completely unmoving, like Mount Meru — the mind, of the kind explained before, that thinks, "By all means I will accomplish the great goal."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-58]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Having thought again and again of having obtained supreme leisure — hard to obtain — after a long time, one should hold this mind, of the kind explained before, completely unmoved by afflictions, like Mount Meru.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-107]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Having thought again and again that one must make meaningful this human body — which, after a long time in the three lower realms and so on, has become the supreme leisure through abandoning the eight states without freedom (མི་ཁོམ་པ་བརྒྱད) — one should hold firmly, completely unmoved by bad friends, by the suffering of ascetic hardship (དཀའ་སྤྱད་ཀྱི་སྡུག་བསྔལ), by hearing of the peace and bliss of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas, and the like, the mind of the kind taught before — utterly certain (ཤིན་ཏུ་ངེས་པ), utterly clear (རབ་ཏུ་དང་བ), and so on — just as Mount Meru is unmoved by conditions small, medium, or great; as the following passage says:

> From the *Ornament* (རྒྱན):
> Like Mount Meru, which is unmoved by dust, birds, and oceans,
> the steadfast are unmoved by bad friends, by suffering,
> and by hearing the profound.

*(Editorial note: ཕྱེ་མ་ལེབ is rendered "dust" tentatively, as in the curated day file, which records that the meaning of ལེབ is unclear; the rail's own teaching points gloss it as "dust particles." The rail's note calls this citation an Ālaṃkāra verse, and its teaching points identify it as the Sūtrālaṃkāra, མདོ་སྡེ་རྒྱན.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-58]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Having wandered in the states without freedom for a long time, one has only now — through the power of roots of virtue such as ethical discipline — not been born in those bad states of existence, and has obtained here the supreme leisure that is hard to find: how wondrous! Thinking again and again in this way about how leisure and opportunity are hard to find, and thinking now, "I will take the essence of this and make it of great meaning," in the manner of the many methods, explained above, for attaching the mind to virtuous objects of focus — for instance, just as the king of mountains, Mount Meru, remains firm, unmoved by any conditions, so one should bring one's own mind under control by holding it, through the power of the antidote, completely unmoved by the conditions of what is to be abandoned, the opposing side: afflictions, suffering, and so on. *(Editorial note: Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites Sabzang Mati Panchen's own commentary by name, noting that Sabzang draws the same Ālaṃkāra citation to illustrate Mount Meru unmoved by the "three conditions" — small, medium, and great.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-58]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

After a long time — from the previous lives of beginningless samsara until now — one has finally been freed from the eight states without freedom, the adverse conditions for practicing Dharma, and has obtained a supreme support of leisure for the body. Whether one considers cause, example, or number, this too is of a nature extremely hard to find. Thinking this again and again, if one places the mind as described above, it is without any movement, like the king of mountains; if one releases it, it goes to all virtuous objects of focus. In short, like Mount Meru, the king of mountains, unmoved by any condition, great, medium, or small, one should hold the mind firm and steadfast, completely unmoved by bad friends or by afflictions and conceptual thoughts — such is the meaning.

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Reflect Again and Again on the Rarity of Leisure and Opportunity, and Hold the Mind Firm Like Mount Meru

This verse sets out that, having thought again and again about how hard it is, after a long time, to obtain the body-support of leisure and opportunity, one must hold the mind firm like Mount Meru, unmoved by conditions such as bad friends and afflictions.

###### 2. Sabzang Mati Panchen Cites a Verse from the Ornament: Meru Is Unmoved by Dust, Birds, and Oceans

By one verse cited from the *Ornament* (the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*, མདོ་སྡེ་རྒྱན), Sabzang Mati Panchen shows that just as Mount Meru is not moved by dust particles (ཕྱེ་མ་ལེབ), birds, or oceans, one is not moved by bad friends, by suffering, or by hearing of the profound.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam, Citing Sabzang by Name, Explains the Three Conditions as Small, Medium, and Great

Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing Sabzang Mati Panchen's commentary by name, explains that just as Mount Meru is unmoved by the three conditions — small, medium, and great — so one is unmoved by the three kinds of conditions such as bad friends.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Shows the Rarity of Leisure and Opportunity by Cause, Example, and Number

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk shows that, if one considers the cause, the example, and the number, the body-support of leisure obtained after being freed from the eight states without freedom is extremely hard to find.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that, having reflected again and again on having obtained — after a long time, and after being freed from the states without freedom — the supreme body of leisure and opportunity that is so hard to find, one must hold one's mind firm like Mount Meru, the king of mountains, unmoved by afflictions and adverse conditions.

**Key points.**

- Having wandered long in the states without freedom and finally obtained a good support of leisure and opportunity, one reflects again and again that one must accomplish great goals and make what has been obtained meaningful.
- One holds the mind firm like Mount Meru, unmoved by the wind of conceptual thoughts such as desire and by the conditions of the afflictions.
- Unmoved by any condition — small, medium, or great — such as bad friends, the suffering of ascetic hardship, and hearing of the peace of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas; this is explained in line with the passage from the *Ornament of the Sūtras* (མདོ་སྡེའི་རྒྱན).
- Reflecting on the difficulty of finding leisure and opportunity by way of cause, example, and number: if the mind is set down, it is unmoving like Mount Meru; if released, it can reach any virtuous object of focus.
- Even while striving in virtue for the sake of oneself and others, one does not give rise to pride and conceit, and one must take the essence from the support that accomplishes great goals.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, because one root condition of the obstacles that arise to practicing the Dharma comes about through attachment to the body, meditating on the faults of the body is of great importance. That is to say, however dearly one holds this body, in the end, once it has become a corpse — separated from consciousness — even if vultures attached to flesh drag it back and forth everywhere and carry it off to other places, one is not displeased at that time, for it is by nature something with nothing to be attached to or cling to. So why now, with such tight clinging, should one fuss over the body? It is not fitting.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-59]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In this way the mind pampers the body: when hungry it gives it food, when thirsty it gives it drink, and even if someone finds fault with no more than its eyes and nose (མིག་སྣ་ཙམ), it answers back in each case. The thought of protecting this body — that conceptual thought which is to be abandoned — is here vigorously argued against by the antidotal conceptual thought that cuts off cherishing the body, as follows: vultures, jackals, and the like, attached and clinging to flesh, drag and carry off the intestines and so on back and forth everywhere and eat them; yet if at that time you, mind, are not displeased at those vultures and so on, then why now do you cling to this body and fuss over it — in order to benefit it with food, clothing, and the like, and to protect it from harm? It is not right to do so.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

When this very body, separated from the mind, is taken to the charnel ground, vultures attached to flesh drag it back and forth everywhere and eat it; yet if you, mind, are not displeased at that time, then why now do you cling to this body and fuss over it for the sake of protecting it? <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

A general statement of the reason it is not right to guard the body out of attachment: above it was said that the mind is to be held firm, and one might think, "This body too is hard to find, so one should hold it dear and protect it." That is not right, and here is why. Now, out of attachment, to take the body as "mine" and fuss over it in order to guard it, is not right. When one has died, and the aggregate bereft of mind is taken to the charnel ground and discarded, and many vultures attached to the flesh of their food drag the limbs, arms, legs, intestines, stomach, and so on hither and thither — tearing and pulling — and eat them, if you, mind, are not displeased then, what reason is there now to cling and be attached to the body, hold it dear, and fuss or take pains to guard it? It is not right — such is the meaning.

Also, there is the reading "If you, body, are not displeased" (ལུས་ཁྱོད་མི་དགར་མི་བྱེད་ན): if the body itself has no power to be displeased at that time, then why now do you, mind, fuss over the body? It is pointless — such is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-59]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

To cherish and cling intensely to this body, and to nourish it with whatever wealth and goods one needs, is not fitting. For when vultures attached to human flesh and blood drag it about on the ground back and forth everywhere at the time of death, and, tearing at it greedily, scatter it, eat it, and drag it about — even at that time you, the mind (ཡིད་དམ་སེམས) of self-grasping, feel no displeasure, do not take its side, and do not protect it. Then why now, while alive, do you fuss over this heap of flesh and blood — covering it with "poison-skin" (དུག་སློག, sense uncertain), praising it with food, bathing and anointing it, and so on? It is not fitting to do so — such is the meaning.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: The Vulture Example Shows Why It Is Not Fitting to Cling to the Body Now

This verse sets out, through the example of vultures eating the flesh at death, the reason it is not fitting to cling to the body and fuss over it: if the mind is not displeased then, there is no reason to cherish the body now.

###### 2. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Names Attachment to the Body as the Root of Obstacles to Practice

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that attachment to the body is the single root of the obstacles to practicing the Dharma, and sets out separately the importance of meditating on the faults of the body.

###### 3. ⚑ Minyak Kunzang Sonam Gives Two Readings of the Third Line: "You, Mind" and "You, Body"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the third line has two ways of reading: "you, mind…" (the rebuke addressed to the mind) and "you, body…" (the rebuke addressed to the body). On the first reading, the line shows that it is not fitting for the mind to cling to the body; on the second, that the body itself is unable to be displeased.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that if the mind is not displeased when, at death, vultures and the like carry off and eat the body's flesh and entrails, then it is not fitting now, while alive, to cling to this body, hold it dear, and fuss over it.

**Key points.**

- The root condition of obstacles to practicing the Dharma is attachment to the body, and so the faults of the body must be meditated on.
- While alive, to fuss over and guard the body — giving it food when hungry, drink when thirsty, anointing it, and giving it tasty food — is not fitting.
- After death, even when vultures carry off the flesh and entrails in the charnel ground and eat them, the self-grasping mind does not become displeased or prevent it.
