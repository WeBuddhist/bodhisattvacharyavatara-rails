## Today's Verse

> In this and in my other lives Throughout beginningless samsara, Deluded, I have done misdeeds. I’ve instigated them, and also— ^2-28

> Compelled by ignorant confusion— I have rejoiced in them. I see They were mistakes, and I confess To the protectors from my depths. ^2-29

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 28 and 29 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. Throughout our lives, knowingly and unknowingly, we gather faults in three ways: wrongdoing we commit ourselves, wrongdoing we get others to do, and wrongdoing we are quietly pleased about when others do it. These verses do not let us make excuses for any of the three. They give us the courage to name our mistakes honestly and to confess them from the heart rather than with the mouth alone.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

Khenpo Kunzang Palden and Minyak Kunsö both mark this verse as the beginning of something structured. Confession is not one gesture; it works through four powers, and verse 28 opens the first of them, the power of regret. The other three follow later: relying on the buddhas, applying the antidotes, and holding back from doing it again. So what looks like an outpouring of guilt is actually the first step of a method.

Regret needs an honest inventory, and Shantideva gives one. Across beginningless samsara he did misdeeds himself, he got others to do them, and he was pleased when others did them. Kunzang Palden makes the point that would not occur to us: there is no difference in weight between the three. Being glad about someone else's wrongdoing carries the same fault as doing it. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk fills in what is actually being confessed across the three doors, and it is ordinary: killing with the body, lying with the speech, wanting what belongs to someone else with the mind.

Both teachers then explain why we do any of this. Not cruelty, but not knowing. Kunga Wangchuk says plainly that we fail to recognize a misdeed as a misdeed and do not see that there is any fault in it. Minyak Kunsö goes further: on the question of what to take up and what to drop, we are as confused as an animal is, following whatever pleases us now and never looking at where it leads.

Which sets up the two conditions the commentaries insist on. Kunzang Palden and Kunga Wangchuk say the fault must be **seen as a fault first** — verse 29's "I see they were mistakes" comes before the confessing, not after. And Kunzang Palden and Gyaltsab add that the confession is not finished when the words end: the undertaking not to do it again is part of it. Regret without that is just feeling bad.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, when I see someone being criticized or harmed, I will not let myself feel pleased. I will recognize what is happening as a mistake.

**Explanation:** _(Avoiding wrongdoing)_ Most of us do not commit serious harm ourselves. But when someone else does something bad, or when a person we dislike meets defeat or trouble, a quiet satisfaction rises in us that we never admit to. Today's verses say that being glad about another person's wrongdoing carries the same fault as doing it ourselves. So today I will watch for that quiet satisfaction and set it down. When I hear that someone has failed or been caught out, I will name it inwardly as a mistake rather than as good news. Giving up this one habit closes a door through which we gather harm without ever noticing.
