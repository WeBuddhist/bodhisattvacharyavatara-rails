## Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva (One Who Works to Free All Beings) ^I-0

In Sanskrit: Bodhisatvacarya avatara ^I-1

In Tibetan: Byang chub sems dpa'i spyod pa la 'jug pa ^I-2

I bow with respect to all enlightened beings and all those working toward enlightenment to help others. ^I-3

## 1. The Benefits of the Wish to Help All Beings ^1-0

I bow with respect to the buddhas, who embody perfect truth, and to their spiritual children, and to all who deserve honor. Following the scriptures, I will briefly teach the way to take up the bodhisattva's commitment to help all beings. ^1-1

I won't say anything that hasn't been said before, and I have no skill as a poet. So I haven't written this for the sake of others - I've written it to train my own mind. ^1-2

Still, writing it will strengthen my faith and help me grow in goodness for now. And if someone else with a similar outlook happens to see it, then it may be worthwhile for them too. ^1-3

I have gained something extremely hard to find - the freedom and opportunity to truly benefit others. If I don't make good use of this chance now, how will I ever find it again? ^1-4

Just as lightning briefly lights up a pitch-black, cloudy night sky, so too, through the buddhas' power, good thoughts about helping the world rarely and briefly arise in people's minds. ^1-5

This is why goodness is always so weak, while the power of wrongdoing is terribly strong. Apart from the wish to reach full enlightenment for the sake of all, what other kind of goodness can overcome it? ^1-6

The great sages, who reflected deeply over countless ages, saw that only this wish truly helps. Through it, countless beings can easily reach the highest happiness. ^1-7

No one who wants to end the unhappiness of all beings, overcome the many miseries of existence, and share in life's many joys should ever give up this wish to help everyone. ^1-8

The moment this wish arises, even someone trapped and suffering in the endless cycle of rebirth is called a child of the buddhas, and is honored by gods and humans alike. ^1-9

Like the finest alchemy, it takes this impure body and turns it into something priceless - the body of a buddha. So hold firmly onto this wish to help all beings. ^1-10

The one guide of all beings, with his boundless wisdom, examined it closely and found it of immense worth. So all of you who wish to leave behind the realms of suffering, hold firmly onto this precious wish. ^1-11

Every other kind of goodness is like a banana tree - it bears fruit once and is then used up. But the tree of this wish to help all beings bears fruit endlessly, growing richer without end. ^1-12

Just as someone in great danger relies on a hero to save them, why wouldn't a careful person rely on this, which frees them instantly, even from terrible wrongs they've done? ^1-13

Like the fire at the end of an age, it burns away great wrongdoing in a single instant. The wise guardian Maitreya explained its countless benefits to the young seeker Sudhana. ^1-14

In short, this wish comes in two forms: the wish that longs for full awakening, and the wish that actively engages in reaching it. ^1-15

Just as one can tell the difference between wanting to go somewhere and actually going there, the wise understand the difference between these two respectively. ^1-16

Even while still caught in the cycle of rebirth, the longing wish brings great results. But unlike the engaged wish, its good effects don't continue without a break. ^1-17

But from the very moment you truly take up this resolve - firmly, without turning back - in order to free beings across countless realms, ^1-18

from that moment on, endless streams of goodness flow forth without pause, vast as the sky itself, even while you sleep or aren't paying attention. ^1-19

The Buddha himself explained this, and the reasons for it, for the sake of those inclined toward a more basic path, in the discourse requested by Subahu. ^1-20

If someone who simply wishes, out of kindness, to relieve another person's headache holds boundless goodness in doing so, ^1-21

what need is there to mention someone who wishes to free every single being from countless troubles, and to give each one boundless good qualities? ^1-22

Who else has ever had such caring concern as this? Not even a father or mother. Do the gods have it? Do the great sages? Do even the highest divine beings have it? ^1-23

If beings have never had such a wish before, even for their own sake, even in their dreams, how could they possibly have it for the sake of someone else? ^1-24

This wish to help others - a wish that even they themselves have never held for their own sake - this rare and precious state of mind arises as a wonder never seen before. ^1-25

It is the source of joy for every being, and the cure for their suffering. How could anyone ever measure the goodness of this precious mind? ^1-26

If simply intending to benefit others is greater than honoring the Buddha himself, how much more true is this for someone who actually strives for the happiness of all beings? ^1-27

Beings want to escape suffering, yet they run straight toward it. They long for happiness, but out of confusion, they destroy their own well-being as if it were an enemy. ^1-28

For those who lack happiness and suffer in so many ways, this wish provides every kind of joy and cuts through every kind of suffering. ^1-29

It also clears away confusion about how things really are. Could there be any goodness greater than this? Could there be any friend better than this? Could there be any merit greater than this? ^1-30

If it's praiseworthy to repay a kindness done for you, even briefly, how much more so for the bodhisattvas, who do good for others without even being asked? ^1-31

People praise as generous those who give a few people an occasional meal - even grudgingly, and only plain food enough to last half a day. ^1-32

What need is there to mention those who, for a very long time, constantly give countless beings the sublime happiness of the buddhas, fulfilling every one of their wishes? ^1-33

Anyone who has an unkind thought toward such a giver - a child of the buddhas - will, the Buddha said, remain in a hellish state for as many ages as they had bad thoughts. ^1-34

But the good results that come from feeling faith toward them grow far more abundantly. Even in difficult times, being around bodhisattvas doesn't lead to wrongdoing - it naturally produces more goodness. ^1-35

I bow to the very bodies of those in whom this precious attitude has been born. Even harming them somehow leads toward happiness. I go for protection and guidance to these wellsprings of joy. ^1-36

## The first chapter, "The Benefits of the Wish to Help All Beings," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 2. Admitting My Wrongdoings ^2-0

In order to hold onto that precious wish to help everyone, I make the finest offerings to the buddhas, to the true teachings - that flawless jewel - and to the bodhisattvas, whose good qualities are as vast as oceans. ^2-1

Whatever fruits and flowers there are, whatever kinds of medicine exist, however many jewels there are in the world, and all the clean, pleasant waters there may be, ^2-2

mountains of jewels, peaceful forest groves in solitary and delightful places, bushes decorated with beautiful flowers, and trees whose branches bend low with wonderful fruit, ^2-3

incense and perfumes like those from the heavenly realms, and wish-fulfilling trees, jeweled trees, and crops that grow without needing to be planted - all ornaments fit to be offered, ^2-4

lakes and pools covered with lotus flowers, where lovely swans call out sweetly - everything unclaimed by anyone, all the way to the edges of infinite space - ^2-5

I picture taking all of this and offering it fully to the great sages and their spiritual children, these sublime and deeply compassionate beings. Please think of me kindly and accept these offerings from me. ^2-6

I have no store of goodness, I am truly poor, and I have nothing else of worth to offer. May the guardians, who care for the well-being of others, accept these gifts for my sake through their own power. ^2-7

From now on, I offer my whole body and being to the buddhas and their spiritual children. Sublime beings, please accept me completely - I will be your devoted servant. ^2-8

Because you have accepted me completely, I will no longer fear this life, and I will work to benefit all beings. I will rise above my past wrongdoings and never do wrong again. ^2-9

In this sweetly scented bathing house, where the bright, gleaming floor is paved with crystal, where lovely pillars shine with jewels, and glistening pearls hang from the canopy above, ^2-10

I bathe the buddhas and bodhisattvas, pouring water scented with many fragrances from precious vases, while music and song fill the air. ^2-11

With cloths beyond compare, clean and delicately scented, I dry their bodies. Then I offer them the finest robes, beautifully dyed and delightfully perfumed. ^2-12

I drape Samantabhadra, Lokeshvara, Manjushri, and the other noble bodhisattvas in soft, delicate fabrics and hundreds of the finest ornaments. ^2-13

Like polishing the purest gold, I apply the finest fragrances - scents that spread through billions of worlds - onto the radiant bodies of all the buddhas. ^2-14

To these great beings I honor, these foremost sages, I offer every fragrant flower - mandarava blossoms, lotuses, jasmine, and more - along with beautifully arranged garlands. ^2-15

I also offer billowing clouds of incense filled with the sweetest, most enchanting scents, along with royal feasts of every kind of food and drink. ^2-16

I offer jeweled lamps arranged in rows atop golden lotuses, and I scatter beautiful flower petals over a floor anointed with perfume. ^2-17

To these compassionate beings, I offer countless palaces that adorn the sky, glowing beautifully with strings of pearls and jewels, filled with the sound of melodious songs of praise. ^2-18

I continually offer to the great sages beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles, finely shaped and pleasing to look at, their edges trimmed with lovely ornaments. ^2-19

And may there also arise endless clouds of beautiful offerings, filled with lovely music and harmony, that soothe the suffering of every being. ^2-20

May showers of jewels, flowers, and more fall continuously upon every treasure of the true teachings, as well as upon shrines and sacred images. ^2-21

Just as Manjughosha and the other great bodhisattvas made offerings to the buddhas, I too offer these things to the buddhas - the protectors - and their spiritual children. ^2-22

I praise these beings, whose good qualities are as vast as oceans, with oceans of praise and beautiful words. May clouds of songs praising them arise continuously like this. ^2-23

I bow down, prostrating with as many bodies as there are specks of dust in the universe, to all the buddhas of past, present, and future, to their teachings, and to the sublime spiritual community. ^2-24

I bow to the sacred places and shrines of the bodhisattva. I also bow to the senior teachers, the masters, and the greatest practitioners. ^2-25

Until I reach the very essence of enlightenment, I turn to the buddhas for guidance and protection. I also turn to their teachings, and to the community of bodhisattvas, for guidance and protection. ^2-26

To the perfect buddhas and bodhisattvas who dwell in every direction, filled with great compassion, I join my palms together and make this request: ^2-27

In this life and in all my other lives throughout this beginningless cycle of rebirth, confused as I've been, I have done wrong. I have caused others to do wrong, and - ^2-28

driven by ignorance and confusion - I have even taken pleasure in wrongdoing. Now I see these were mistakes, and I confess them from the depths of my heart to the guardians. ^2-29

Because of my inner afflictions - my anger, greed, and confusion - I have caused harm through my body, speech, and mind to the Three Jewels, to my parents, to my teachers, and to others. ^2-30

I, who am flawed and stained by so many failings, have committed terrible wrongs. Before these guides, I confess all of them. ^2-31

I may die quickly, before I've cleansed myself of these wrongs. How can I be freed from them? I beg you, please save me. ^2-32

Death is untrustworthy - it won't wait for things to be finished or unfinished. So no one, whether healthy or sick, should place their trust in this fleeting life. ^2-33

I must leave everything behind and go, yet I still haven't truly understood this. For the sake of those I like or dislike, I have done all kinds of wrong. ^2-34

Those I dislike will cease to exist. Those I love will cease to exist. I myself will cease to exist. Everyone, in the end, will cease to exist. ^2-35

Everything I have ever experienced will become nothing but a memory, like something recalled from a dream. I will never again see what has already passed. ^2-36

Even in this one lifetime, many people I've loved or disliked have already passed away. Yet the terrible wrongs I did for their sake still remain with me. ^2-37

Because I failed to recognize that I too am impermanent, I have done many wrongs out of confusion, greed, and hatred. ^2-38

Without pausing even a single day or night, this life is constantly running out, with no way to add more time to it. So why wouldn't someone like me eventually die? ^2-39

While I lie in my bed, surrounded by all my family, I alone will feel the sensation of my life being cut off. ^2-40

When death's messengers come to seize me, what good will friends do? What good will family do? Only goodness I have built up can protect me then - and sadly, I haven't built up much. ^2-41

Guardians, I have carelessly committed countless wrongs for the sake of this brief, passing life, never realizing such danger existed. ^2-42

If people being led away today to have their limbs cut off already look different - mouths parched, eyes bloodshot with fear - ^2-43

how much more wretched will I be when death's agents seize me, their faces terrifying, and I am gripped by unbearable pain? ^2-44

"Who will truly protect me from this terrifying danger?" I will cry out, eyes wide with fear, searching in every direction for somewhere safe. ^2-45

But finding no protection anywhere, I will fall into despair. If there is no safety to be found there, what will I do at such a moment? ^2-46

So from today onward, I turn for refuge to the buddhas, the guardians of all beings, who work to protect every wandering being, who have great power to dispel all fear. ^2-47

In the same way, I truly turn for refuge to the truth they have realized, which dispels the terrors of endless rebirth, and to the community of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Terrified, I give myself completely into the care of Samantabhadra. I also offer my own body to Manjughosha. ^2-49

I cry out in distress to the guardian Avalokiteshvara, whose acts of compassion never fail. I beg: please protect me, who have done such wrong. ^2-50

To noble Akashagarbha, to Kshitigarbha, and to every one of these deeply compassionate guardians, I cry out from my heart in search of protection. ^2-51

I turn for refuge to Vajrapani, at the very sight of whom harmful beings - like death's own agents - flee in every direction out of fear. ^2-52

In the past I have gone against your guidance, but now that I see the great danger clearly, I turn to you for refuge and plead: quickly remove this fear from me. ^2-53

If people must follow a doctor's advice out of fear of an ordinary illness, how much more so when constantly infected by the hundred illnesses of craving and the like? ^2-54

If even one of these afflictions could destroy everyone in the world, and no other medicine to cure it could be found anywhere, ^2-55

then it would be foolish and deeply confused to ignore the guidance of the all-knowing healer who can remove every one of these afflictions. ^2-56

If I must be careful even around a small, ordinary cliff edge, how much more careful should I be around the endless fall of a thousand miles that awaits me? ^2-57

It isn't right to sit back comfortably and say, "I won't die today." The time when I cease to exist is unavoidable - it will surely come. ^2-58

Who will free me from this fear? How will I ever escape it? I will certainly cease to exist one day. How can my mind possibly stay at ease? ^2-59

My past experiences are gone, and what do I have left from them now? Yet out of my attachment to them, I have gone against the guidance of my teachers. ^2-60

If I must leave this life behind, along with my family and friends, and go on alone to somewhere else, what good will all those I liked or disliked have done me? ^2-61

It would be right to think, day and night, only this: "Wrongdoing leads to suffering. How can I free myself from it?" ^2-62

Whatever wrongs I have done out of ignorance and confusion - whether they were naturally harmful or against my own better judgment - ^2-63

in the presence of the guardians, out of fear of the suffering that follows, I join my palms and bow again and again, confessing every one of them. ^2-64

I ask you, guides, to accept that my wrongdoings were mistakes. They were not good, and for that reason, I will never do such things again. ^2-65

## The second chapter, "Admitting My Wrongdoings," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 3. Taking Up the Wish to Help All Beings ^3-0

With joy, I celebrate the goodness that brings relief from the pain of the lower realms, and the happiness of those who suffer but now dwell in well-being. ^3-1

I celebrate all the goodness that leads toward enlightenment. ^3-2

I celebrate beings' freedom from the suffering of endless rebirth, the buddhahood of the guardians, and the bodhisattvas' stages of realization. ^3-3

I joyfully celebrate the vast, ocean-like wish to help everyone, which brings all beings to happiness and benefits them. ^3-4

With my palms joined, I ask the buddhas in every direction: please light the lamp of your teachings in the darkness of confused beings' suffering! ^3-5

With my palms joined, I ask the buddhas who wish to pass into final peace: please don't leave these wandering beings in the dark - stay with us for countless ages! ^3-6

May the goodness I have gathered by doing all of this remove all the suffering of every single being. ^3-7

For as long as beings are sick, until their illnesses are cured, may I be their medicine, their doctor, and their caregiver. ^3-8

May showers of food and drink relieve the pain of hunger and thirst. In times of terrible famine, may I myself become food and drink for those in need. ^3-9

May I be an endless treasure for those who are poor and lacking, and may I remain close by them as whatever they need and want. ^3-10

To benefit all beings, I give without hesitation my body, my possessions, and all the goodness I have built up, past, present, and future. ^3-11

Final peace means letting go of everything, and that is what I wish to attain. Since everything must be given up eventually, it is best to give it all to benefit others now. ^3-12

Just as I have given this body to living beings for their use, may they always do with it as they wish - even if they choose to harm it, insult it, or strike it. ^3-13

Even if they treat my body as a joke, mock it, or laugh at it, I have already given it to them - why would I cling to it now? ^3-14

So let them do whatever they wish with it, so long as it brings them no harm. May thinking of me never be pointless for anyone. ^3-15

Whether someone who thinks of me feels anger or trust, may that response become the very cause that fulfills everything good for them. ^3-16

May anyone who speaks badly of me, anyone who harms me, and anyone who criticizes me all have the good fortune to reach enlightenment. ^3-17

May I be a protector for those with no one to defend them, a guide for travelers, and a boat, a ship, or a bridge for those who need to cross over. ^3-18

May I be solid ground for those seeking ground, a bed for those without one, and for all beings who need help, may I become their willing servant. ^3-19

May I be a wish-fulfilling jewel, a vase that grants every need, a powerful remedy, the finest medicine, a tree that grants heavenly wishes, and a bountiful cow that provides for all beings. ^3-20

Like the earth and the other basic elements, and like open space itself, may I always be the ground that supports the lives of beings in every way. ^3-21

In this same way, for all beings without exception, to the very edges of space, may I be their source of support and sustenance until every one of them reaches final peace. ^3-22

Just as the buddhas of the past first gave rise to the wish to help all beings, and just as they trained step by step in the bodhisattva path, ^3-23

so too will I, for the benefit of all wandering beings, give rise to this same wish to help everyone, and I too will train step by step in the bodhisattva path. ^3-24

Once a wise person has sincerely taken up this wish to help all beings in this way, they take joy in their resolve so that it continues to grow ever stronger. ^3-25

My life has now become truly worthwhile. I have made good use of this precious human existence. Today I have been born into the family of the buddhas, and I have become their child. ^3-26

From now on, I will always act in ways that honor this family, so that I never bring shame upon this pure and venerable lineage. ^3-27

Like a blind man who happens to find a precious jewel buried in a heap of garbage, this wish to help all beings has, by some rare chance, arisen within me. ^3-28

This is the great elixir that overcomes death itself. It is the endless treasure that ends the poverty of every being. ^3-29

This is the finest medicine, curing the sicknesses of the world - a resting tree for beings who are worn out from wandering the many paths of existence. ^3-30

This is the universal bridge that frees all beings from the lower realms, the rising moon of the mind that eases the torment of every being's inner afflictions. ^3-31

It is the great sun that dispels the darkness of beings' confusion. It is the purest essence, drawn out like butter churned from the milk of the true teachings. ^3-32

For all travelers wandering the paths of existence, longing to taste happiness, this sublime joy stands close at hand, ready to satisfy every one of these honored guests - all sentient beings. ^3-33

## The third chapter, "Taking Up the Wish to Help All Beings," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 4. Teachings on Carefulness ^4-0

The buddhas' children who have firmly taken up this wish to help all beings must strive without any laziness never to break their commitments. ^4-1

When something has been started hastily, or wasn't properly thought through, it's best to consider carefully whether to go ahead with it - even if a promise was already made. ^4-2

But why would I delay something that the buddhas and their spiritual children have examined with great wisdom, and that I myself have also carefully considered? ^4-3

If, having made this promise, I now fail to carry it out through my actions, I will have let down every being who was counting on me - so what will become of me? ^4-4

It is said that someone who thinks of giving even a small, trivial thing, but then doesn't follow through, will be reborn as a hungry, suffering spirit. ^4-5

Having invited all beings from my heart to share in the highest happiness, if I then let them all down, will I really reach the higher realms? ^4-6

How it is that people who abandon their wish to help all beings can still eventually be freed is a workings of cause and effect so subtle that only a fully enlightened being can understand it. ^4-7

For a bodhisattva, breaking this commitment is the most serious failure of all. When it happens, their ability to benefit all beings is badly weakened. ^4-8

Anyone else who blocks even a moment of this goodness will be reborn endlessly in the lower realms, since they have diminished the well-being of all beings. ^4-9

If someone who ruins the happiness of even a single being brings ruin on themselves, how much more true is this for someone who ruins the well-being of countless beings throughout all of space? ^4-10

If I keep swinging back and forth between serious failures and a strong wish to help everyone, mixing the two together through repeated rebirths, it will take a very long time to reach the higher stages of the path. ^4-11

So I will dedicate myself fully to carrying out what I have promised. If I make no effort from now on, I will keep falling from one lower state to another. ^4-12

Even though countless buddhas have appeared and benefited every being, because of my own past wrongs, I was never among those who received their help. ^4-13

If I keep acting this way, I will continue, again and again, to experience sickness, imprisonment, mutilation, and worse in the lower realms. ^4-14

It is rare for a buddha to appear, rare to gain genuine faith, a human life, and the ability to cultivate goodness. When will I ever have all of this again? ^4-15

Although today I may be healthy, well fed, and safe, life can betray me in an instant - this body is only borrowed for a short while. ^4-16

If I keep behaving this way, I will never gain a human life again. And without a human life, there is only wrongdoing ahead, never goodness. ^4-17

If I fail to act with goodness despite having this good fortune now, what will I do once I'm overwhelmed by the suffering of the lower realms? ^4-18

If I do no good and instead pile up wrongdoing, I won't even hear the words "the higher realms" for billions of ages to come. ^4-19

This is why the Buddha taught that gaining a human life is as rare as a sea turtle happening to poke its neck through a wooden yoke floating on a vast ocean. ^4-20

If a single moment of wrongdoing can lead to ages spent in the deepest hell of endless suffering, how could the wrongs I've done throughout this beginningless cycle of rebirth possibly lead me to the higher realms? ^4-21

And after I've endured all of that, I still won't be free of it, because while I'm experiencing that suffering, I'll likely create even more wrongdoing. ^4-22

Now that I have gained such a rare opportunity as this, if I still don't cultivate goodness, there is no greater self-deception, and no greater foolishness, than this. ^4-23

And if, even after realizing this, I remain confused and discouraged, then when the time comes for me to die, I will feel overwhelming grief. ^4-24

When the unbearable flames of hell have burned my body for a very long time, there is no doubt that a fire of terrible regret will also burn through my mind. ^4-25

By sheer good fortune, I have gained this rare opportunity to do good. If, even while fully aware, I let myself be led back into those hells again, ^4-26

it would be as if I've been placed under some spell, with no will of my own. I don't even know what has confused me so - what is really going on inside me? ^4-27

Aversion, craving, and all the rest - these enemies of mine - have no limbs, no weapons, no courage, and no wisdom, and yet they've somehow made me their slave. ^4-28

As long as they live in my mind, they will keep harming me however they please. It's wrong to simply accept this without resistance - staying passive here would be a mistake. ^4-29

Even if every god and demigod rose up against me as enemies, they could not force me into the fires of the deepest hell. ^4-30

But this powerful enemy - my own negative emotions - can throw me there in an instant, into a fire so intense it could reduce even the greatest mountain to ash without a trace. ^4-31

These adversaries, my negative emotions, have lasted without any real beginning or end. No other enemy could ever endure as long as they have. ^4-32

Everyone else, when treated kindly, tends to become helpful and pleasant in return. But my negative emotions, no matter how well I treat them, only cause me more suffering. ^4-33

If these long-standing, constant enemies - the sole source of every kind of harm - continue to live within my heart, how can I possibly be safe or happy anywhere in this cycle of rebirth? ^4-34

If the very jailers of this endless cycle - like the cruel executioners of the hells - live on within the grip of greed in my own mind, how can there be any happiness for me at all? ^4-35

So until I see clearly that I have truly defeated this enemy, I will not ease up in my efforts. Just as someone consumed by pride, once angered, cannot rest until they've dealt with even a minor insult, ^4-36

just as those driven by aggression, facing death, charge fearlessly into battle wanting only to win - ignoring the pain of being struck by spears and arrows, refusing to retreat until they succeed - ^4-37

how much more should I, striving to conquer my true, natural enemies, which are the actual cause of every suffering, refuse to be discouraged or intimidated by the countless small causes of misery I meet along the way. ^4-38

If someone wounded pointlessly by an enemy proudly shows off their scars as if they were decorations, then how could hardship possibly harm me when I'm striving toward something truly worthwhile? ^4-39

If fishermen, outcasts, farmers, and others endure the pain of heat, cold, and hardship just to make a living, why shouldn't I endure the same for the sake of helping all beings? ^4-40

When I vowed to free every being throughout all of space from their negative emotions, I myself was, at that time, still not free of them. ^4-41

Wasn't it senseless to make such a promise without even knowing my own limits? So I will never turn back from the task of overcoming these negative emotions. ^4-42

I will hold onto this resolve firmly and go into battle bearing a real grudge - but only against the kind of negative emotion that stands in the way of finally overcoming negative emotions altogether. ^4-43

It would be better to be burned alive or beheaded than to ever bow down before my true enemy - my own negative emotions. ^4-44

Ordinary enemies, when driven out, simply settle elsewhere and rebuild their strength before returning. But the enemy of negative emotion is not like that at all. ^4-45

Once these troublesome negative emotions are driven out of my mind by the sharp eye of wisdom, where could they possibly go? Where could they hide, waiting to return and harm me again? The only real problem is that I, weak-minded as I am, haven't made the effort to keep them out. ^4-46

These negative emotions don't live in outer objects, in the senses, or anywhere in between, nor do they live somewhere else entirely. So where exactly do they live, that they can harm all beings this way? They are like illusions - nothing more. So let go of your fear, and put in the effort needed to gain wisdom. Why suffer needlessly in hell for no good reason? ^4-47

Having thought this through, I will now strive to take up these practices as described. After all, how can a sick person be cured if they ignore what the doctor tells them? ^4-48

## The fourth chapter, "Teachings on Carefulness," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 5. Guarding Awareness ^5-0

All those who wish to keep their commitments should focus and guard their minds closely. If you don't protect your mind, you won't be able to keep any commitment at all. ^5-1

A wild elephant running loose doesn't cause nearly as much harm in this world as letting the "elephant" of an untamed mind run wild - which can lead all the way to the hell of endless suffering. ^5-2

But when the elephant of the mind is tied securely with the rope of mindfulness, every danger comes to nothing, and every kind of goodness falls naturally into your hands. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, and every kind of enemy, the guardians of hell, evil spirits, and demons - ^5-4

all of these are bound simply by binding this mind alone. All of these are tamed simply by taming this mind alone. ^5-5

The one who spoke the truth has taught that everything we fear, and every kind of suffering beyond measure, comes from the mind. ^5-6

Who specifically crafted the weapons used in the hells? Who created the ground of burning iron? Where did the tormenting lovers found in hell come from? ^5-7

The wise teacher has said that all of these arise from harmful thoughts. So in all of existence, there is truly no danger other than the mind itself. ^5-8

If simply ending poverty in the world were what made generosity perfect, then since there are still poor people today, how could the guardians of the past ever have perfected it? ^5-9

Perfect generosity is taught to be the wish to give away all your possessions - along with the good results of doing so - for the benefit of everyone. And that wish is a matter of the mind itself. ^5-10

Where, after all, could fish and other creatures be sent so that they would never be killed? Perfect discipline is taught to come from gaining the sincere intention to abstain from harm. ^5-11

How could anyone ever defeat hostile beings, who are as endless as space itself? But overcoming this one angry mind is the same as defeating every one of those enemies. ^5-12

Where could enough leather ever be found to cover the whole surface of the earth? Yet with just the leather covering the soles of my feet, it is as if the entire earth were covered. ^5-13

In the same way, although I can't hold back every external threat, I can hold this mind of mine in check - so why bother trying to control everything else? ^5-14

Even careful conduct with body and speech, if done half-heartedly, cannot match the result of rousing just one clear, wholehearted good thought - which can lead to rebirth in the very highest heavenly realms. ^5-15

No matter how long they're practiced, the Buddha - who understands reality as it truly is - taught that all rituals and hardships are pointless if the mind is distracted elsewhere. ^5-16

Those who don't understand this secret of the mind - the most sublime of all teachings - want to find joy and end suffering, yet they simply wander on and on, achieving nothing. ^5-17

So I will take hold of this mind of mine and protect it well. Setting aside this discipline of guarding the mind, what use would any other discipline be? ^5-18

Just as someone in a rough, jostling crowd carefully protects an open wound, so too, when surrounded by people acting harmfully, one should always guard the tender wound that is the mind. ^5-19

If you're careful with a physical wound out of fear of even slight pain, why not protect the wound of the mind out of fear of being crushed under the weight of hell? ^5-20

Living with this kind of conduct, a steadfast person who keeps their commitments will not fall, whether surrounded by harmful people or in any tempting situation. ^5-21

It would be better to lose my possessions, my life, my limbs, my reputation, or my livelihood, and even let other good qualities slip, than to ever let this resolve weaken. ^5-22

With my palms joined, I urge everyone who wishes to protect their mind: with all your effort, please protect your mindfulness and your awareness. ^5-23

People weakened by illness have no strength to do anything at all, and minds weakened by confusion have no strength to do anything at all either. ^5-24

Even if someone lacking awareness listens to teachings, reflects on them, or meditates, none of it will stick in their memory - like water poured into a leaky jar. ^5-25

Because of this lack of awareness, even many people with real learning, faith, and dedicated effort end up tainted by their own failures. ^5-26

This band of robbers - carelessness and lack of awareness - follows behind a weakened mindfulness, ready to steal away all the goodness you've built up, even as you head toward the lower realms. ^5-27

This gang of thieves, our own negative emotions, are always watching for an opportunity. Given the chance, they will plunder your goodness and destroy your chances of a higher rebirth. ^5-28

So never let your mindfulness wander from the door of your mind. And if it does wander off, bring it back at once by remembering the suffering of the lower realms. ^5-29

Through the company of a good teacher, through instruction from a mentor, and through a healthy sense of caution, those who are fortunate and respected develop mindfulness with ease. ^5-30

"The buddhas and bodhisattvas see everything, everywhere, without any obstruction. So I am always, in truth, standing in their very presence." ^5-31

Thinking this way, with conscientious care, respect, and a healthy sense of caution, the memory of the Buddha will then arise again and again. ^5-32

When mindfulness is set in place like this to guard the door of the mind, awareness too will come, and will return even if it has wandered off. ^5-33

First of all, in every moment, I must notice whether my mind has become clouded by negative emotion, and at that point I must be able to stay perfectly still, like a block of wood. ^5-34

I will never let myself glance around aimlessly out of distraction. Instead, with my mind focused, I will keep my gaze lowered. ^5-35

But now and then I might look around briefly to rest my tired eyes. And if someone happens to come into view, I'll look at them and greet them warmly. ^5-36

To notice any dangers along the road, I'll look often in all four directions, and before turning around, I'll pause first to check behind me. ^5-37

Having looked both ahead and behind in this way, I will then either move forward or turn back. In every situation, I will act only once I understand what is actually needed. ^5-38

When beginning any task, I'll set the intention, "I will hold my body just so," and then check in from time to time: "How is my body positioned right now?" ^5-39

With all my effort, I must keep checking that the wild elephant of my mind hasn't slipped free from the sturdy post of good, wholesome thoughts. ^5-40

Striving to stay focused and calm, I will examine my own mind by asking, "What is my mind doing right now?" - so as not to lose track of it even for a moment. ^5-41

If, because of danger, a feast, or some similar situation, you truly can't manage this, then do whatever is easiest. It is taught that in moments such as generous giving, some rules may be set aside. ^5-42

Once you've thought something through and begun a task, don't let your attention wander to something else. Keep your focus there and finish that one task first. ^5-43

If you do this, everything gets done properly. Otherwise, neither task will be completed well. In this way, you won't feed the nearby danger of carelessness. ^5-44

If you find yourself drawn into idle gossip or various kinds of entertaining spectacles, let go of your eagerness for them. ^5-45

If you're about to dig the ground, cut plants, or draw idly in the dirt without any real purpose, remember the Buddha's guidance and stop yourself out of a healthy sense of caution. ^5-46

Whenever you feel the urge to move or the urge to speak, check your own mind first, and then act firmly in a proper way. ^5-47

Whenever my mind feels the pull of craving or the urge of anger, I must not act and must not speak - I will hold still, like a block of wood. ^5-48

When I'm overexcited or overjoyed, when pride or arrogance arises, when I feel the urge to point out others' faults, stir up old conflicts, or act deceitfully, ^5-49

when I'm inclined to boast or to put others down, when I become abusive or argumentative, I will hold still, like a block of wood. ^5-50

When I crave profit, recognition, or fame, or long to be surrounded by attendants, or want to be waited on by others, I will hold still, like a block of wood. ^5-51

When the wish arises to give up on others' well-being and focus only on my own benefit, or when I simply feel like chattering, I will hold still, like a block of wood. ^5-52

And when impatience, laziness, fear, rudeness, or excessive talking arises, or a bias in favor of myself, I will hold still, like a block of wood. ^5-53

Noticing this way whenever their mind becomes clouded by negative emotion or occupied with something pointless, true practitioners use the right remedies to keep it firmly under control. ^5-54

I will be resolute, full of trust, steady, respectful, courteous, conscientious, appropriately cautious, calm, and dedicated to putting others at ease. ^5-55

I won't lose heart over the conflicting wishes of immature people who can't get along. I will meet them with kindness, understanding that such behavior comes from their own negative emotions. ^5-56

Guided by what is truly blameless, for my own sake and for the sake of others, I will always keep my mind steady - without clinging to a fixed sense of self - as if I were simply a helpful presence in the world. ^5-57

Again and again, I will remind myself how, after such a long time, I have finally found this greatest of opportunities, and I will keep my mind as unshakable as the greatest mountain in existence. ^5-58

If, mind, you won't be troubled when vultures hungry for flesh tear this body apart together, why do you cherish it so dearly right now? ^5-59

Mind, why is it that you cling to this body as "yours" and guard it so carefully? If you and it are truly separate things, what good does it actually do you? ^5-60

Confused mind, why don't you instead cling to a clean wooden figure? What sense is there in protecting this festering machine full of impurity? ^5-61

First, with careful thought, peel back and separate the layers of skin. Then, with the blade of wisdom, cut the flesh away from the cage of bones. ^5-62

Then break open the bones themselves. Look all the way in to the marrow, and examine it closely for yourself. What real essence does it actually contain? ^5-63

If, even after searching this carefully, you find no essence there at all, why do you still protect this body with such attachment? ^5-64

Its filth isn't fit for you to eat, nor its blood fit to drink, nor its insides fit to consume. So what exactly does this body do for you? ^5-65

It would actually make more sense to leave it as food for jackals and vultures. The bodies of all human beings are, in the end, simply something to be used. ^5-66

Even with all your protecting, when merciless death eventually takes this body to be given to birds and dogs, will there be anything at all you can do about it? ^5-67

If you wouldn't even bother clothing a servant who refuses to work for you, why exhaust yourself keeping up this body, which - however well you feed it - will eventually move on regardless? ^5-68

Now that you've paid its wages, so to speak, put this body to work for your own true benefit. Don't give it everything unconditionally if there's no real benefit in doing so. ^5-69

Instead, think of this body as a boat - simply a vehicle for getting from one place to another. Make it serve your true purpose: benefiting all beings. ^5-70

In this way, stay self-controlled and always keep a warm, smiling face. Let go of frowning and scowling. Be friendly toward others and straightforward. ^5-71

Don't shove chairs and furniture around carelessly or noisily. Don't slam doors open forcefully. Always take pleasure in being quiet and gentle. ^5-72

Cranes, cats, and burglars all move quietly and unobtrusively to accomplish what they set out to do. Wise practitioners should behave in just the same way. ^5-73

Always ready to learn from anyone, respectfully accept advice - whether it's asked for or not - from those who are skilled at helping others improve. ^5-74

To anyone who has spoken well, say, "That was well said." When you see someone doing something good, make them feel encouraged by praising them. ^5-75

Speak of others' good qualities quietly and sincerely, and repeat them when others mention them too. When someone speaks of your own good qualities, understand that they simply recognize goodness when they see it. ^5-76

All effort is ultimately aimed at happiness, yet even happiness bought with wealth is hard to find. So instead, I will find my own joy simply by delighting in the goodness that others do. ^5-77

In doing this, I lose nothing in this life, and I gain great happiness in lives to come. Wrongdoing, by contrast, brings unease and suffering now, and great misery later. ^5-78

When speaking, be sincere, clear, easy to follow, pleasant to hear, free from both craving and irritation, gentle, and appropriately brief. ^5-79

When you look at another being, think, "It is only because of this being that I will one day reach full enlightenment," and look at them with sincere warmth and love. ^5-80

Whether inspired by genuine longing or by consciously applying the right remedies, great goodness is found in three fields: the field of good qualities, the field of those who help us, and the field of those who suffer. ^5-81

Both skillfully and with a cheerful spirit, I will always carry out my own tasks myself. In none of my actions will I simply depend on others to do the work for me. ^5-82

The perfections - generosity, discipline, and so on - each build on the one before, each more important than the last. Don't set aside the greater one for the sake of the lesser. Keep the benefit of others foremost in mind. ^5-83

Understanding this, make continual effort for the sake of others. The compassionate, far-seeing Buddha permits, for their sake, things that would otherwise not be allowed. ^5-84

Share what you have with those who have fallen on hard times, with the unprotected, and with those practicing austerity. Eat only what you need, and give away everything except the three robes needed for practice. ^5-85

Don't harm this body - which exists for practicing the true teachings - over minor matters. Taking care of it this way, you will soon be able to fulfill the wishes of all beings. ^5-86

Unless your compassionate intention is genuinely pure, don't give away your own body. Instead, use it as a means to accomplish great goals both now and in the future. ^5-87

Don't teach the deeper truths to those who show no respect - those who, though not actually sick, cover their heads with turbans or parasols, or who carry staffs or weapons. ^5-88

Don't teach vast and profound truths to those not yet ready for them, and don't teach women unless a man is present as well. Treat the more basic and the more advanced teachings with equal respect. ^5-89

Don't offer only a basic teaching to someone genuinely ready for the deeper truths. Also, don't ignore proper conduct, and don't mislead people by misusing scriptures or sacred formulas. ^5-90

Cover it up when you discard a used tooth-cleaning stick or when you spit. It's disrespectful to urinate or do similar things on land or in water that others rely on. ^5-91

When eating, don't stuff your mouth, eat noisily, or chew with your mouth open. Don't sit with your legs stretched out carelessly, and don't rub both arms at the same time. ^5-92

Don't be alone with someone else's spouse while traveling, resting, or sitting together. Watch carefully, ask when unsure, and avoid anything that would cause people to lose their trust in you. ^5-93

Don't point at things with a single finger. Instead, gesture respectfully using your whole right hand, such as when showing someone the way. ^5-94

To get someone's attention, don't wave your whole arm wildly - just move it slightly and make a small sound, such as by snapping your fingers. Anything more looks undisciplined. ^5-95

Lie down to sleep in whatever direction you prefer, just as the Buddha did at the time of his final passing, and from the very start, resolve with clear awareness that you will get up promptly. ^5-96

The bodhisattva's practices are said to be countless in number. Among all of them, be sure to first take up the practice of purifying your own mind. ^5-97

Three times during the day and three times during the night, recite the verses that call to mind the buddhas and the wish to help all beings - this eases the weight of any remaining failures. ^5-98

Whatever you're doing, at any time, whether for your own sake or for others', make the effort to train according to the guidance given for that particular situation. ^5-99

There is nothing at all that a buddha's spiritual child should refuse to learn, and for someone wise who lives this way, there is nothing that cannot become a source of goodness. ^5-100

Do nothing except what benefits beings, whether directly or indirectly. Dedicate everything, purely for the sake of all beings, toward full enlightenment. ^5-101

Even at the cost of your own life, never abandon a spiritual guide who is well versed in the path of universal compassion and firmly committed to helping all beings. ^5-102

Learn how to properly serve a teacher from the story of Shri Sambhava. This, along with the Buddha's other teachings, should be studied by reading the scriptures. ^5-103

The guidelines for practice are laid out in the scriptures, so you should read them. As a good starting point, read the Sutra of Akashagarbha. ^5-104

Because it explains in detail what must be practiced continually, you should also read the Compendium of Trainings. ^5-105

Or, for now, you can read the shorter Compendium of Sutras, and you should also try to study the two texts written by the great teacher Nagarjuna. ^5-106

Whatever these texts don't specifically prohibit is good to act upon. And to guard the minds of ordinary people, act correctly, mindful of these guidelines. ^5-107

In short, the essence of maintaining awareness is simply this: check again and again on the state of your own mind and body. ^5-108

I will put all of this into practice with my very body - what good is merely reciting the words? Does simply reading a medical textbook actually help a sick patient get well? ^5-109

## The fifth chapter, "Guarding Awareness," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 6. The Perfection of Patience ^6-0

A single moment of anger can shatter all the goodness built up over a thousand ages - goodness such as generosity or honoring the buddhas. ^6-1

There is no wrongdoing like hatred, and no discipline like patience. So work diligently, in every way you can, to cultivate patience. ^6-2

When the grip of hatred takes hold of the mind, it finds no peace at all - no joy, no comfort, no restful sleep, no steadiness of any kind. ^6-3

Even someone who has been treated with wealth and respect will be turned on and even killed by those who depend on them, if that person is filled with hatred. ^6-4

Hatred drives away friends and family. Even those drawn in by gifts won't stay loyal. In short, there is nothing at all that makes an angry person happy. ^6-5

This enemy, anger, creates exactly this kind of suffering. Those who work to overcome it find happiness both now and in the future. ^6-6

When something happens that I don't want, or when my wishes are blocked, frustration arises and fuels my hatred, which then grows enough to destroy me. ^6-7

So I will cut off the very fuel of this, my true enemy. Apart from causing me harm, this enemy serves no purpose at all. ^6-8

Whatever happens, I won't let it disturb my sense of well-being. Being upset never gets me what I want - it only strips away my own goodness. ^6-9

If a problem can still be fixed, what is the use of being upset about it? And if it can't be fixed, what good does being upset do either way? ^6-10

None of us wants pain, humiliation, insults, or a bad reputation - for ourselves or for those we care about. Yet we wish exactly that on those we consider our enemies. ^6-11

The causes of happiness are rare, while the causes of suffering are everywhere. Without suffering, there would be no wish to be free of it - so, mind, stay steady and resolved. ^6-12

Certain ascetics pointlessly endure burns and wounds for the sake of their own rituals - so why would I be too cowardly to endure hardship for the sake of true freedom? ^6-13

There is nothing at all that doesn't become manageable once you're used to it. By growing accustomed to small discomforts, you'll come to bear even great hardships. ^6-14

Don't I already put up with pointless discomforts like snake and mosquito bites, hunger, thirst, and skin rashes? ^6-15

So I won't be overly sensitive about heat, cold, wind, rain, illness, confinement, or being struck - reacting too strongly only makes these things worse. ^6-16

Some people, seeing their own blood, become unusually brave, while others, seeing someone else's blood, faint and lose consciousness entirely. ^6-17

This difference comes either from steadiness of mind or from a kind of timidity. So disregard your injuries - don't let pain get the better of you. ^6-18

Even when pain arises, those who are wise don't let it disturb their inner peace. When waging war against negative emotions, plenty of hardship is only to be expected in the fight. ^6-19

True heroes are those who ignore all pain and overcome enemies like hatred. Everyone else, by comparison, is merely striking down lifeless bodies. ^6-20

What's more, suffering has real benefits: it humbles arrogance, awakens compassion for those trapped in this cycle of rebirth, turns you away from wrongdoing, and draws you toward goodness. ^6-21

We don't get angry at bile or other bodily imbalances, even though they're major sources of suffering - so why get angry at other people? They too are driven by conditions beyond their control. ^6-22

Just as such illnesses arise involuntarily, negative emotions also arise involuntarily, without anyone choosing them. ^6-23

People don't decide in advance, "I will get angry now," yet everyday annoyances provoke them anyway. They don't decide, "I will feel this rage," yet it arises regardless. ^6-24

Every kind of wrongdoing, every variety of misdeed, arises because of its underlying conditions - none of it happens through some independent free choice. ^6-25

The collection of conditions that produces something has no intention of "I will produce this," and what it produces has no intention of "I will be produced" either. ^6-26

Even the "primal substance" some claim exists, or the fixed "self" people imagine, doesn't think, "I will now come into being," and arise on purpose. ^6-27

Before it comes into being, it doesn't exist yet - so what exactly would there be to want to arise? And if it's always fixed on some object, it could never stop existing either. ^6-28

If the self were permanent, like space itself, it clearly couldn't do anything at all. So when it meets other conditions, what part of something unchanging could possibly act? ^6-29

If, during the action, it stays exactly the same as before, what did the action actually do to it? And if you say something else is what acts on it, then how is that connected to the self at all? ^6-30

In this way, everything arises dependent on conditions and has no independent control of its own. Once you understand this, you won't get angry at anything - it's all like an illusion. ^6-31

"But who is stopping what? Even the idea of stopping something would then make no sense." It does make sense, though, to say that suffering can end when its causes are removed. ^6-32

So when you see a friend or an enemy acting badly, remind yourself calmly that this is simply happening because of certain conditions. ^6-33

If everything happened purely through free choice, as people imagine, then no living being would ever suffer at all, since nobody actually wants to suffer. ^6-34

Yet out of carelessness, people injure themselves on thorns and the like. Driven by craving, they starve themselves and do all sorts of things for the sake of romance and pleasure. ^6-35

Some hang themselves, jump from cliffs, take poison, eat harmful food, or otherwise act in destructive ways, causing harm to themselves. ^6-36

When overwhelmed by negative emotions, people will even kill the very self they cherish most. Given that, how could they possibly refrain from harming others as well? ^6-37

Even though we rarely feel compassion for those whose negative emotions drive them to kill themselves or do similar things, what good does it do to get angry at them instead? ^6-38

If harming others were simply the fixed nature of foolish, untrained people, then being angry at them would make as little sense as resenting fire for being hot. ^6-39

But if this fault is just a passing condition, and people's underlying nature is actually gentle, then anger at them makes no more sense than resenting the sky for carrying smoke. ^6-40

If I overlook the real cause - the stick itself - and instead get angry only at the person who swings it, driven as they are by their own anger, then it would make far more sense to direct my anger at the anger itself. ^6-41

I myself, in the past, have caused this same kind of harm to other beings, so it's only fitting that harm of this kind should now come back to me, who once hurt others. ^6-42

Both their weapon and my own body are, in truth, the causes of this suffering. They wield the weapon; I supply the body. So which of us should I really be angry at? ^6-43

Blinded by craving, I have grasped onto this human body - fragile as a blister, unable to bear even a light touch. When it gets hurt, who exactly is there to be angry at? ^6-44

Foolish, untrained people don't want suffering, and yet they can't stop craving its very causes. So when I'm harmed because of my own past wrongs, how can I really resent the other person? ^6-45

If this harm, like the punishments dealt out in hell or the forests of sword-like leaves there, is ultimately produced by my own past actions, then who exactly should I be angry at? ^6-46

If those who, prompted by the results of my own past actions, appear and harm me will themselves end up in a hellish state because of it, then haven't I, in effect, brought about their downfall? ^6-47

Because of them, I get to purify many of my own past wrongs through practicing patience. Because of me, they will face long and terrible suffering in return. ^6-48

If, in truth, I am the one harming them, even while they seem to be helping me purify my wrongs, then why, cruel mind, are you getting angry at them by mistake? ^6-49

If I can maintain a good intention throughout, I won't end up in a hellish state myself - so what would even happen to them now, if I simply protect my own state of mind? ^6-50

But if I strike back at them in return, then they won't be protected from harm either, and meanwhile my own conduct will be corrupted, and my discipline undone. ^6-51

Because the mind has no physical form, nothing can actually wound it directly. It's only because of our attachment to the body that pain is able to harm us at all. ^6-52

Contempt, harsh words, and unpleasant speech cause no real injury to the body. So mind, why do you let them provoke you so strongly? ^6-53

Other people's displeasure with me won't devour me in this life or in any future one - so why do I dislike it so much? ^6-54

Even though I dislike it because it blocks material gain, I will leave all my possessions behind when I die, while any wrongdoing I've done will follow me for a long time. ^6-55

It would be better to die right now than to live a long life through wrong ways of making a living. Even if I lived a very long time, the pain of death would be just the same in the end. ^6-56

One person feels pleasure for a hundred years in a dream, then wakes up. Another experiences pleasure for just a single moment, then wakes up. ^6-57

For both of them, once awake, that pleasure never returns. In the same way, a long life and a short life both come to the very same end when death arrives. ^6-58

Even if I gain many possessions and enjoy them for a long time, I will still leave this life as if robbed by bandits - empty-handed, with nothing at all. ^6-59

If I'm living off my gains in order to reduce my wrongdoing and practice goodness, then wouldn't getting angry over material things itself waste that goodness and become a wrongdoing in its own right? ^6-60

And if the very purpose of my life becomes corrupted this way, what good is this life at all, if all I end up doing is committing more wrongs? ^6-61

You say you get angry at someone who insults you because it harms others too. But then why don't you also get upset when someone else is insulted? ^6-62

If you can forgive people who lose faith in something simply because it depends on outside causes, then since negative emotions also depend on conditions, why not forgive your critics in just the same way? ^6-63

It isn't right to get angry at those who insult or destroy sacred images, shrines, or the true teachings - the buddhas themselves are not actually harmed by any of this. ^6-64

As explained earlier, see anyone who harms your teacher, family, or friends as acting out of the very same web of conditions, and let that understanding hold back your anger. ^6-65

If harm to living beings comes from both living and non-living causes alike, why single out and resent only the living cause? Better to simply bear the harm patiently either way. ^6-66

Some people, out of ignorance, do wrong. Others, out of ignorance, get angry in response. Which of the two, if either, is truly innocent, and which is truly guilty? ^6-67

Why did they, in the past, do the kinds of things that would lead others to harm them now? Since everything unfolds according to cause and effect, why hold a grudge against the other person at all? ^6-68

Understanding this, whatever happens, I will make every effort to build goodness, so that all beings might come to hold loving thoughts toward one another. ^6-69

When a fire that has burned down one house threatens to spread to another, you quickly gather up and throw away the straw and kindling that would feed it. ^6-70

In the very same way, immediately let go of anything you're attached to that feeds the fire of hatred - out of fear that it will burn away all the goodness you've built. ^6-71

What's wrong with someone condemned to death having their sentence reduced to just losing a hand instead? What's wrong with ordinary human suffering being the thing that spares you from the hells? ^6-72

If I can't even bear this small amount of suffering here and now, then why don't I let go of my anger, which is the very cause of the far greater suffering of the hells? ^6-73

For the sake of fleeting desires, I have already endured being burned and tormented in hell a thousand times over, and it accomplished nothing good for myself or for anyone else. ^6-74

But this present hardship is nothing compared to that, and it serves a truly great purpose. I should actually be glad to endure suffering that removes far greater harm to beings. ^6-75

If people feel happy when someone is praised for their good qualities, then why, mind, don't you also praise them and share in that same joy? ^6-76

The joy you feel from celebrating others' goodness is a completely blameless source of happiness - one that good people themselves encourage, and the very best way to draw others closer. ^6-77

"But if I do that, others will end up happy too." If you don't want that kind of shared joy for yourself, then you should stop paying anyone kindness at all, and you'll end up losing benefit both now and later. ^6-78

When your own good qualities are praised, you want others to feel pleased about it. But when someone else's good qualities are praised, you don't want to feel pleased yourself. ^6-79

If you gave rise to the wish to help all beings because you want everyone to be happy, then why does it upset you when beings actually find happiness on their own? ^6-80

If you wish for beings to reach the full enlightenment honored throughout the universe, why does it bother you so much to see them receive some small measure of recognition now? ^6-81

If a family member you support were to find their own means of living, would that make you angry, or would it make you glad? ^6-82

If you don't even want that much for other beings, how can you claim to wish them full enlightenment? And if you're bothered by others having wealth, how can you really have the wish to help all beings? ^6-83

Whether what someone gains stays with them or ends up somewhere else entirely, either way, it was never yours to begin with. So whether it's given away or not, why does it concern you at all? ^6-84

Why would you throw away your own goodness, your faith, and your good qualities like this? Tell me - why aren't you instead angry at yourself, for not being able to let go of your own attachment to gain? ^6-85

Not only do you feel no regret over the wrongs you've done yourself, but you even want to compete with those who have actually done good? ^6-86

Even when your enemies are made unhappy, what benefit is that really to you? The wishes in your own mind alone cannot actually cause them any harm. ^6-87

And even if your wish for their suffering somehow came true, what real satisfaction would that bring you? You might say you'd feel satisfied - but what could be more ruinous than that? ^6-88

The hook cast by these fishermen - our own negative emotions - is unbearably sharp. Once caught on it, I will surely be dragged off to be tormented by the guardians of hell. ^6-89

Praise, fame, and honor will not bring you goodness or a longer life. They won't give you strength, good health, or physical well-being either. ^6-90

If I truly understood what's good for me, what benefit would I find in these things? If all you're really after is a pleasant feeling in the mind, you might as well seek it through drink or games of chance. ^6-91

For the sake of fame, some people waste their wealth, or even get themselves killed. What use are mere words of praise, when you're dead - who exactly do they make happy then? ^6-92

When their sandcastles are destroyed, children cry out in distress. In just the same way, when I lose praise and fame, my own reaction is really no different from a child's. ^6-93

Words themselves have no mind, so it's impossible for them to actually intend to praise me. Still, hearing that someone approves of me might genuinely bring me pleasure - ^6-94

If their happiness makes me happy, then I should feel that way about everyone's happiness. So why don't I feel pleased when people are made happy by their fondness for someone else instead of me? ^6-96

What use do I, who am seeking freedom, have for the shackles of gain and reputation? And why would I get angry at those who release me when I'm bound by them? ^6-100

A buddha's good qualities are cultivated through both ordinary beings and the buddhas alike. So how is it right to honor the buddhas but not ordinary beings? ^6-113

Since beings have a real part in bringing about a buddha's supreme qualities, they deserve honor to that same extent. ^6-118

Other than working to make beings happy, what other way is there to repay these steadfast friends who bring about immeasurable benefit for us? ^6-119

How is it, then, that I - so foolish - remain full of pride and fail to act like a true servant toward those for whose sake my spiritual guides disregarded even their own bodies? ^6-121

Making beings happy brings joy to the compassionate ones, whose happiness depends on beings' well-being and who are pained by beings' suffering. Harming beings, in turn, brings pain to those same compassionate ones. ^6-122

Just as nothing brings pleasure while your own body is on fire, there is no way for those of great compassion to feel pleased if you cause harm to other beings. ^6-123

There is no doubt that those whose very nature is compassion regard all these beings as no different from themselves. Since beings, in essence, are exactly what these compassionate guardians cherish most, how could they ever deserve disrespect? ^6-126

This alone is what truly pleases the buddhas. This alone is what truly fulfills my own deepest purpose. This alone removes the suffering of the world. So this is what I will always strive to do. ^6-127

because that soldier is not acting alone, but backed by the full power of the king - in just the same way, don't look down on seemingly weak people who cause you harm, ^6-129

for behind them stand the forces of the lower realms and the power of the compassionate ones. Just as subjects appease even a harsh ruler, treat all beings with the same kind of respect. ^6-130

Even an angry king could never inflict on you the kind of suffering that comes from causing beings despair - the agonies of the deepest hell. ^6-131

And even a delighted king could never grant you what comes from making beings genuinely happy - nothing less than full enlightenment itself. ^6-132

Setting aside future enlightenment altogether, don't you already see in this very life that making beings happy brings you great renown, good standing, and joy? ^6-133

While still within this cycle of rebirth, patience brings beauty, good health, a respected standing, a very long life, and even the greatest pleasures a ruler of the world could know. ^6-134

## The sixth chapter, "The Perfection of Patience," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 7. Teachings on Diligence ^7-0

So, with patience established, be diligent. Enlightenment depends on this kind of sustained effort. Just as there's no movement without wind, there's no real goodness without diligence. ^7-1

Diligence means having real enthusiasm for doing good. What's its opposite? To put it simply, it's laziness - clinging to unhelpful habits, sluggishness, and putting yourself down. ^7-2

Laziness comes from not being tired enough of the pain of this endless cycle of rebirth, from craving sleep, and from savoring the comfort of doing nothing. ^7-3

Your skin is so sensitive that even scalding water hurts it, yet you have done the kind of things that lead to the hottest hells. Why, then, do you still feel so at ease? ^7-12

"But I'm afraid of having to give up my limbs and so on," I say - without really thinking through what's actually serious and what's minor. This is simply confusion leaving me needlessly afraid. ^7-20

For countless millions of ages, I have already been cut, stabbed, burned, and torn apart again and again, all without ever achieving enlightenment. ^7-21

But the suffering needed to actually reach enlightenment has a clear limit - like the brief pain of a surgical cut made to remove something harmful lodged inside the body. ^7-22

Every doctor cures illness through some amount of uncomfortable treatment. In the same way, put up with a little discomfort now in order to overcome far greater suffering later. ^7-23

There is no real pain in giving up wrongdoing, and no real sorrow that comes from wisdom, because physical suffering comes from past wrongdoing, and mental suffering comes from mistaken ways of thinking. ^7-27

Because the power of the wish to help all beings both clears away one's past wrongs and gathers vast, ocean-like goodness, it's taught that this path surpasses even that of those who seek liberation for themselves alone. ^7-29

## The seventh chapter, "Teachings on Diligence," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 8. Teachings on Meditation ^8-0

If, even with this evidence in front of you, you still doubt that the body is truly impure in this way, then go look at other decaying bodies left in a burial ground. ^8-63

And once its skin has been stripped away, you know full well you would be horrified - so how is it that you're still drawn to that very same thing while it's intact? ^8-64

The pleasant scent applied to a body is nothing but sandalwood or perfume - nothing that actually belongs to the body itself. So why does a scent that comes from somewhere else make you desire the person wearing it? ^8-65

Wouldn't it be better not to be drawn at all to something that naturally has no pleasant smell of its own? Why do people in the world, chasing this pointlessly, cover it over with pleasant fragrances instead? ^8-66

If that sweet scent actually belongs to the sandalwood, then what exactly does the body itself contribute? Why would you be drawn to someone simply because of a scent that comes from something else entirely? ^8-67

By its own nature, the body grows long hair and nails, and its teeth turn a dull, yellowish white. It gives off unpleasant smells and is streaked with grime. Left uncovered, it would be quite unsettling to look at. ^8-68

Why do you work so hard to polish up something that's really like a blade capable of wounding you? Those caught up in this kind of self-deception have thrown the whole world into turmoil because of it. ^8-69

If seeing nothing but bare skeletons in a burial ground fills you with unease, how could you possibly be drawn to towns full of the same skeletons, just still animated and moving around? ^8-70

Something this impure cannot be obtained without a real cost - exhaustion from working hard to earn it, and even suffering in the hells or other difficult states because of how it was pursued. ^8-71

As children, people can't yet earn a living. In youth, how is chasing after this really happiness? You spend your whole life gathering wealth, and by the time you're old, what good do your unfulfilled desires actually do you? ^8-72

Some people, driven by passion yet humble in circumstance, exhausted after a full day's labor, return home only to collapse, their worn-out bodies lying still like a corpse. ^8-73

Others travel far from home and suffer through weariness and long, difficult journeys. Longing to see their spouse and children, they may go a whole year without seeing them at all. ^8-74

Confused by their own self-interest, some sell themselves into work meant to help themselves get ahead. And even then, having failed to gain what they wanted, they're simply driven along by the pointless demands of serving others instead. ^8-75

Some sell their very bodies and are put to work for others, with no freedom of their own. Women give birth wherever they can - under a tree, or out in the wild. ^8-76

Claiming they just want to make a living, foolish people, deceived by their own desires, risk their lives going into battle or entering servitude, all for the sake of profit. ^8-77

Driven by greed, some end up wounded, others impaled, some pierced through by spears, and some are even burned alive. ^8-78

Understand that the effort of getting wealth, keeping it, and eventually losing it makes it an endless source of trouble. For those distracted by greed for wealth, there is no real chance of ever being free from the suffering of this existence. ^8-79

For those driven by desire, problems like these are plentiful, while the actual rewards are small - just as an ox pulling a heavy cart gets only a few mouthfuls of grass along the way. ^8-80

For the sake of such small, common rewards - the kind even oxen manage to get - this rare and precious human opportunity, so hard to come by, ends up crushed under the weight of the effort spent chasing it. ^8-81

With just a tiny fraction of the tremendous, ongoing effort that people pour into these minor pursuits - pleasures that are certain to fade away ^8-82

and that can even lead to the hells - they could instead become fully enlightened. Yet those driven by greed end up with great suffering and no enlightenment at all, instead of a life of true, awakened conduct. ^8-83

Considering the sufferings of hell and similar states, there is truly no weapon, no poison, no fire, no chasm, and no enemy that compares to the harm caused by desire itself. ^8-84

So grow weary of these desires, and cultivate real delight in solitude instead. Deep within a peaceful forest, free of conflict and disturbance, ^8-85

on broad, flat stones - like a delightful palace cooled by moonlight as if by sandalwood - those fortunate enough to be there walk calmly, fanned by a gentle woodland breeze, quiet and at ease, reflecting on the well-being of others. ^8-86

In an empty hut, beneath a tree, or inside a cave, you may stay as long as you wish. Leaving behind the burden of acquiring and guarding possessions, you live free of worry, depending on no one. ^8-87

To act freely, without desires and without being tied down to anyone, is a comfort and contentment so satisfying that even a prince would find it hard to come by. ^8-88

Having reflected on these and other benefits of solitude, fully calm your scattered thoughts, and meditate on the wish to help all beings. ^8-89

First, meditate wholeheartedly on the basic equality of yourself and others. Since everyone is equal in wanting comfort and avoiding pain, protect all beings just as you would protect yourself. ^8-90

Even though the hands and other parts of the body are many and different, together they form a single body to be cared for. In the same way, even though beings are distinct and separate in their individual joys and pains, they are all equal in this: every one of them, just like me, wants comfort and well-being. ^8-91

Even though my own suffering doesn't cause any harm to other people's bodies, it still feels unbearable to me - simply because of my attachment to a sense of self. ^8-92

In the same way, even though others' suffering has no direct effect on me, if I truly cared as much about them as myself, their suffering would feel just as unbearable, again because of this same attachment to self. ^8-93

So I will work to remove others' suffering, simply because it is suffering, exactly like my own. And I will work to benefit others, simply because they are living beings, exactly like myself. ^8-94

Since both I and others are equally beings who want happiness, and others are really no different from me in this regard, why would I work only for my own pleasure? ^8-95

And since both I and others are equally beings who want to avoid suffering, and others are really no different from me in this regard, why would I protect only myself and not others? ^8-96

If I say I won't protect others because their suffering doesn't harm me directly, then by that same logic, my own future suffering doesn't harm me right now either - so why guard against that? ^8-97

It's actually a mistake to think, "It is I who will experience that future suffering," because, strictly speaking, it is one being who dies in that moment and, in effect, another being entirely who is born afterward. ^8-98

If you say that whoever experiences a suffering should be the one responsible for guarding against it, well, the foot's pain isn't the hand's pain either - so why would one part protect the other at all? ^8-99

"Even though it may not be entirely logical, we act this way out of attachment to self," you might say. Yet we should still work as much as possible to release these mistaken notions of a fixed self and other. ^8-100

A stream of moments, or a collection of parts - like a string of beads, an army made of soldiers, and so on - has no independent existence of its own beyond its parts. In the same way, there is no truly independent "self" that suffers - so exactly who does that suffering really belong to? ^8-101

Since suffering has no true, independent owner, there's no real difference between anyone's suffering. I will simply work to remove it, precisely because it is suffering - so why would I place any arbitrary limit on that? ^8-102

There's no reasonable argument that suffering, wherever it occurs, shouldn't be prevented. If my own suffering should be prevented, then everyone's suffering should be prevented in just the same way - or else, by that same logic, mine shouldn't be prevented either. ^8-103

"But compassion causes so much pain," you might say, "so why make such effort to cultivate it?" Yet when you actually consider the suffering of all beings, how could the discomfort of feeling compassion possibly outweigh that? ^8-104

Because if a single, smaller suffering can prevent many greater miseries, then someone with real compassion will willingly bring that smaller suffering upon themselves, for the sake of both themselves and others. ^8-105

This is why, even knowing the king would harm him for it, Supushpachandra did not hold back from accepting pain, in order to put an end to the suffering of many. ^8-106

Those who have trained their own minds this way will willingly enter even the deepest hell with genuine joy, simply to ease the suffering of others - like swans gliding down onto a lotus pond. ^8-107

When beings are finally set free from suffering, isn't the sheer ocean of joy that brings enough on its own? What further need is there to seek liberation just for oneself? ^8-108

So even though I've done good for others, I feel no pride or self-satisfaction about it. Simply enjoying the act of helping, I hold no expectation of any reward in return. ^8-109

Just as I protect myself from even the smallest criticism, I will, in that same way, be protective and compassionate toward others as well. ^8-110

Just as, through sheer habit, I have come to think of the drops that formed this body as "myself," even though that idea has no real, ultimate basis, ^8-111

why couldn't I, in the very same way, come to regard the bodies of other beings as "myself" too? It isn't actually any harder to see my own body as belonging to someone else. ^8-112

Recognizing that I have my own faults, while others hold vast, ocean-like good qualities, I will meditate on letting go of my attachment to self and taking up the well-being of others instead. ^8-113

Just as we naturally accept that our hands and other limbs are simply parts of one body, why shouldn't we accept, in the same way, that all living beings are simply parts of one interconnected world? ^8-114

The very idea that this selfless collection of parts is somehow "me" arose only through habit and repetition. So why couldn't the idea that others are also "me" arise through that same kind of habit and repetition? ^8-115

So even though I do good for others, I hold no pride or self-satisfaction about it - just as there's no expectation of thanks when I simply eat my own food to nourish myself. ^8-116

In just the same way that I protect myself from even the smallest fault, I will cultivate this same protective, compassionate care for all beings. ^8-117

This is exactly why Avalokiteshvara, out of great compassion, blessed his own name with the power to calm the fears that beings feel, even in the middle of a crowd. ^8-118

So don't shy away from this difficult practice. Through the power of habit and repetition, even something that once caused fear - like hearing a certain name - eventually becomes something you can't feel at peace without. ^8-119

All those who wish to quickly protect both themselves and others should take up this most excellent secret practice: exchanging concern for oneself with concern for others. ^8-120

Because of our attachment to this body, we become frightened even by the smallest threat. Who wouldn't want to be free of this fear-prone body, treating it almost like an enemy? ^8-121

Driven by the wish to relieve hunger, thirst, and similar discomforts, the body will lead you to kill birds, fish, and deer, or lie in wait along the road to ambush travelers. ^8-122

And if it leads you to harm your own parents, or to steal what has been offered to the Three Jewels for the sake of gain or reputation, it can even lead you to be burned in the deepest hell. ^8-123

No wise person would want a body like this, nor would they protect it or hold it in high regard. Who wouldn't look at it and see something like an enemy? Who wouldn't feel some healthy detachment from it? ^8-124

"If I give this away, what will I have left to enjoy?" - this kind of selfish thinking is the way of small-minded, self-centered beings. "If I enjoy this myself, what will I have left to give?" - this kind of generous thinking is the way of true, sacred practice. ^8-125

If you harm others for your own benefit, you will suffer for it in hell and elsewhere. If you accept harm for the benefit of others, you will gain everything truly excellent. ^8-126

Those who seek high status for themselves alone end up lowly and foolish in the lower realms, while those who offer that same status to others gain real respect in the higher realms. ^8-127

If you use others merely for your own selfish ends, you will end up experiencing servitude yourself. If you use your own efforts for the sake of others, you will experience true, natural leadership. ^8-128

All the happiness that exists in the world comes from wishing happiness for others. All the suffering that exists in the world comes from wanting happiness only for oneself. ^8-129

What need is there to say more? Untrained, ordinary people act only for their own sake, while the truly wise act for the sake of others. Just look at the difference between the two. ^8-130

If you don't learn to trade your own comfort for the discomfort of others, you will never achieve full enlightenment, nor will you find any real happiness even in this ordinary life. ^8-131

Never mind the next life - even in this one, a servant who refuses to work, and a master who refuses to pay, will both fail to accomplish what they're actually after. ^8-132

Foolish people throw away the complete happiness that comes both now and later, and instead, by causing misery to others, they bring terrible suffering upon themselves. ^8-133

All the violence, fear, and suffering that exists in the world comes from clinging to a fixed sense of self. So really, what good does this so-called "self" actually do for me? ^8-134

Without letting go of this clinging to self, you cannot let go of suffering - just as, without letting go of fire, you cannot avoid being burned by it. ^8-135

So, in order to calm the harm done to myself and ease the suffering of others, I will give myself over entirely to others, and hold onto others as though they were my own self. ^8-136

Mind, understand this with real certainty: I now belong to other beings. From this point on, think of nothing except benefiting all beings. ^8-137

It isn't right to use eyes and other things that, in truth, belong to all beings, purely for selfish ends. And it isn't right to treat others poorly using capacities that exist to benefit them. ^8-138

So I will put the well-being of others first. Whatever I notice about my own body, I will set it aside and put it to use for the sake of benefiting others instead. ^8-139

Take those who seem lesser, and in your meditation, place yourself in their position, and place them in the position you once held as "yourself." Then meditate with a mind entirely free of envy, rivalry, and pride. ^8-140

"They are respected; I am not. I don't receive as much as they do. They are praised, while I am mocked, and they are happy, while I suffer. ^8-141

"I'm the one doing all the hard work, yet they live comfortably. They're seen as important by everyone, while I'm said to have no real qualities at all." ^8-142

"No good qualities? What am I supposed to do about that? Actually, I do have good qualities of my own. There are some people worse off than them, and some I'm even better than." ^8-143

"My discipline, my understanding, and so on have all been affected by my negative emotions - I can't help it, so they should try to help me as best they can, and I'll accept whatever difficulty that involves." ^8-144

"But they don't even bother to help someone like me - why do they treat me with such contempt instead? And if they really do have good qualities, what use are those qualities to me anyway?" ^8-145

"They show no real compassion for those trapped in the terrifying grip of the lower realms. Puffed up with mere outward appearances of virtue, they only want to compete with those who are genuinely wise." ^8-146

When I see those who are truly my equals, this is how I might think: "To make myself look better than them, I'll take what they have and win the respect instead, even if I have to fight for it. ^8-147

"No matter what, I'll make sure my own good qualities become known throughout the world. I'll make certain that no one ever hears about any of theirs. ^8-148

"And I'll hide my own faults carefully, so that I'm the one who's honored, and they are not. I'll make sure I gain possessions easily and receive respect - while they receive none." ^8-149

"When they act inappropriately, I'll watch with quiet satisfaction for a long time. I'll make sure they become a laughingstock, mocked by everyone around them." ^8-150

"This poor fool thinks they can rival me," people say - but can they really compare to me in learning, intelligence, appearance, stature, or wealth? ^8-151

"When they hear about my accomplishments, ones that everyone already knows about, let them feel a thrill of excitement just from hearing of them." ^8-152

"Even if they have some wealth, we'll take it all away by force. And if they do work for us, we'll only give them just enough to get by." ^8-153

"We'll deprive them of happiness and keep them constantly burdened with our own troubles instead. This person has caused us countless miseries, over and over, throughout this endless cycle of rebirth." ^8-154

Mind, you have spent countless ages seeking only your own benefit, yet even with all that tremendous effort, all you've actually produced is more suffering. ^8-155

So you must now apply yourself completely to benefiting others instead. Later, you will see the real value in this - the wise teacher's words never mislead. ^8-156

If you had taken up this practice earlier, a situation like this one - falling so far short of a buddha's perfect happiness - would never have come about. ^8-157

And so, just as you have come to cling to the very drops that formed this body as being "yourself," you must, in that same way, learn to think of others as yourself too. ^8-158

You should act, in a sense, as a spy on behalf of others. Whatever you notice about your own body, take that very thing and use it to benefit other beings instead. ^8-159

"I'm comfortable; others aren't. I have high standing; others are lowly. I'm well supported; others aren't." Why aren't you envious of yourself, hearing that? ^8-160

Deprive yourself of comfort. Take on others' suffering as your own. Whenever you catch yourself thinking, "What is this person up to?" turn that same scrutiny onto your own shortcomings instead. ^8-161

Even wrongs that others commit should be regarded, in your own reflection, as if they were your own faults. And even the small wrongs you yourself commit should be openly admitted to many others. ^8-162

Make a point of spreading others' good reputation, so that it outshines your own. Put yourself entirely at their service, as if you were the lowliest of servants. ^8-163

This mind of mine is, by nature, full of flaws - so don't praise its passing, occasional virtues. Do everything you can to make sure no one hears about whatever good qualities it might seem to have. ^8-164

In short, for the sake of your own selfish interests, you have caused injury to others in the past. Now make sure that, for the sake of all beings instead, every bit of that harm falls on you. ^8-165

Don't let this mind of yours act with careless arrogance or stubbornness. Instead, keep it restrained, as modest and cautious as a shy new bride. ^8-166

"Behave like this! You must stay exactly this way! And you must never act like that!" Exercise real authority over your own mind, and correct it firmly whenever it disobeys. ^8-167

Mind, if you still won't listen even after being told this clearly, know that you will indeed be brought under control, since every wrongdoing ultimately comes from you. ^8-168

The days when you were able to lead me astray are now behind us. I see clearly now, wherever you try to go. I will put an end to all your vanity and self-importance. ^8-169

Let go of the idea that you still have purposes that are purely your own. Since I have now dedicated you entirely to the service of others, offer up your strength willingly, without resentment. ^8-170

If I grow careless and fail to dedicate you fully to the service of all beings, it's certain that you, in turn, will hand me over to the guardians of the lower realms. ^8-171

I have suffered for a long time because of how often you have led me astray in this way. Now, remembering that, I will put an end to your selfish habits for good. ^8-172

So if you truly want happiness, stop seeking pleasure only for yourself. And if you truly want to protect yourself, keep protecting others instead, continuously. ^8-173

The more I coddle and protect this body, the more fragile and prone to breaking down it becomes. ^8-174

If the desires of someone who has fallen into this kind of self-indulgence couldn't be satisfied even by the entire earth, then who could possibly satisfy them at all? ^8-175

Desires that can never be satisfied only give rise to more negative emotion and a weakening of good qualities. Those who depend on nothing outside themselves enjoy a kind of excellence that never runs out. ^8-176

So don't give physical desires any chance to grow stronger. Not clinging to anything at all as something you must have is truly the finest way to live. ^8-177

In the end, this body will become nothing but ash - unmoving on its own, moved only by outside forces. So why do I cling to this unclean and, frankly, unsettling form as though it were truly "me"? ^8-178

Whether it's alive or dead, of what real use is this body-machine to me? If it's really no different from a lump of clay or wood, why on earth do I still cling to pride about it? ^8-179

Because of my fondness for this body, I have brought needless suffering upon myself. What use is there in feeling attachment or anger over something that is, in truth, no different from a piece of wood? ^8-180

Whether I take great care of this body, or vultures and other creatures eventually consume it, the body itself feels neither attachment nor aversion either way - so why am I so attached to it? ^8-181

If the body itself feels no anger when insulted and no pleasure when praised, then who exactly am I wearing myself out for? ^8-182

If you say that whoever is fond of this body must be my true friend, well, everyone is fond of their own body in just the same way - so why am I not equally fond of them? ^8-183

So I will give up my attachment to this body and instead use it entirely for the benefit of all beings. Even though it has many flaws, I will keep it around simply as a tool for doing good. ^8-184

I have had enough of untrained, ordinary behavior. I will now follow in the footsteps of the wise. Recalling these teachings on carefulness, I will guard myself against sleepiness and sluggishness. ^8-185

## The eighth chapter, "Teachings on Meditation," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. 9. The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0

The wise teacher taught all of these earlier practices for the sake of wisdom. So those who wish to bring suffering to an end should cultivate wisdom. ^9-1

Two levels of truth are described: the everyday, conventional truth, and the ultimate truth. The ultimate truth is beyond the reach of an ordinary, confused mind - and it's this ordinary mind that operates at the conventional level. ^9-2

Because of this, two kinds of people can be recognized: those with deep meditative insight, and ordinary people. Between the two, the views of ordinary people are shown to be mistaken by those with deeper insight. ^9-3

And because of differing depths of understanding, even among those with insight, the more advanced correct the views of those less advanced, using examples both sides already accept, without needing to settle every detail for this purpose. ^9-4

Ordinary people think that the things they perceive are simply real, just as they appear, and not illusion-like at all. This is exactly where those with deeper insight disagree with the ordinary view. ^9-5

Even things like the perception of physical form are only a matter of common agreement, not real, valid knowledge - just as it's common agreement, yet false, that something impure could be considered clean. ^9-6

In order to guide ordinary people gradually, the Buddha taught, at first, that things simply exist. In truth, though, they don't even last from one moment to the next. "But doesn't that contradict everyday truth?" you might ask. ^9-7

There's no problem here: this second, deeper way of seeing things is simply how someone with real insight sees reality as it truly is, compared to the ordinary view - similar to how someone might correct the ordinary view that this body is something other than impure matter. ^9-8

Buddhas who arise like illusions still produce real goodness, just as those who seem solid and real would. "But if beings are like illusions, how can they be reborn after death?" ^9-9

Illusions arise for as long as the right conditions come together. Does merely lasting a long time actually prove that beings truly, independently exist? ^9-10

Killing a being that is like an illusion carries no real wrongdoing, since an illusion has no mind of its own. But real goodness and wrongdoing do arise for beings whose minds, though illusion-like, still experience things. ^9-11

Because ordinary spells and such don't have the power to create a mind, illusions of that kind don't actually think or feel. But since illusions arise from many different kinds of conditions, illusions themselves come in many different kinds. ^9-12

There is no single condition anywhere capable of producing every kind of result on its own. "If beings still go through this cycle of rebirth on the everyday level, even though, ultimately, they are already at peace, ^9-13

then even the buddhas themselves would still be caught in this cycle - so why bother with the path to enlightenment at all?" Unless the ongoing chain of conditions is actually cut, the illusion will simply continue to arise. ^9-14

But once that chain of conditions has truly been cut, the illusion won't arise again, even on the everyday level. "But if there's no truly confused mind to begin with, then what exactly is it that perceives these illusions?" ^9-15

But if, in your own view, the illusion itself doesn't ultimately exist, then what exactly is there left to be perceived? "In ultimate reality, there is still something else present - that appearance is simply the mind itself." ^9-16

But if the mind itself is just as illusion-like as everything else, then at that point, what is it that perceives what? And the Buddha himself, the Protector of the World, has said, "Mind cannot see mind." ^9-17

Just as a sword's blade cannot cut itself, the mind cannot perceive itself either. "But," you might say, "it's like a lamp, which illuminates its own nature as well as everything else." ^9-18

A lamp isn't actually something that needs to be illuminated in the first place, since it was never obscured by darkness to begin with. "But unlike a crystal, which needs something else to appear blue, blue itself doesn't depend on anything else to be blue." ^9-19

"In the same way, some things are seen to depend on something else to exist, while other things aren't." But something that isn't blue to begin with can't make itself blue on its own either. ^9-20

There is ordinary awareness that recognizes and describes a lamp as being luminous. But what exactly is it that recognizes and describes the mind itself as being inherently luminous? ^9-21

Whether the mind is luminous or not, if it can't actually be perceived by anything else, then discussing it at all is as pointless as discussing the charms of the imaginary daughter of a childless woman. ^9-22

"But if there's no such thing as self-awareness, then how could we ever remember a past experience of consciousness at all?" When something else was experienced at the same time, the memory arises through that connection - like remembering the sting of a rat's venomous bite through the pain that followed it. ^9-23

"But this self-awareness is perceived because of other supporting conditions, so it genuinely illuminates itself." Yet when magic eye ointment is applied, you see the vase in front of you, but never the ointment itself, even though it's what allows you to see. ^9-24

What is actually being challenged here isn't the ordinary way that things are seen, heard, or known. What's being challenged is the deeper cause of suffering - the mistaken belief that these things are ultimately, independently real. ^9-25

If you think illusions are neither the same as the mind that perceives them nor entirely different from it - if they were truly real, how could they not be different from mind? And if they aren't different from mind, then they can't be truly, independently real either. ^9-26

What is seen - the illusion - isn't ultimately real, and neither is the mind that perceives it, ultimately real either. "But this whole cycle of rebirth must have some truly real basis, otherwise it would be just like empty space, with nothing happening at all." ^9-27

But how would something being based on something "real" actually explain how something unreal could perform any real action? And besides, if your mind existed with no connection to anything else at all, it would be completely alone and isolated. ^9-28

If the mind became free of any perceived object altogether, then everyone would already be a fully enlightened buddha. In that case, what real benefit is there in insisting that only mind exists, and nothing else? ^9-29

"But how does merely knowing that everything is like an illusion actually stop negative emotions from arising? After all, even an illusionist might still feel desire for the dreamlike lover they themselves conjured up." ^9-30

That illusionist simply hasn't yet let go of the deep habitual tendency toward negative emotions regarding the kinds of things they perceive, so when they see them, their habit of recognizing emptiness is still too weak to override it. ^9-31

By deeply cultivating the habit of recognizing emptiness, we gradually remove the older habit of seeing things as solid and real. And once we've trained ourselves to see that "nothing ultimately exists," even that very idea is eventually let go of as well. ^9-32

When we say "nothing ultimately exists," the very thing being examined isn't found to exist independently in the first place. And once it has no basis to stand on at all, how could the idea of "nothing" itself remain fixed before the mind? ^9-33

And when neither "something" nor "nothing" remains fixed before the mind any longer, since there's no other alternative left to grasp onto, the mind rests with nothing left to focus on at all - complete, genuine peace. ^9-34

Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-granting tree fulfills every request made of it, the buddhas' magnificent forms appear in the world because of the aspirations and needs of those who follow their path. ^9-35

The craftsman who built a certain protective pillar passed away soon after blessing it, and yet the pillar still continues to neutralize poison long after his death. ^9-36

In just the same way, a bodhisattva who follows the path to enlightenment builds this kind of "protective pillar" and then passes into final peace - yet continues to accomplish beneficial deeds long afterward. ^9-37

"But how can making offerings to someone who no longer has a mind actually produce any real result?" This has been taught to work the very same way, whether the buddha is presently alive or has already passed into final peace. ^9-38

According to the scriptures, real results occur whether we're speaking on the everyday level or the level of ultimate truth - just as, for example, there would still be a real result from making an offering to a living buddha. ^9-39

"One can already be freed just by directly seeing the four truths of suffering - so why bother seeing emptiness as well?" Because, as the scriptures teach, there is no true enlightenment possible without following this particular path. ^9-40

"If the Great Vehicle teachings aren't proven to be authentic, then how are your own scriptures proven to be authentic either?" "Because they're accepted as authentic by both of us." But they weren't accepted as authentic by you from the very start either. ^9-41

The very same reasons you use to justify believing your scriptures apply equally well to the Great Vehicle teachings. And if something is true simply because two parties happen to agree on it, then even the ancient scriptures of the Brahmins would have to be considered true as well. ^9-42

If your objection is that the Great Vehicle is disputed by others, then you should reject your own scriptures too, since they are also disputed by non-Buddhists - just as certain teachings are disputed between you and other Buddhist schools as well. ^9-43

The very foundation of the teachings is the life of a fully ordained monastic, and that life is genuinely difficult to sustain. For minds still fixed on some object of focus, reaching lasting peace is difficult as well. ^9-44

If someone is freed from suffering simply by letting go of their negative emotions, that liberation should happen right away. And yet we still see the power of past actions affecting even those who no longer have negative emotions. ^9-45

"If it's certain, at that point, that they no longer have the underlying cause - craving - then why don't they also lack the more subtle habitual tendency of craving, in the same way that even confusion itself must eventually be uprooted?" ^9-46

Ordinary craving depends on the condition of feeling, and such people still do have feeling. A mind still focused on some object continues to dwell on one thing or another. ^9-47

Just as with a certain meditative state free of ordinary thought, a mind lacking a true realization of emptiness might come to a temporary stop, but it will eventually arise again - so it's essential to meditate on emptiness itself. ^9-48

If you accept as the Buddha's genuine teaching whatever words are found within your own scriptures, then don't you have to accept that the Great Vehicle scriptures are, for the most part, essentially the same as yours? ^9-49

And if you say all of the Great Vehicle teachings are flawed simply because a single one of your scriptures isn't included among them, then since at least one of your scriptures is essentially identical to ours, why wouldn't all of it also count as the Buddha's genuine teaching? ^9-50

Just because even a great disciple like Mahakashyapa and others weren't able to fully fathom these teachings, who's to say they shouldn't still be accepted as genuine? ^9-51

Free from the extremes of both craving and fear, remaining within this cycle of rebirth for the sake of those who suffer through their own confusion - this is precisely the fruit of realizing emptiness. ^9-52

The various objections raised against the view of emptiness don't hold up under real scrutiny. So don't hold onto any doubts, and go ahead and meditate on emptiness. ^9-53

Emptiness is the true remedy for the darkness caused both by mistaken views and by negative emotions. So wouldn't anyone who genuinely seeks complete, all-knowing wisdom want to cultivate it as quickly as possible? ^9-54

If fear arises from the very things that actually cause suffering, then why does emptiness - which is the true cure for suffering - provoke fear instead? ^9-55

If there really were some fixed, independent "me," then there could be genuine reason to fear all sorts of things. But since no such fixed self actually exists at all, who exactly is left to be afraid? ^9-56

Teeth, hair, and nails are not "me." Bones and blood are not "me" either. Mucus is not "me," and neither is phlegm, nor lymph, nor pus. ^9-57

Fat is not "me"; sweat is not "me"; neither are the lungs or liver "me," nor are any of the other internal organs. Feces and urine are not "me" either. ^9-58

Muscle, skin, and body heat are not "me," nor is the breath. The body's openings are not "me," and none of the six types of sensory awareness are "me" either. ^9-59

If the awareness that hears sound were something permanent and unchanging, it would have to be perceiving sound at all times. But when there's nothing there to be known, what exactly is doing the knowing? And why would we even call it "awareness" at that point? ^9-60

If you say it's an awareness that isn't actually aware of anything, then a block of wood would have to count as a kind of awareness too. So it must be certain that there is simply no awareness present when there's nothing there to be known. ^9-61

And if that very same awareness that perceives visual form is what also hears sound, then why doesn't it hear sound at the very same moment it sees form? If you say that's because there's no sound present at that moment, then in that case, there's no awareness of sound present either. ^9-62

How could something whose nature is to perceive sound also become the very thing that perceives visual form? One might think of a single person as both a parent and a child at once, but that doesn't reflect how things truly, independently exist. ^9-63

In just the same way, the three basic qualities of nature that some philosophers describe - goodness, activity, and inertia - can't truly be both parent and child at once either. So this same awareness can't really be understood as something with a single, unified nature that perceives sound. ^9-64

If, like a skilled actor playing different roles, this awareness simply appears in different forms, then it isn't actually permanent and unchanging at all. And if these different appearances were all genuinely one and the same thing, then that kind of oneness would be entirely unprecedented and doesn't match anything we actually observe. ^9-65

But if you say these different appearances aren't ultimately true at all, then please tell me: what exactly is this awareness's own true nature? If you say, "It's simply consciousness itself, plain and undifferentiated," then it would follow that all people are ultimately just one single thing. ^9-66

It would even follow that living, sentient things and lifeless, non-sentient things are ultimately the same thing too, since their basic existence would be identical in this view. And once you say that their individual differences aren't ultimately real, what basis is left for claiming they're even similar to one another? ^9-67

Something that is non-sentient obviously cannot be a "self," since it has no mind of its own, just like a clay jug. But if you say it becomes aware simply by being joined together with mind, then it would follow that this non-sentient thing ceases to exist as such the moment it's joined with mind. ^9-68

If there's truly no change at all in this fixed self, then what could awareness possibly do for it or add to it? In this way, you've effectively turned the supposed "self" into something as unaware and inert as empty space. ^9-69

"But without a fixed self, the whole relationship between an action and its result would make no sense at all. Since the person who acts is gone the moment the act is finished, whose action would it even be?" you ask. ^9-70

We both already agree that the basis for the action and the basis for its later result are genuinely distinct from one another in time, and that no fixed, unchanging self is what actually performs or receives them. So isn't this whole debate ultimately beside the point? ^9-71

It's simply not possible to observe a case where the very same person who creates the cause is also, at that same instant, the one who experiences the result. Instead, the relationship between someone who acts and someone who later experiences the result is explained simply in terms of belonging to a single, continuous stream of moments. ^9-72

A past moment of mind and a future moment of mind are not truly "me," since neither one actually exists right now. And if the mind that has just arisen in this very moment were truly "me," then once that moment has passed, this "me" would no longer exist either. ^9-73

Just as when you cut apart the trunk of a banana tree layer by layer, you find nothing solid at its core, in exactly the same way, when carefully examined and analyzed, this "me" turns out to have no real, independent existence either. ^9-74

"But if sentient beings don't ultimately exist at all, then for whom should we even bother cultivating compassion?" For those beings who appear through the workings of confusion - beings we still recognize and accept for the sake of achieving real, beneficial results. ^9-75

"But whose result would that compassion even bring about, if there are no truly existing beings to receive it?" That's true - the wish itself does arise out of this same underlying confusion. But for the sake of actually ending suffering, don't reject this kind of confusion when it comes to caring about the outcome. ^9-76

This same confused clinging to a fixed self only increases the very root cause of suffering - our attachment to "me." "But," you might say, "there's simply no way to stop that." Yet cultivating a clear understanding of selflessness is, in fact, the very best way to do exactly that. ^9-77

The body isn't the feet or the calves, and it isn't the thighs or the hips either. It isn't the stomach or the back, and it isn't the chest or the arms. ^9-78

It isn't the ribs, the hands, the armpits, or the shoulders, and it isn't any of the internal organs either. The head and throat are not the body - so then, what exactly is "the body" here, if not simply a label for all these parts together? ^9-79

Now, if the body were said to exist only partially within each of these different parts, then each part would simply contain its own portion of itself - so where, in that case, would the body as a whole actually reside on its own? ^9-80

But if a single, complete "body" were fully present in the hands and every other part, then since there are many parts - hands and all the rest - you'd end up with just as many separate "bodies" as there are parts. ^9-81

If "body" doesn't exist outside its parts or within any single part, then how could it possibly reside among its parts at all? And since it isn't something separate from its parts, how can you say it exists as its own real thing? ^9-82

So there really is no independent "body." It's simply through a kind of illusion that we look at hands and other parts arranged together and form the idea of "a body" - just as a pile of stones, arranged in a certain shape, might be mistaken for a person. ^9-83

As long as the right conditions come together, that pile of stones will appear to be a person. In the very same way, as long as all the parts are present together, a "body" will appear to be there too. ^9-84

In the same way, since a hand is really just a collection of fingers, the hand itself has no independent existence either. And the fingers themselves are made of joints, and each joint is made up of still smaller parts. ^9-85

Those smaller parts break down further into tiny particles, and even those particles can be divided according to direction and side. These fragments, too, have no truly partless core - they're just like empty space. So in the end, even particles have no independent existence. ^9-86

All physical form, then, is like something seen in a dream - so who, upon investigating this closely, would remain attached to it? The body, in this way, has no independent existence at all - so what, then, is truly "male" and what is truly "female"? ^9-87

If suffering itself were truly, independently real, why wouldn't it be completely wiped out by joy? And if pleasure were truly real in the same way, why wouldn't a pleasant taste bring comfort and delight to someone in the depths of agony? ^9-88

If a feeling fails to be experienced because it's overwhelmed by something stronger at the time, then how can we rightly call it a "feeling" at all, given that it isn't actually being felt? ^9-89

Perhaps you'll say that only a subtler form of the pain remains, its more obvious form simply overpowered by something else - or that it's now experienced as mere pleasure instead. But even this "subtle" version is still just what it always was. ^9-90

And if discomfort simply fails to appear because its opposite happens to be present at the time, then isn't the whole claim that "this is a feeling" really nothing more than a label we've mentally added on? ^9-91

Since this is so, the true remedy for suffering is meditation and careful examination of exactly this kind. This kind of focused reflection is, in truth, the very food and sustenance of serious meditators. ^9-92

If there's a gap between a sense faculty and its object, how could the two of them ever actually meet? And if there's no gap at all between them, then they'd simply be one and the same thing - so what, in that case, is even meeting what? ^9-93

One tiny particle cannot truly penetrate into another, since both are equally without any real volume of their own. But if they can't penetrate one another, they can't truly merge either - and if they can't merge, there's no real encounter between them at all. ^9-94

How, then, could anyone accept that something with no parts at all could ever be said to "meet" anything else? Show me, if you've ever actually observed it, a genuine case of two truly partless things making contact. ^9-95

Consciousness itself has no physical substance, so it doesn't make sense to speak of it making "contact" with anything in a literal sense. And as we've already shown, a mere combination of parts has no independent reality of its own either. ^9-96

So if there's no real touch or contact happening anywhere in this process, where exactly does "feeling" actually come from? What, then, is the point of all this effort - and what exactly is torturing what? ^9-97

Since there's no truly independent subject who experiences a sensation, and the sensation itself has no independent existence either, how could craving possibly persist once all of this becomes clear? ^9-98

What we see and what we touch is really no different from the substance of dreams and mirages. If a feeling truly arose together with the very consciousness that perceives it, then it would follow that this feeling could never actually be perceived by that consciousness at all. ^9-99

If one arises first and the other comes after, then all we really have is a memory afterward, not a direct experience of the sensation itself in the moment. A sensation, in truth, doesn't perceive itself, nor is it perceived by some other separate thing. ^9-100

Since there is no truly independent self who experiences a sensation, the sensation itself has no independent existence either. So what real harm, then, can this sensation actually do to this collection of parts, which has no independent self to begin with? ^9-101

The mind doesn't truly reside within the senses themselves, nor does it exist out among external things like physical form. And it doesn't exist somewhere in between either - the mind cannot truly be pinned down as being outside, inside, or anywhere else at all. ^9-102

It isn't located within the body, yet it isn't located anywhere else either - it neither merges completely with the body nor stands entirely apart from it. Something described this way simply doesn't exist as any fixed, independent thing at all. Beings, by their very nature, are already beyond the reach of any truly independent suffering. ^9-103

If consciousness arises before the object it perceives, then what exactly does it arise in relation to at that point? And if consciousness arises at the very same instant as its object, then again, what exactly is it arising in relation to? ^9-104

And if consciousness arises only after its object, then once again, what does it actually arise from? So, in truth, the very origin of all things and experiences lies entirely beyond what ordinary understanding can fully grasp. ^9-105

"If that's the case," you say, "then there's no everyday, conventional reality at all - so what becomes of the two truths, the conventional and the ultimate? And besides, if conventional reality only comes from beings' confused minds, how could beings ever actually become free of their suffering?" ^9-106

But that's simply a misunderstanding of what others actually intend - it isn't what I mean by "conventional reality" at all. If further thoughts continue afterward, conventional reality still continues along with them; if they don't, conventional reality has, in that sense, truly ceased. ^9-107

The analyzing mind and the thing being analyzed are simply linked together, each depending on the other. It's entirely on the basis of everyday, shared agreement that any of this analysis can even be expressed in the first place. ^9-108

"But," you say, "if we then turn around and analyze this very process of analysis itself, that analysis, too, could be analyzed all over again - leading to an endless chain with no real stopping point." ^9-109

But once something has genuinely been fully analyzed in this way, there's no remaining basis left for further analysis to stand on. And once the object being examined has dissolved in this way, the examining mind settles too - and that, precisely, is what is called lasting peace. ^9-110

Those who claim that both consciousness and its objects are equally, ultimately real find it very hard to actually defend that position. Because if consciousness is what reveals the truth of things, then on what basis, in turn, does consciousness itself truly exist? ^9-111

"If known objects prove that consciousness exists, then what exactly proves that those objects themselves exist?" If you say the two simply depend on each other to exist, then, by that very reasoning, both of them lose any claim to truly independent existence. ^9-112

Just as a man cannot truly be called "a father" without a son, and there's no way to say where such a son could even come from without a father, in exactly the same way, mind and its object have no truly independent existence of their own. ^9-113

"A sprout," you say, "grows from a seed, and through the sprout we can infer that a seed once existed. In just the same way, consciousness arising from its object proves that the object exists - how could it be otherwise?" ^9-114

But it's a consciousness genuinely separate from the sprout itself that lets us infer the existence of the seed. So what, in this case, actually proves that consciousness itself exists, in order for it to prove the existence of its object in turn? ^9-115

In everyday, ordinary experience, we recognize that everything has some cause behind it - just as the many different parts of a single lotus flower arise from a whole variety of different causes and conditions. ^9-116

"But what gives rise," you might ask, "to this whole variety of causes in the first place?" An even earlier variety of causes, we would answer. "And how, then, do causes actually produce their results?" Simply through the power carried forward from those earlier causes, we reply. ^9-117

If a Creator God is said to be the ultimate cause of all beings, then you'll need to explain to us exactly what this God's true nature is. If, by "God," you simply mean the basic physical elements of the world, then there's little point in arguing over mere names. ^9-118

Yet earth and the other basic elements are many in number, impermanent, without awareness, and not divine in themselves. Trampled underfoot and treated as ordinary matter, they clearly cannot be an all-powerful, sacred God. ^9-119

This God can't simply be empty space either, since space is inert and produces nothing on its own. And he can't be a fixed, independent self, since we've already shown that no such self exists. If you say he's simply beyond ordinary thought, then his supposed role as "creator" becomes equally beyond ordinary thought too - so what real point is there in even making such a claim? ^9-120

What exactly would such a God even want to create? Would he have to create the self and all the physical elements too? But if the self and the elements and this God himself are all said to be eternal and unchanging, that clashes with what we've already shown: that consciousness only arises depending on its object. ^9-121

Pleasure and pain, since beginningless time, have always arisen from the working of cause and effect - so tell us, what exactly has this God's divine power actually produced? And if there is no true starting point in the cause, how could its effects have any real starting point either? ^9-122

Why, then, aren't beings simply being created by this God at every single moment, continuously, if he truly relies on nothing at all outside himself? And if there's nothing whatsoever that he hasn't already made, what could possibly remain for him to depend on in the act of creating? ^9-123

If this God does depend on something else in order to create, then the true cause of everything is really just the coming together of the right conditions, not this God himself. When those conditions are present, he creates without being able to help it; when they're absent, he's simply powerless to create anything at all. ^9-124

If an all-powerful God creates without any actual intention to do so, then something else must be forcing him to act. And if he creates because he wants to, then he's simply being driven by his own desire - so in what sense, then, is he truly "all-powerful" as Creator? ^9-125

Those who claim that tiny particles are permanent and unchanging have already been refuted earlier in this analysis. As for the Samkhya philosophers, they hold that an eternal, unchanging substance they call prakriti is the true cause of this ever-changing universe. ^9-126

According to them, what we call "pleasure," "pain," and "neutral feeling" are three basic qualities which, when perfectly balanced and at rest, are what they call prakriti itself. The universe, they say, comes into being only once that balance is disturbed. ^9-127

But it doesn't make sense for three distinct natures to combine into a single, unified thing, and so prakriti, understood this way, cannot truly exist. And these same three supposed qualities can't truly exist either, since each one, on closer analysis, would itself have to consist of all three again. ^9-128

And if these qualities don't truly exist, then it becomes very implausible to say that something like sound is made of them at all. Likewise, cloth and other things with no mind of their own simply cannot be the true seat of feelings like pleasure. ^9-129

"But," you say, "these things simply carry the nature of their underlying cause." Yet haven't we already examined and refuted the very idea of "things" having such fixed natures? For you, the cause is said to be "pleasure" itself and the like - yet cloth has clearly never actually sprung directly from pleasure. ^9-130

If anything, it's pleasure that arises from wearing the cloth, not the other way around. And if that pleasure doesn't exist independently on its own, then this supposed permanence of pleasure and the other qualities turns out to be something that has simply never actually been observed. ^9-131

If pleasure and the other qualities were genuinely, constantly present in an obvious way, then why aren't they always perceived as such? And if you say they simply take on a more subtle, hidden form at such times, then how can the very same thing be both obviously present and subtly hidden at once? ^9-132

If the obvious, "gross" form is set aside and only the subtle form remains, then neither the gross nor the subtle form can really be considered permanent at all. So why not simply accept that, in this same way, everything without exception has this same changing, impermanent character? ^9-133

And if this so-called "gross" form of pleasure is really nothing other than pleasure itself, then it's clear that pleasure itself must be impermanent too. If you insist instead that something with absolutely no existence at all could never actually appear or manifest, ^9-134

then, even though you've already denied that anything can newly arise from what didn't exist before, that's exactly what you're claiming again here. And if you insist that results must already exist within their causes beforehand, then it would follow, absurdly, that anyone who eats food is really just eating their own future waste. ^9-135

By that same logic, instead of buying cloth to wear, people should just buy raw cotton seeds and wear those instead! "But," you say, "ordinary people are simply ignorant and don't see things clearly. This deeper truth is instead taught by those who truly understand reality." ^9-136

But then this same deeper knowledge should be available to ordinary people too - and if they do have it, why don't they actually perceive things this way? And if you say that what ordinary people clearly perceive has no real validity at all, then you're really just saying that their entire clear experience of the world is false. ^9-137

"But," you might object, "if there's no genuine validity even in what we consider valid knowledge, doesn't that mean everything such knowledge assesses is also false? And in that case, it becomes pointless to even meditate on emptiness as some kind of ultimate reality." ^9-138

But if there's truly no independent object there to begin with for us to analyze in the first place, then there's also nothing genuine for us to grasp on to as being "nonexistent" either. So whatever illusory objects there may be, their apparent nonexistence is, in exactly the same way, just as illusory as they are. ^9-139

Consider, for instance, someone who dreams that their child has died: the later thought, "My child is gone," simply replaces the earlier thought, "My child is alive." And yet both of these thoughts, dreamed or not, are equally illusory in the first place. ^9-140

So, as we can see through this whole line of investigation, nothing exists at all without some cause behind it. And yet nothing exists as some independent thing already sitting within its causes either - not within any single cause, nor even within the whole collection of causes taken together. ^9-141

This apparent thing doesn't come from anywhere else, nor does it stay put anywhere, nor does it truly go anywhere when it ceases. So how, in the end, is what our own confusion mistakes for reality actually any different from a simple mirage? ^9-142

Whether something is conjured up by a magician's spell or brought about through ordinary causes and conditions, in either case we should really ask: where did it actually come from, and where does it actually go? ^9-143

Something that only appears when the right conditions happen to come together, and disappears the moment they're absent, is not truly, independently real - it's just like a reflection appearing in a mirror. How, then, could we ever attribute genuine, independent existence to it? ^9-144

What real need would there even be for a cause, if the result already existed independently and truly on its own? And, by the very same reasoning, what real need is there for a cause to explain something that, in truth, doesn't independently exist at all? ^9-145

Even given a hundred million different causes, nothing genuinely nonexistent could ever be turned into something existent. Because in that state of "nonexistence," how could "existent things" possibly emerge from it at all? And into what, exactly, would this so-called nonexistent thing even be transforming? ^9-146

Since something cannot truly come into being while it remains genuinely nonexistent, there's simply no moment at which such an "existing thing" could ever actually arise this way. And since these apparent entities never truly arise as independent things in the first place, there's likewise no independent "nonentity" for them to depart into either. ^9-147

And if this state of "nonentity" is never truly dispelled in an independent sense, there's no real opportunity for an "entity" to independently arise from it either. Nor can an "entity" simply transform into a "nonentity," since that would require it to hold two contradictory natures at once. ^9-148

So, in truth, there is no independent arising of things, and likewise no independent ceasing of them either. And in exactly the same way, all beings, every single one of them, are ultimately without any true, independent origin, and never truly cease to exist either. ^9-149

Wandering beings, understood this way, resemble nothing more than a dream - or, if you examine closely, the layers of a banana tree trunk, with no solid core at its center. In ultimate reality, there is no real difference at all to be found between the state of suffering and the state that lies beyond all suffering. ^9-150

Given that all things, understood this way, are ultimately empty of independent existence, what is there really to gain, and what is there really to lose? Who, in that case, is there to honor and respect me, and who is there to scorn and insult me? ^9-151

Where, really, do pleasure and sorrow even come from in the first place? What is there, ultimately, to bring me joy, and what to cause me pain? And if I search for the true, ultimate nature of all of this, who exactly is doing the craving, and what exactly is being craved? ^9-152

Examine closely this whole world of living beings: who, in ultimate truth, is there to actually pass away? What is there that is yet to come, and what has already been? And who, in the end, are truly "relatives" and "friends" in any fixed, independent sense? ^9-153

May beings just like myself come to fully understand and truly grasp that all things share this same nature, empty and open like space itself! Yet in the meantime, those still seeking their own comfort and ease, whether through conflict or through worldly pleasures, ^9-154

remain deeply troubled at times, and thrilled with excitement at others. They suffer, strain, and struggle against one another, wounding and hurting each other in countless ways - living out their lives caught up in wrongdoing and hardship. ^9-155

From time to time they rise up into states of temporary happiness, losing themselves in all sorts of pleasures. But when they die, they fall back down into states of prolonged, unbearable torment. ^9-156

There are so many pitfalls and abysses within this cycle of existence, places where the true nature of reality is nowhere to be found. Everything here is contradiction and confusion - the true nature of reality in this world is simply not what it appears to be. ^9-157

Here, beyond what words can fully capture, lies a boundless, shoreless sea of unbearable suffering. Here, our own strength and resilience are so limited, and our lives flicker by so briefly and fragilely. ^9-158

So much of life is spent simply trying to survive and stay healthy, relieving hunger and exhaustion, sleeping away the hours, dealing with accidents and injuries, and forming shallow, unreliable friendships with those who are still untrained in genuine wisdom - ^9-159

and so life slips by quickly, with so little real meaning gained from it. True insight and understanding are genuinely hard to come by - so how, then, will we ever find a way to rein in the mind's endless, pointless wandering? ^9-160

What's more, harmful forces work hard, straining constantly, to drag us down into states of misery and loss. All around us are countless false and misleading paths, and it is genuinely difficult to clear away our own doubts and confusion. ^9-161

It is hard enough just to find this precious state of freedom and opportunity again, and even harder still to encounter truly enlightened teachers along the way. And harder yet is it to turn back the powerful current of our own negative emotions! How sad it is that our sorrows keep pouring down upon us in such endless streams! ^9-162

How sad it truly is that living beings, swept along by this flood of bitter suffering, remain, even in the midst of such terrible circumstances, largely unaware of just how much they are suffering! ^9-163

They are like people who bathe themselves again and again in cold water, only to then turn around and scorch themselves with fire. They suffer greatly in this way, and yet they still insist, loudly and proudly, that they are perfectly happy. ^9-164

In just the same way, there are those who go about their lives acting as though old age and death will simply never come for them. But first they are struck down by death, and only then comes the dreadful, sudden fall into states of great loss and suffering. ^9-165

When will I finally be able to calm and quench this terrible, blazing fire of suffering, pouring down abundant rains of my own true happiness, drawn from the gathered clouds of goodness I have built up? ^9-166

And having gathered this wealth of genuine goodness, offered respectfully and without clinging to any fixed idea of it, when will I finally be able to reveal this truth of emptiness to those who are bringing about their own ruin through their belief in the fixed, independent reality of things? ^9-167

## 10. Dedication ^10-0

Through all the goodness I have now gathered by writing this book, which speaks of entering the bodhisattva's way, may every single being walk the path toward full enlightenment. ^10-1

May all beings everywhere who suffer torment in body and mind find, through the power of my own goodness, joy and happiness beyond all measure. ^10-2

For as long as they remain within this cycle of rebirth, may their joy never diminish. May they continually taste the very finest happiness, without a single break. ^10-3

Throughout every corner and reach of the universe, in however many hellish states there may be, may the beings dwelling there instead experience the bliss and peace of the pure land of perfect happiness. ^10-4

May those trapped in freezing ice be warmed, and may great clouds of bodhisattvas send down endless, gentle rains to cool those burning in the fires of hell. ^10-5

May forests of blade-like leaves become sweet, pleasant groves instead. And may wonderful, miraculous trees appear in place of the terrible tree of hell. ^10-6

May the very depths of hell become sweet instead, filled with fragrant pools scented like lotus blossoms, beautiful with the calls of swans, geese, and waterfowl, pleasant to hear. ^10-7

May burning coals turn into heaps of jewels, may the scorching ground become smooth as crystal, and may crushing mountains become magnificent dwelling places - temples and homes fit for the buddhas themselves. ^10-8

May the rain of weapons, molten rock, and burning stones become instead a gentle rain of flowers. And may all the mutual wounding done with sharp blades become nothing more than a playful shower of petals. ^10-9

And may those trapped in the terrible river of hell, their flesh destroyed and their bones bleached white as jasmine petals, through the power of all my goodness, take on beautiful, radiant forms and play joyfully with heavenly companions in peaceful, flowing streams. ^10-10

"What is this fear that grips death's own agents, and these frightful vultures and crows?" they'll ask. "What noble strength is it that brings us such joy and drives away this dreadful darkness?" And looking up, they'll see the radiant form of Vajrapani. May their wrongdoing then dissolve in joy, and may they go to him. ^10-11

And when they see the raging, molten flood of hell suddenly extinguished by a rain of flowers, soaked instead in fragrant streams, filled at once with wonder and bliss, they'll ask, "How can this be?" May the beings in hell then behold Avalokiteshvara, the one who holds the lotus. ^10-12

"Friends, cast aside your fear and quickly gather here! Who is this who comes to banish our dread - this radiant, youthful being with hair bound up, this loving bodhisattva who saves and protects every being, whose power relieves all suffering and grants true joy?" ^10-13

"Look - a hundred gods bow their crowned heads before his feet! Flowers rain down upon him, his eyes filled with warm compassion, and the splendor of his presence echoes with the praises of a thousand heavenly beings!" May those in hell cry out like this upon seeing Manjughosha. ^10-14

And likewise, through the power of my own goodness, may all those suffering in hell, upon seeing pure bodhisattvas like Samantabhadra - like clouds of bliss pouring down cool, fragrant rain - come at last to complete and perfect joy. ^10-15

And may animals living in fear of being hunted and eaten by one another be freed from that fear entirely. And may hungry, suffering spirits find the same abundance and joy as those living in the most fortunate of realms. ^10-16

And may they be fully nourished and satisfied by streams of milk flowing from the hand of the noble Avalokiteshvara, and may bathing in it bring them comfort and refreshment. ^10-17

And may the blind be able to see, and may the deaf be able to hear. And may women who are close to giving birth do so as easily and painlessly as Queen Mayadevi, the Buddha's own mother. ^10-18

And may the naked be clothed, and may the hungry be fully fed. And may those suffering from thirst receive pure, delicious water to drink. ^10-19

May the poor and destitute find true wealth. May the exhausted and the careworn find real joy. And may those in despair find wholeness of mind, along with lasting strength and steadiness. ^10-20

May every being suffering from illness be instantly freed from every ailment. And may every kind of sickness that afflicts the living disappear from the world completely and forever. ^10-21

May those living in fear be free of fear. May prisoners be released and set free. May the weak find true strength. And may all beings treat one another with kindness and helpfulness. ^10-22

May travelers on every road find happiness wherever they go, and may they reach whatever they set their hearts on, without needless struggle. ^10-23

May those setting out to sea in boats or ships reach the harbors they long for, and may they arrive safely on shore, joyfully reunited with their loved ones. ^10-24

May those who have lost their way in the wilderness find fellow travelers to guide them. And, safe from the danger of thieves and wild animals, may their journey be light and untiring. ^10-25

May children, the elderly, and all those without protection, wandering through frightening and unmarked places, along with those who drift into danger unaware, be watched over by kind, protective spirits. ^10-26

May all beings be freed from every form of bondage, and may they be blessed with wisdom, faith, and love. With perfect nourishment and good conduct, may they always remember their past lives clearly. ^10-27

May everyone enjoy limitless abundance, as vast and inexhaustible as open space itself, and may they enjoy it exactly as they wish, free from any harm or ill will toward others. ^10-28

May beings who feel dull or unremarkable become radiant and full of light. And may those who suffer from illness or disfigurement gain true beauty and wholeness. ^10-29

May every woman in the world, if she wishes, gain the strength and standing so often unjustly reserved for men. And may the lowly rise to true excellence, while the proud and arrogant let go of their conceit. ^10-30

And so, through all the goodness I have gathered, may every being, leaving no one out, let go of harmful ways entirely and embrace goodness from now on and always. ^10-31

May they never be separated from their wish to help all beings, and may they continually engage in the bodhisattva's way of acting. May they be welcomed as true students by the buddhas, and may they turn away completely from harmful influences. ^10-32

And may every one of these beings enjoy an unmatched, full lifespan. Living always in contentment, may even the very word "death" become unfamiliar to them. ^10-33

In every direction, on every side, may groves of wish-fulfilling trees flourish, echoing with the sweet sound of the teachings, spoken by the buddhas and their bodhisattva children. ^10-34

And may the earth everywhere become wholesome and gentle - free of boulders, cliffs, and chasms - flat and even like the palm of a hand, and smooth like the finest polished stone. ^10-35

For the many circles of students gathered to learn, may countless bodhisattvas dwell in every land, adorning each one with every kind of excellence. ^10-36

From birdsong, from the rustling of trees, from beams of light, and from the sky itself, may every single being come to hear the constant, gentle sound of the true teachings. ^10-37

And may they always come into the presence of the buddhas, and meet with the bodhisattvas, their spiritual children. With boundless clouds of offerings, may these teachers of the world be honored. ^10-38

May kindly spirits bring the rains at just the right time, so that harvests are rich and plentiful. May rulers govern according to what is right and good, and may the whole world be blessed with true prosperity. ^10-39

May medicines be truly effective, and may words of healing and protection be spoken with success. May spirits that feed on harm instead become kind, their minds filled with compassion. ^10-40

May no being ever suffer anguish. Let none be sick, and let none act harmfully. May no one feel fear or suffer insult, and may every mind always be free from sorrow. ^10-41

In monasteries, temples, and similar places, may study and recitation flourish widely. May harmony prevail within every spiritual community, and may all its good aims be fulfilled. ^10-42

May ordained practitioners, committed to their practice, find truly peaceful places for quiet retreat, let go of every wandering thought, and meditate with minds that are well trained and clear. ^10-43

May nuns have everything they need provided for them, and may conflict and ill will become unfamiliar to them. And may all who have taken up the monastic life uphold their commitments purely and without compromise. ^10-44

May those who have broken their commitments feel genuine regret, and may they always strive to correct their mistakes. And through this, may they gain a fortunate rebirth in which to practice with a clear, unbroken commitment. ^10-45

May the wise and learned be honored, and may they always be well supported through the generosity of others. May their minds remain pure, and may their good reputation spread far and wide. ^10-46

May beings never languish in the lower realms, and may hardship and suffering become unknown to them. With bodies even more magnificent than the gods, may they reach full enlightenment without delay. ^10-47

May beings, again and again, make offerings to all the buddhas, and with the boundless joy of the buddhas themselves, may they enjoy undimmed and lasting happiness. ^10-48

May all the bodhisattvas now fulfill their noble intentions for the sake of all wandering beings. And may every being receive exactly what their guardians wish for them. ^10-49

And may those following the paths of personal liberation also find their own complete and perfect happiness. ^10-50

And until, through Manjughosha's perfect kindness, I reach the first true stage of a bodhisattva's realization - the stage called Perfect Joy - may I remember all my past lives, and may I always enter into a life of genuine spiritual practice. ^10-51

May I live sustained by simple, ordinary food and provisions, and in every life, may I find a dwelling place of true, peaceful solitude. ^10-52

Whenever I wish to see him, or to ask him even the smallest question, may I behold, without any obstruction, my own guide and protector, Manjughosha. ^10-53

To meet the needs of every being dwelling in all directions, to the very edges of the sky, may I reflect, in everything I do, the perfect example set by Manjushri. ^10-54

And now, for as long as space itself endures, and for as long as there are beings still to be found, may I too remain, in just the same way, working to drive away the sorrows of the world. ^10-55

May all the pain and sorrow of every wandering being ripen and fall entirely upon myself instead. And may the noble company of bodhisattvas always bring about true happiness for all beings. ^10-56

May these teachings - the only true cure for suffering, the very source of every happiness and joy - be sustained with the resources they need, upheld with genuine respect, and may they endure across a vast span of time. ^10-57

And now I bow down to Manjughosha, whose kindness is the very wellspring of my good intentions. And I bow as well to all my virtuous friends and teachers, whose inspiration gave me the strength to grow. ^10-58

This brings the tenth chapter of Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva, "Dedication," to an end. ^10-a

## The Translators' Colophon ^b-0

This text was first translated and established in Tibetan, based on a manuscript from Kashmir, by the Indian teacher Sarvajnadeva together with the chief editor and translator Bandé Paltsek. ^b-1

Later, it was revised and re-translated to match a manuscript and commentary from central India by the Indian teacher Dharmashribhadra together with the chief editors and translators Bandé Rinchen Zangpo and Shakya Lodrö. ^b-2

And later still, it was corrected, revised, and re-translated, and so given its finest, most polished form, by the Indian teacher Sumatikirti together with the chief editor and translator, the monk Loden Sherab. ^b-3
