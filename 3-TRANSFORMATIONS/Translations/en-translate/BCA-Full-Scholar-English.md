# A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life
### Scholarly English Translation
Homage to the Buddha. ^I-1

*Zero-shot translation from the Tibetan of Blo ldan shes rab, cross-checked against the Sanskrit of Śāntideva*

## Chapter 1: The Benefits of Bodhicitta

With reverence I bow to the Sugatas, to the Dharma that is their dharmakāya, to their heirs the bodhisattvas, and to all who are worthy of homage; and following the scriptures, I shall set forth in brief the manner of entering the bodhisattva's vow. ^1-1

Nothing said here is unprecedented, nor do I possess skill in poetic composition; therefore I have no thought of benefitting others — I have undertaken this work only to cultivate my own mind. ^1-2

Yet through composing this, the force of my faith in virtue may for a time increase; and if others of fortune equal to mine should read it, perhaps some purpose may be served. ^1-3

This life of freedom and endowment is exceedingly difficult to obtain; if I do not accomplish the aims of a capable person now, when will such favorable conditions arise again? ^1-4

Just as on a dark, cloudy night a flash of lightning illumines all for an instant, so too, through the power of the buddhas, merit and insight in the world arise but rarely and fleetingly. ^1-5

Thus virtue is ever slight, while misdeed is immensely powerful and unbearable; nothing can overpower it except complete bodhicitta. ^1-6

Across countless aeons the Jinas reflected deeply and saw that this alone is truly beneficial; through it boundless hosts of beings easily obtain supreme bliss. ^1-7

Whoever wishes to crush the hundreds of thousands of sufferings of saṃsāra, to dispel the distress of sentient beings, and to partake of the many hundreds of joys should never abandon bodhicitta. ^1-8

The moment bodhicitta is born, even a wretched being bound in saṃsāra's prison is called a child of the Sugatas, and gods and humans alike will pay homage. ^1-9

Like the supreme elixir that transforms base metal into gold, bodhicitta takes this impure body and makes of it the priceless form of a Victorious One; therefore hold fast to what is called bodhicitta. ^1-10

The sole guide of beings, with his boundless wisdom, has thoroughly examined it and found it precious; therefore those who wish to leave behind the states of saṃsāra should hold firmly to this precious bodhicitta. ^1-11

Every other virtue is like a plantain tree: having borne fruit, it is exhausted and withers away; but the tree of bodhicitta constantly bears fruit and flourishes without ever being depleted. ^1-12

Even one who has committed unbearable misdeeds is liberated in an instant by relying on bodhicitta — as one in great peril is saved by relying on a hero; why then should the careful not rely upon it? ^1-13

For it burns away great misdeeds in a single instant, like the fire at the end of an aeon; its immeasurable benefits were well expounded by the compassionate Maitreya to Sudhana. ^1-14

Bodhicitta is briefly summarized in two aspects to be understood: the mind aspiring to awakening, and engagement in awakening itself. ^1-15

As one should understand the distinction between wishing to go somewhere and actually going, so too the wise should understand in sequence the distinction between these two aspects of bodhicitta. ^1-16

From the mind aspiring to awakening great results arise even within saṃsāra; yet unlike the mind of engagement, it does not give rise to a continuous stream of merit. ^1-17

But from the moment one adopts that mind with genuine resolve — resolve never to turn back until one has liberated the immeasurable realm of sentient beings without exception — ^1-18

from that moment onward an unbroken current of merit flows forth, vast as the sky, continuing even while one sleeps or is heedless. ^1-19

This, together with its reasons, the Tathāgata himself declared when Subahu asked — for the sake of beings of lesser aspiration. ^1-20

Even the thought, "May I dispel the headache of sentient beings," if it is a thought of benefit, possesses immeasurable merit. ^1-21

What need then to speak of one who wishes to dispel the immeasurable suffering of each sentient being and to establish each in immeasurable qualities? ^1-22

Does any father or mother harbor such a wish? Do the gods? Do the great sages? Does even Brahmā? ^1-23

Those very beings have never, even in dreams for their own sake, entertained such a mind; how then could it arise for the sake of another? ^1-24

Since this does not arise even for their own sake in most beings, this precious jewel of a mind — the thought of benefitting others — is a wonder without precedent. ^1-25

It is the cause of joy for all beings and the antidote to sentient beings' suffering; how could the merit of this precious mind be measured? ^1-26

If even the mere thought of benefit surpasses worship of the buddhas, what need to speak of striving for the happiness of all sentient beings without exception? ^1-27

Though they wish to cast off suffering, they run headlong toward suffering; though they wish for happiness, through avidyā they destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy. ^1-28

Where is there a friend equal to one who fills with every joy those bereft of happiness and laden with suffering, who severs all their suffering, ^1-29

and who dispels their avidyā as well? Where is there merit equal to that? ^1-30

Even one who repays a kindness is for a time deemed praiseworthy; what need then to speak of a bodhisattva who acts for others' good though not entreated? ^1-31

If one prepares a morning meal for a few beings — giving but a moment's sustenance, grudgingly, and honored only for half a day — common people call that a virtuous deed. ^1-32

What need then to speak of one who forever bestows upon immeasurable sentient beings the unsurpassed bliss of the Sugatas, fulfilling every aspiration completely? ^1-33

The Victorious One declared that whoever harbors ill will toward such a generous heir of the Jinas will dwell in hellish states for as many aeons as the number of harmful thoughts produced. ^1-34

But if one's mind turns with supreme joy toward a bodhisattva, the result grows even greater than that; even when grave misfortune befalls them, bodhisattvas do not produce misdeeds — their virtue increases spontaneously. ^1-35

I bow to the person in whom this precious, sacred mind has arisen; I take refuge in this source of happiness — even one who harms it becomes connected with bliss. ^1-36

*Thus ends Chapter One: "The Benefits of Bodhicitta."* ^1-a

## Chapter 2: Confessing Wrongdoing

In order to hold fast to that precious bodhicitta, I make excellent offerings to the Tathāgatas, to the sublime Dharma — that flawless jewel of the Three Jewels — and to the heirs of the buddhas, who are like oceans of qualities. ^2-1

I offer every flower and fruit that exists, every kind of medicine, every jewel in the world, and every stretch of clear, delightful water. ^2-2

I offer jewel mountains and likewise the quiet, beautiful forests; trees adorned with blossoms, and branches bending under fine fruit. ^2-3

I offer the sweet scents of the heavens, incense, wish-fulfilling trees, and jewel trees; crops that grow without cultivation, and every other ornament worthy of offering. ^2-4

I offer lakes adorned with lotuses, where swans sing with exquisite voice; and everything, extending to the limits of boundless space — all that belongs to no possessor. ^2-5

Having taken all this up in mind, I offer it well to the supreme heirs of the Jinas together with the buddhas; O worthy objects of offering, great compassionate ones, look upon me with kindness and accept these gifts. ^2-6

I am devoid of merit and utterly destitute — no other offering wealth is mine; therefore, O protectors who intend others' good, accept this through your own power, for my sake. ^2-7

To the Victorious Ones and their heirs I offer my entire body forever; O supreme bodhisattvas, fully accept me — with reverence I shall become your servant. ^2-8

Now that you have fully accepted me, I shall not fear even in saṃsāra and shall work for sentient beings' welfare; I shall properly leave behind past misdeeds and shall not commit them again hereafter. ^2-9

Picture a bathing hall of exquisite fragrance, with a floor of clear, radiant crystal; its pillars blaze with jewels, and pearls hang shimmering from a canopy above. ^2-10

There, with many jeweled vases of perfumed water, and with song and music all around, I bathe the Tathāgatas and their heirs. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with peerless cloths, clean and richly scented; then I offer them robes of beautiful hue, sublime and fragrant. ^2-12

With fine, soft garments of every kind, and with hundreds of supreme ornaments, I adorn the exalted bodhisattvas — Samantabhadra, Mañjuśrī, Avalokiteśvara, and the rest. ^2-13

With the finest perfumes, whose fragrance pervades the trichiliocosm, I anoint the bodies of all the Jinas — bodies blazing like refined, burnished gold. ^2-14

To the Jinas, supreme objects of offering, I offer delightful flowers — mandārava, lotus, blue lotus, and every fragrant bloom — and well-woven, pleasing garlands. ^2-15

I offer clouds of the finest incense, whose captivating fragrance fills the air; and I offer every kind of divine food and drink. ^2-16

I offer rows of jeweled lamps set upon golden lotuses; and upon ground anointed with incense I scatter petals of beautiful flowers. ^2-17

I offer measureless palaces resounding with pleasing hymns of praise, hung with strings of pearls and jewels that adorn the sky — all this I offer to those whose very nature is compassion. ^2-18

I offer beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles, adorned on every side, raised high and graceful — these I offer always to the Jinas. ^2-19

And beyond all these, may there arise everywhere great clouds of offerings, filled with melodious music, that soothe the suffering of sentient beings and bring them peace. ^2-20

May a rain of jewels, flowers, and the like fall without cease upon the true Dharma, upon all the Three Jewels, upon every stūpa and every sacred image. ^2-21

Just as Mañjuśrī and the others once made offerings to the Victorious Ones, so too do I now make the same offerings to the Tathāgatas, the Protector, together with their heirs. ^2-22

With an ocean of branches of praise I praise these beings whose qualities are an ocean; may clouds of melodious praise arise before them again and again without fail. ^2-23

To all the buddhas of past, present, and future, together with the Dharma and the supreme saṅgha, I prostrate with as many bodies as there are particles of dust in the realms. ^2-24

I prostrate to the places where bodhicitta first arose, and to every stūpa; I prostrate to the abbots and preceptors, and to those who uphold the supreme discipline. ^2-25

Until I reach the heart of awakening I take refuge in the buddhas; likewise I take refuge in the Dharma and in the saṅgha of bodhisattvas. ^2-26

To the fully awakened buddhas abiding in every direction, to their great compassion, and to the bodhicitta arising in their disciples, I join my palms and make this supplication. ^2-27

Throughout beginningless saṃsāra, in this life and in others, whatever misdeeds I have committed myself, or caused others to commit, ^2-28

or whatever I have rejoiced in — overpowered by the delusion of avidyā — now that I see these faults, I confess them all from the depths of my heart before the Protector. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have caused through body, speech, or mind, driven by the gateway of the kleśas, to the Three Jewels, to my parents, or to any other teacher, ^2-30

whatever unbearable misdeeds I, one marked by many faults, have committed — all of this I confess now before every buddha who guides beings. ^2-31

Perhaps death will come before I have purified this misdeed; protect me quickly, I pray, that I may be surely liberated from it in time. ^2-32

Death is not to be trusted; it does not wait until one's work is done; whether one is well or ill, no one can rely on life — it is unpredictable. ^2-33

I must depart, leaving everything behind; not understanding this, I have committed misdeeds of many kinds for the sake of friends and enemies alike. ^2-34

My enemies will be no more; my friends will be no more; I too will be no more; likewise, everything will be no more. ^2-35

Whatever things I have enjoyed become objects of memory alone, like experiences in a dream; once past, they are never seen again. ^2-36

Even in this very lifetime many friends and enemies have passed away; yet the unbearable misdeeds committed for their sake still stand before me. ^2-37

Because I never understood that I too am but transient, I have committed many kinds of misdeeds through avidyā, attachment, and aversion. ^2-38

Day and night my life is consumed without replenishment; if no surplus is ever added, how could I possibly escape death? ^2-39

Though surrounded by kin and companions as I lie upon my bed, I alone shall experience the sensations of the life-force ceasing. ^2-40

When seized by Yama's messengers, what good are relatives? What good are friends? Only merit can protect one then — and I have cultivated so little. ^2-41

Carelessly, O Protector, I never recognized a danger of this magnitude; for the sake of this impermanent life I have heaped up abundant misdeeds. ^2-42

Consider one led away to lose a limb: even before the deed, one is already stricken — mouth dry, eyes distorted, appearance utterly changed from before. ^2-43

How much more then when seized by Yama's terrifying, corpse-like messengers, afflicted by fears and sickness beyond compare — what need to speak of one utterly wretched? ^2-44

"Who will truly save me from this great terror?" — with eyes starting from their sockets, that person searches in every direction for refuge. ^2-45

Seeing no refuge in any direction, one falls into complete despair; and if there is truly no refuge anywhere — what shall I do when my time comes? ^2-46

Therefore from this very day I take refuge in the buddhas — protectors of the world, striving for beings' welfare, whose great power dispels every fear. ^2-47

Likewise I take true refuge in their Dharma — the teaching that dispels the terrors of saṃsāra — and in the saṅgha of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Trembling with fear, I offer myself to the bodhisattva Samantabhadra; to Mañjuśrī I offer my body. ^2-49

And to Avalokiteśvara, the protector whose compassion never fails, I cry out in desperation: "Protect me, though I am laden with misdeeds." ^2-50

From the depths of my heart I cry out for refuge to Ākāśagarbha, Kṣitigarbha, and every great compassionate protector. ^2-51

I take refuge in Vajrapāṇi — at whose mere sight Yama's messengers and the rest flee in terror, scattering in every direction. ^2-52

Formerly I transgressed your counsel; now that I truly see this terror, I take refuge in you — please dispel this fear quickly. ^2-53

If one must follow a physician's word even when frightened by an ordinary illness, how much more when repeatedly stricken by the sickness of a hundred faults such as craving and the other kleśas? ^2-54

A single one of these is enough to destroy every person on earth; and no other medicine for them is found anywhere in the world. ^2-55

To refuse the counsel of the physician who knows all and who heals every pain — that would be the height of folly. ^2-56

If one must be heedful even when standing near a small, ordinary precipice, how much more at a fall that may last thousands of aeons into the deepest suffering? ^2-57

It is not fitting to rest at ease thinking, "I shall certainly not die today"; the time when I shall cease to exist is absolutely certain to come. ^2-58

Who can grant me immunity from death? How can I be sure of escaping it? Since it is certain I shall not remain forever, how can my mind be at ease? ^2-59

What now remains to me of things once enjoyed and now gone? Yet clinging to them with fixation, I even transgressed my teachers' counsel. ^2-60

Since I must one day leave this life — abandoning every friend and companion — and journey on utterly alone, what ultimately matters whether they were friend or foe? ^2-61

"Suffering arises from non-virtue — how then shall I be surely liberated from it?" This thought alone deserves my constant attention, day and night. ^2-62

Whatever misdeeds I have committed through not knowing better — whether naturally unwholesome or bound by vows I have broken — ^2-63

standing directly before the Protector, palms joined, fearful of the suffering they will bring, I bow again and again and confess them all. ^2-64

O guides, please acknowledge that I have truly committed misdeeds; none of it was wholesome — henceforth I shall not do so again. ^2-65

*Thus ends Chapter Two: "Confessing Wrongdoing."* ^2-a

## Chapter 3: Taking Hold of Bodhicitta

With joy I rejoice in the merit that gives all sentient beings relief from the suffering of the lower realms, and in suffering sentient beings who abide in happiness. ^3-1

I rejoice in merit accumulated as a cause of awakening; I rejoice when embodied beings are definitively freed from the suffering of saṃsāra; and I rejoice in the awakening of the protectors and in the stages attained by the heirs of the Jinas. ^3-2

With joy I rejoice in the ocean-like merit of the aspiration to bring happiness to all sentient beings, and in actions undertaken for the benefit of sentient beings. ^3-3

With palms joined I supplicate the Buddhas in every direction: may they kindle the lamp of Dharma for sentient beings lost in the darkness of suffering. ^3-4

With palms joined I supplicate the Victorious Ones who intend to pass into nirvāṇa: may they not leave this world in darkness, but remain for countless kalpas. ^3-5

Having done all this, may whatever merit I have accumulated clear away all the suffering of every sentient being. ^3-6

For as long as any sentient being is ill, until that being is fully restored to health, may I become medicine, physician, and attendant to that one's sickness. ^3-7

May I shower down food and drink and remove the suffering of hunger and thirst; and in the interval between famines may I myself become food and drink. ^3-8

For sentient beings who are destitute and poor, may I become an inexhaustible treasure, abiding close at hand in the form of whatever diverse implements they require. ^3-9

My body, my possessions, and all the merit of the three times — for the sake of accomplishing the aims of all sentient beings I shall give them away without reservation. ^3-10

By giving everything away one attains nirvāṇa; my mind seeks nirvāṇa. Since everything must ultimately be relinquished, it is best to give it to sentient beings. ^3-11

To all embodied beings I have already given this body for their pleasure; they may always kill it, revile it, trample it, or do with it as they wish. ^3-12

Even if they sport with my body, ridicule it, or treat it as a jest — since I have already given this body away, what concern is mine in the matter? ^3-13

I allow them whatever action will not actually harm it; and may no one who ever directs attention toward me come to any harm whatsoever. ^3-14

When toward me anger or faith arises in anyone, may that very mind forever become the cause of the fulfillment of all their aims. ^3-15

Whoever reviles me, harms others, or mocks me in return — may all of them become fit for awakening. ^3-16

May I become protector of the unprotected, guide of those who travel the path, and for those who wish to cross over — boat, ferry, and bridge. ^3-17

For those who seek an island, an island; for those who desire a resting place, a resting place; and for embodied beings who wish for a servant — may I become the servant of all. ^3-18

May I become for embodied beings the wish-fulfilling jewel, the excellent vase, the great medicine of mantra attainment, and the wish-fulfilling tree — whatever they desire. ^3-19

Like earth and the great elements, and like space itself, may I forever become the manifold foundation of sustenance for immeasurable sentient beings. ^3-20

Likewise, for sentient beings in every realm extending to the limits of space, may I become a source of sustenance until all have attained nirvāṇa. ^3-21

Just as the Sugatas of the past first gave rise to the mind of awakening and then, step by step, abode in the trainings of the bodhisattva — ^3-22

so too, for the benefit of sentient beings, I shall give rise to bodhicitta, and in the same way I shall train step by step in those trainings. ^3-23

Having thus taken up bodhicitta with a clear mind, a person of intelligence should praise the mind in the following way, so that it may further increase: ^3-24

Today my life has borne fruit; I have well obtained the human existence. Today I am born into the Buddha lineage; today I have become an heir of the Jinas. ^3-25

Henceforth, in whatever I undertake, I must act in accord with this lineage and never bring stain upon this faultless, noble family. ^3-26

Just as a blind person finds a precious jewel in a heap of dust, so by some means bodhicitta has arisen in me. ^3-27

It is the supreme nectar that destroys the death-lord of sentient beings; it is also the inexhaustible treasure that dispels the poverty of sentient beings. ^3-28

It is the supreme medicine that thoroughly pacifies the sicknesses of sentient beings; it is the resting-tree for sentient beings weary from wandering the path of saṃsāra. ^3-29

It is the bridge that frees all sentient beings from the lower realms; it is the moon of the mind rising to pacify the torment of the kleśas of sentient beings. ^3-30

It is the great sun that dispels the mist of ignorance among sentient beings; it is the fresh butter that rises from churning the milk of the true Dharma. ^3-31

For guest-travelers journeying the path of saṃsāra who wish to enjoy the pleasures of happiness, bodhicitta stands present as the supreme feast that fully satisfies the great guest sentient beings. ^3-32

Today, in the presence of all the protectors, I have invited all sentient beings as guests to happiness on the way to the Sugata himself; therefore gods, asuras, and the rest — rejoice! ^3-33

*Thus ends Chapter Three: "Taking Hold of Bodhicitta."* ^3-a

## Chapter 4: Carefulness

Once an heir of the Jinas has firmly taken hold of bodhicitta in this way, one should always strive without sloth never to depart from the trainings. ^4-1

What is begun rashly, or what has not been well examined — even if a pledge has been made regarding it — is of the kind that may be reviewed as to whether it should be done or set aside. ^4-2

Bodhicitta has been examined by the Buddhas and their heirs with great prajñā, and I too have examined it according to my capacity; so what reason is there to shrink from it? ^4-3

If I make such a pledge and then do not carry it out in action, having deceived all those sentient beings, what sort of existence will be mine? ^4-4

It is taught that a person who even intends in mind to give some small object and then does not give it becomes a hungry ghost; ^4-5

so if, having invited all sentient beings with sincere intention to supreme happiness, I then deceive them, what happy rebirth could I expect? ^4-6

It is said that even those connected with someone who has abandoned bodhicitta may somehow be liberated — yet the manner of that is inconceivable by thought; only the omniscient ones know it. ^4-7

For a bodhisattva this is the heaviest of downfalls: if it occurs, one fails the aim of all sentient beings at once. ^4-8

Whoever, even for a single moment, obstructs a bodhisattva's merit harms the aim of sentient beings and will know no end of the lower realms. ^4-9

If destroying even one sentient being's happiness brings harm to oneself, what need to speak of destroying the happiness of embodied beings as limitless as the extent of space? ^4-10

When the power of downfall and the power of bodhicitta thus alternate back and forth in saṃsāra, reaching a bodhisattva ground is long delayed. ^4-11

Therefore I must carry out what I have pledged with reverence; if from today I do not apply effort, I shall go lower and lower. ^4-12

Countless Buddhas who benefit all sentient beings have already passed away, yet through my own faults I have not become an object of their healing activity. ^4-13

If I continue to act in that same way even now, again and again I shall experience the lower realms — sickness, bondage, cutting, slaughter, and the like. ^4-14

A Tathāgata's appearance, faith, obtaining a human body, and suitability for practice of virtue — when will I obtain something so exceedingly rare? ^4-15

Even a day free of illness such as this, provided with food and without harm, is deceptive: life is lost in an instant, and the body is like something borrowed for a moment. ^4-16

Through conduct such as mine I shall not even obtain a human body; and if a human body is not obtained, there is only misdeed and no virtue. ^4-17

When the occasion for virtuous practice is at hand and I do not perform virtue, what will I be able to do later, confused by all the suffering of the lower realms? ^4-18

If I perform no virtue and instead accumulate misdeeds, for a hundred million kalpas I shall not even hear the word "happy rebirth." ^4-19

For that very reason the Bhagavān has taught that a human birth is exceedingly difficult to obtain — like a blind turtle's neck passing through the hole in a yoke adrift on a vast ocean. ^4-20

If a single instant of misdeed can lead to residence in the Avīci hell for an age, what need to speak of happy rebirth given misdeeds accumulated since beginningless saṃsāra? ^4-21

Nor is one liberated merely by experiencing that much alone; rather, even while experiencing it, further misdeeds arise vigorously. ^4-22

Having found leisure so difficult to obtain, if I do not practice virtue, there is no greater deception than this and no greater delusion. ^4-23

If, having understood this, I nevertheless relapse into laziness through delusion, then at the time death arrives great grief will surely arise. ^4-24

If my body is burned for a long time in the unbearable fires of hell, the fire of remorse that torments the mind is equally unbearable — of that there is no doubt. ^4-25

Having somehow obtained by good fortune a ground so hard to find and so beneficial, how could I, knowing as I do, lead myself again into that hell? ^4-26

As though bewitched by a spell, I seem to have no mind here; I do not even know by what I am deluded — what is it that dwells within me? ^4-27

Kleśas such as hatred and craving possess neither hands nor feet; they are neither brave nor clever — so by what means have they made a servant of me? ^4-28

Abiding right in my mind, they happily harm me, and yet I do not become angry at them; such "patience" is tolerance in the wrong place — a thing to be condemned. ^4-29

Even if all the gods and asuras were to rise up as my enemies, they could not lead me into the fires of Avīci; ^4-30

but this powerful enemy, the kleśas — in a single instant it casts me there, and even Mount Meru, should it meet them, would not leave ashes behind. ^4-31

The kleśa-enemy that is mine lasts from beginningless time without end; no other enemy can endure so long. ^4-32

All other relationships, when attended to harmoniously, bring benefit and happiness; but the kleśas, when attended to, bring suffering and harm in return. ^4-33

If this constant enemy, the sole cause of the ever-increasing host of harms, is allowed to settle firmly in my heart, how could fearlessness and joy arise for me in saṃsāra? ^4-34

These are the jailers of saṃsāra and the executioners in hell; if they remain in the snare of a mind clinging to its ground, how could happiness exist for me? ^4-35

Therefore, until I have directly conquered this enemy, I shall not abandon effort here; even the proud and arrogant, angered by a small injury, do not sleep until they have conquered it — ^4-36

and soldiers, though death and suffering from spears, lances, and weapons are natural on the battlefield, do not turn back before their aim is accomplished, even when pierced by countless arrow-wounds of pain. ^4-37

I, who today strive to conquer this natural enemy that is the constant cause of all suffering — why should I despair because of any of the hundred thousand hardships that produce it? ^4-38

Scars left on the body by useless enemies are worn almost as ornaments; so why should the hardships I encounter while striving for a great aim harm me? ^4-39

Fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and the like endure harm from cold, heat, and the rest even for the sake of a mere livelihood; so why should I not endure the same for the happiness of sentient beings? ^4-40

I pledged to liberate sentient beings in the ten directions, to the limits of space, from the kleśas — yet I myself am not liberated from the kleśas. ^4-41

Was that not speech like a madman's, not knowing my own measure? Nevertheless, having spoken thus, I must never turn back from destroying the kleśas. ^4-42

In this I shall apply myself, grasp it firmly, and enter battle: apart from the kleśa that destroys kleśas, there is no kleśa of that kind. ^4-43

Even if I am burned, killed, or beheaded, in no way whatsoever shall I bow down to the kleśa-enemy. ^4-44

An ordinary enemy, even when driven from one place, may dwell elsewhere, gather strength, and return; the kleśa-enemy is not of that kind — ^4-45

for a kleśa abandoned from my mind through the eye of prajñā — where could it go, and from where could it return to harm me? It can only seem to do so because my mind lacks exertion. ^4-46

The kleśas do not abide in objects, in the sense faculties, in the space between, or anywhere else; so where do they dwell, that they harm all sentient beings? They are like an illusion; therefore abandon fear in the heart and apply effort for the sake of knowing this — why pointlessly harm yourself in the hells and the rest? ^4-47

Having reflected in this way, I shall apply effort to carry out the training as described; for if a patient will not heed the physician's words, how could medicines alone make the sick well? ^4-48

*Thus ends Chapter Four: "Carefulness."* ^4-a

## Chapter 5: Guarding Awareness

Whoever wishes to keep the trainings must strive with utmost effort to guard the mind, for without guarding the mind there is no way to keep the trainings. ^5-1

When the elephant of the mind is let loose, it inflicts harm like the torments of Avīci — a degree of harm that even an untrained, intoxicated elephant would not inflict here in this world. ^5-2

If the elephant of the mind is firmly tethered on every side by the rope of mindfulness (*smṛti*), all fears vanish and every virtue comes within reach. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, every kind of enemy, the guardians of the hell realms, evil spirits, and demons — ^5-4

all of them are bound when this one mind is bound; all of them are tamed when this one mind alone is tamed. ^5-5

The Buddha himself taught this clearly: every fear and every measureless suffering arises from the mind and nowhere else. ^5-6

Who forged the weapons of the hell realms? Who made that burning iron ground? From what did all that fire arise? ^5-7

The Buddha taught that all of it comes from a mind full of misdeeds; in the three worlds, therefore, there is no fear apart from the mind. ^5-8

If the perfection of generosity (*dāna-pāramitā*) meant ending poverty for all sentient beings, then since poor people still exist, how did the buddhas of the past ever perfect it? ^5-9

The perfection of generosity is taught to lie in the intention to give all one's possessions to all sentient beings, together with their fruit — and so it is the mind itself. ^5-10

Where could one place all the fish and the rest so that none would ever be killed? The perfection of ethics (*śīla-pāramitā*) is said to come from the mind that renounces harm, not from stopping every outer harm. ^5-11

Hostile sentient beings are as limitless as space, and I could never overcome them all; but if I destroy this one mind of anger, it is as though I have destroyed every enemy. ^5-12

Where on earth could I find enough leather to cover the whole surface of the ground? Yet with leather the size of a sole alone, it is as though the whole earth were covered. ^5-13

Likewise, I cannot restrain outer things, but if I restrain this mind of mine, what need is there to restrain anything else? ^5-14

Just as a single clear mental state can produce a result like rebirth in the Brahma realm, the results of body and speech do not follow that pattern when conduct is corrupt. ^5-15

Recitation, ascetic practice, and the rest — even if pursued for a long time — are called pointless by those who know, when done with a mind lacking mindfulness and awareness. ^5-16

Whoever fails to grasp this supreme secret of the Dharma, the mind, wanders pointlessly through the night even while wishing to gain happiness and end suffering. ^5-17

Therefore I must seize this mind of mine and guard it well; apart from guarding the mind, what good are many other disciplines? ^5-18

Just as someone with a wound stays alert among a rough crowd, so too among ordinary people I must always guard this wound of the mind. ^5-19

If even a small physical pain makes us careful about a wound, why should we not guard the wound of the mind, when the danger is being crushed in the lower realms? ^5-20

Living in this way, whether among hostile people or among women, a steadfast practitioner of vows does not decline. ^5-21

Let possessions, honor, body, and livelihood be lost; let other virtues decline — but may this mind never decline. ^5-22

To all who wish to guard the mind I join my palms and say: guard mindfulness (*smṛti*) and awareness (*saṃprajanya*) with every effort. ^5-23

Just as someone shaken by illness is powerless in every action, so someone whose mind is shaken by confusion is powerless in every action. ^5-24

Whatever has been heard, reflected on, or cultivated by one who lacks awareness does not remain in memory, like water poured into a leaky jar. ^5-25

Even the learned, the faithful, and the diligent become entangled in downfalls when the fault of lacking awareness clings to them. ^5-26

Thieves lacking awareness follow upon the loss of mindfulness and rob even carefully accumulated merit, carrying one off to a lower rebirth as though plundered. ^5-27

This gang of thieves, the kleśas, watches for its chance; finding an opening, it steals merit and destroys even the life of a good rebirth. ^5-28

Therefore I must never let mindfulness slip away from the door of the mind; and if it does slip away, I should recall the sufferings of the lower realms and settle it close again. ^5-29

Mindfulness arises easily for the fortunate who live near a teacher, follow instruction, and act with respect born of conscientious fear. ^5-30

"The buddhas and bodhisattvas see everything everywhere without obstruction; I always stand before them all" — ^5-31

thinking in this way brings conscience, respect, and fear; and from that, remembrance of the buddhas arises again and again. ^5-32

Whenever mindfulness stands at the door of the mind for the sake of guarding, awareness comes with it; and even when awareness departs, it returns. ^5-33

At the outset, recognizing that this mind is full of faults, I should hold it steady, as one holds a piece of wood in place. ^5-34

I must never let my gaze wander pointlessly; with steady intention I should always keep my eyes cast down. ^5-35

To rest the eyes, I may glance to the side from time to time; and if someone appears in my field of vision, I should look up and say, "It is good that you have come." ^5-36

To watch for danger on the road I should look again and again in the four directions; and when I pause to rest, I should look behind and then to the rear. ^5-37

Having checked ahead and behind, I should go on or turn back; in every situation I should act only after knowing what is needed. ^5-38

Having gathered what is to be done and decided how the body should remain, I should then examine from time to time how the body is actually positioned. ^5-39

Just as one watches carefully so that a wild elephant tied to a post for the sake of the Dharma does not break loose, so should one examine with every effort. ^5-40

"Where is my mind engaged?" — examining it in this way again and again, I should make sure that not even a single moment of meditative effort slips away. ^5-41

When fear, celebration, and the like make that impossible, act as you please; in times of giving, the teachings say, one may relax ethics and equanimity. ^5-42

Once I have reflected and begun a task, I should not think of anything else; with intention fixed on that alone, I should accomplish it for a while. ^5-43

Acting in this way, everything is done well and nothing is left half-done; without awareness the kleśas stay close — but acting this way, they do not increase. ^5-44

When various idle talk, marvels, and entertainments surround me on every side, I should give up attachment to them. ^5-45

If I start digging the earth pointlessly, cutting grass, or drawing on the ground out of boredom, I should recall the Buddha's training and, from fear, drop it at once. ^5-46

Whenever I wish to move or wish to speak, I should first examine my own mind; then I should act or speak with steady awareness. ^5-47

Whenever attachment or anger arises in my mind, I should neither act nor speak, but remain like a piece of wood. ^5-48

When the mind is mocking, mixed with vulgarity, swollen with pride, bent on exposing others' faults, or inclined to deceit and flattery — ^5-49

when it wants praise for myself, wants to blame others, or is harsh and regretful — at such times too I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-50

When the mind wants gain, honor, fame, or attendants and service, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-51

When it wants to cast aside others' welfare and pursue selfish aims, or when the wish to speak arises, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-52

When impatience, laziness, fear, careless speech, or partiality toward my own side arises, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-53

Having examined the mind on every side and found kleśas or pointless striving, I should bravely apply the remedy and hold the mind steady. ^5-54

With firm conviction, deep faith, steadfastness, respect, courtesy, conscience, and fear — calm, and striving to bring others joy — ^5-55

not discouraged by the conflicting wishes of childish people, and compassionate, thinking, "These kleśas have arisen in them" — ^5-56

acting in ways that cause no harm to myself or others, and holding this mind without a solid sense of self, like an illusion — I should always keep it so. ^5-57

After long ages I have gained this supreme leisure; remembering that again and again, I should hold this mind unmoving, like Mount Meru. ^5-58

When vultures fight over flesh, dragging it back and forth, you feel no displeasure; so why, mind, are you now so possessive? ^5-59

You treat this body as your own and guard it — yet you and the body are separate; what can that do for you? ^5-60

Deluded mind, why do you not cherish a clean wooden form instead? What is the use of guarding this filthy machine of decay? ^5-61

First, with my own intelligence, I should separate this sequence of skin; then, with the blade of prajñā, I should cut flesh away from the net of bones. ^5-62

Having broken the bones apart as well and looked down to the marrow, I should examine for myself: what essence is there here? ^5-63

If, searching with effort, you find no essence there, why do you guard this body with such attachment? ^5-64

You would not eat what is unclean, you would not drink the blood, you would not chew the entrails — so what does the body do for you? ^5-65

At best it is fit for jackals and birds to guard this body for the sake of food; a human body like this exists only to be put to use. ^5-66

Even after you guard it, the pitiless lord of death will seize it and give it to dogs and birds — what will you do then? ^5-67

If you would not clothe and supply a servant you can no longer employ, why do you pamper the body when it will go elsewhere? ^5-68

Having paid the body its wages, let it now work for my benefit; I should not give it everything when it does nothing useful. ^5-69

Regard the body only as a vehicle for coming and going; caring for it as for a boat, I should transform it into a body that fulfills sentient beings' aims. ^5-70

With self-control, always keep a smiling face; cast off frowns and scowls and be a sincere friend to the world. ^5-71

Do not drop chairs and the like noisily; do not open doors violently; always take pleasure in being gentle and quiet. ^5-72

Like a heron stalking fish, like a cat stalking prey, moving silently, the able ones always practiced in this way to accomplish their aims. ^5-73

When another skillfully offers helpful counsel, even unasked, accept those words respectfully on your head; always be everyone's student. ^5-74

Whenever something is well said, say, "Well spoken"; when you see merit being done, praise it and increase the person's joy. ^5-75

Speak of others' good qualities behind their backs; when good qualities are mentioned, join in; and when your own good qualities are mentioned, recognize them as qualities. ^5-76

Every undertaking is done for the sake of joy, and that joy cannot be bought even with riches; therefore take delight in the good qualities others have created. ^5-77

In this I lose nothing, and happiness in the future is great; fault-finding brings displeasure now and great suffering later. ^5-78

When you speak, let your words be pleasing, clear, meaningful, free of craving and hatred, gentle, and suited to the occasion. ^5-79

When you look at sentient beings, look at them directly and kindly, thinking, "Through these very sentient beings I will become a buddha." ^5-80

Whether moved by devotion or by applying the remedy, cultivating good qualities and benefit, especially toward those who suffer, brings great goodness. ^5-81

Being capable and energetic, I should always do tasks myself rather than depending on anyone else for them. ^5-82

Practice giving and the other pāramitās in ascending order; do not sacrifice what is greater for what is lesser; above all, think of others' welfare. ^5-83

Knowing this, I should always strive for others' welfare; even what is forbidden is permitted for one endowed with compassion and far-reaching vision. ^5-84

Share food with those who have gone astray, the protectorless, and those living in discipline; eat only what is needed, and give away everything except the three robes. ^5-85

Do not harm this body, which practices the sublime Dharma, for trivial reasons; acting thus, sentient beings' wishes will quickly be fulfilled. ^5-86

Do not give away this body unless the intention of compassion is pure; but when it is pure, give it in this life and others as a means to accomplish what is great. ^5-87

Do not teach the Dharma to the disrespectful — not to one with a bandaged head like the sick, nor to one holding an umbrella, a stick, a weapon, or with the head covered. ^5-88

Do not teach the profound and vast teachings to those of lesser capacity, nor to women when no men are present; treat lower and higher teachings with equal respect and practice them all. ^5-89

Do not set those fit for vast teachings upon lesser ones; do not abandon good conduct; do not attract people by dazzling them with sūtras and mantras. ^5-90

When discarding a toothpick or spitting, cover it; it is also censured to relieve oneself in water, on hard ground, or on ground people use. ^5-91

Do not eat with the mouth full, noisily, or gaping open; do not sit with legs stretched out carelessly, and do not rub the hands together. ^5-92

Do not travel, sit, or lie down alone with another woman; avoid whatever would make worldly people lose faith, having seen or heard of it. ^5-93

Do not point with a single finger; with respect, show the way to everyone with the whole right hand, and teach the path in that way too. ^5-94

Do not move the hands excessively; a slight movement and a small sound, a snap of the fingers and the like, are enough — anything more is undisciplined. ^5-95

Sleep as the Protector slept at his final passing, in the direction you prefer; resolve from the very first moment to rise quickly with awareness. ^5-96

Since the conduct of a bodhisattva is said to be measureless, the conduct of training the mind should certainly be practiced without limit. ^5-97

Three times by day and three by night, recite the confession of the Three Heaps; relying on the buddhas and bodhicitta, let the remainder of downfalls be pacified. ^5-98

In whatever situation, doing whatever action, whether for oneself or under another's influence, strive to apply the training taught for that occasion. ^5-99

There is nothing the heirs of the Jinas do not train in; for the wise who live in this way, nothing fails to become merit. ^5-100

Use body, speech, and mind for nothing except the welfare of sentient beings, directly or indirectly; and dedicate everything to enlightenment for sentient beings' sake alone. ^5-101

Never abandon a good spiritual friend — one skilled in the meaning of the Great Vehicle and supreme in the bodhisattva discipline — even at the cost of life. ^5-102

Learn how to rely on a teacher from the conduct shown in the *Glorious Manifestation*; know this and the rest of the Buddha's command by reading the sūtras. ^5-103

Trainings appear in the sūtras, so read them; the *Akāśagarbha Sūtra* should be read first of all. ^5-104

Because it explains in detail what is to be practiced always, look again and again also at the *Compendium of Trainings*. ^5-105

Or at least look at what was gathered from all the sūtras in brief; and study with effort the second work composed by the noble Nāgārjuna. ^5-106

Whatever is not forbidden there should be practiced; and having seen the trainings, act properly in order to protect the mind of the world. ^5-107

Examine again and again the situations of body and mind — that, in short, is the defining mark of guarding awareness. ^5-108

All of this must be put into practice by the body; what is accomplished by words alone? Does merely reading a medical treatise ever benefit the sick? ^5-109

*Thus ends Chapter 5: "Guarding Awareness."* ^5-a

## Chapter 6: Patience

Whatever generosity, offerings to the buddhas, and good deeds have been gathered over a thousand eons — a single flash of anger can destroy it all. ^6-1

There is no misdeed like hatred, and no discipline like patience (*kṣānti*). So I should cultivate patience with real effort, in every way I can. ^6-2

Someone holding onto the pain of anger never experiences peace of mind. They find no joy or happiness, they cannot sleep, and they become unsettled. ^6-3

Even people who depend on someone for their wealth and favor will turn and try to kill that very person, once anger takes hold. ^6-4

Because of anger, friends grow tired of us; even when we are generous, they do not want to stay close. In short, there is no such thing as living happily while angry. ^6-5

The enemy called anger brings on this kind of suffering. But whoever works hard to defeat anger finds happiness both now and later. ^6-6

When something unwanted happens, or something we want gets blocked, that becomes food for our unhappy mind, and hatred grows strong and destroys us. ^6-7

So I need to destroy the very food this enemy feeds on. This enemy has no other job except to harm me. ^6-8

No matter what happens to me, I should not let it disturb my happy mind. Being upset about it does not get me what I wanted anyway — it just wears away my merit. ^6-9

If something can be fixed, why be upset about it? And if it cannot be fixed, what good does being upset do? ^6-10

We do not want suffering, insult, harsh words, or unpleasant talk aimed at ourselves or our friends — but somehow we are fine with the opposite happening to our enemies. ^6-11

The causes of happiness appear only occasionally; the causes of suffering are extremely common. So, my mind, hold steady — without suffering, there is no way to find true liberation. ^6-12

Devotees of harsh spiritual practices willingly endure being burned or cut, for no real reward at all. So why am I, aiming for liberation, held back by mere cowardice? ^6-13

There is nothing that does not get easier with practice. So, by getting used to small pains first, I should learn to bear even great harm. ^6-14

Do we not already see this happening — with the pain of snakebites, flies, hunger, thirst, and things like skin rashes, which we bear all the time, for no particular reward? ^6-15

I should not be overly sensitive to heat, cold, rain, wind, sickness, being restrained, or being struck. Acting oversensitive like that only makes the harm worse. ^6-16

Some people, seeing their own blood, become even braver and stronger. Others, seeing someone else's blood, faint and collapse. ^6-17

This difference comes from the state of one's mind — whether it is steady, or simply unused to that kind of thing. So I should look past minor harms and stay unshaken by suffering. ^6-18

Even when a wise person experiences suffering, they do not let their clear mind become clouded. Fighting with our own kleśas naturally brings hardship along the way. ^6-19

The truly brave ones are those who overlook every kind of suffering and defeat enemies like hatred. Everyone else is just finishing off something already dead. ^6-20

Suffering also has some good qualities: it wears away pride through humility, it gives rise to compassion for those caught in saṃsāra, it makes us shy away from misdeeds, and it makes us take joy in doing good. ^6-21

We do not get angry at the great sources of suffering, like bodily imbalances and illness — so why do we get angry at people, who also have minds of their own? They are all driven by conditions too. ^6-22

Just as sickness arises even though we do not want it, kleśas arise forcefully in us even though we do not want them either. ^6-23

People get angry without ever deciding, "I will be angry now." Anger just arises on its own, without ever being planned that way. ^6-24

All the various faults and misdeeds that exist come about through the force of conditions — they do not happen through some free, independent choice. ^6-25

The collection of conditions that gives rise to anger does not think, "I will produce this." And what gets produced does not think, "I am being produced," either. ^6-26

Whatever people think of as a fundamental self, or an independently existing "I," never actually arises through some deliberate intention to arise. ^6-27

If that self did not exist before being born, then whatever desire it has to come into being would have to be fixed on some object forever — and it could never actually stop. ^6-28

And if this self is supposed to be permanent, then clearly it cannot do anything at all, like the sky itself. Even coming into contact with other conditions, being unchanging, what could it possibly still do? ^6-29

And if that self stays exactly the same, even while acting, then what is the acting actually doing? What real connection could there be between "the self" and "the self's action"? ^6-30

In this way, everything is controlled by other things; nothing has power over itself alone. Understanding this dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), I will not get angry at anything, since all things are more like illusions, with no one solid really behind them. ^6-31

Someone might object: "Then who is there to be angry at, and who could reasonably remove that anger?" But it is not unreasonable to want to rely on patience anyway, in order to stop suffering. ^6-32

So, whether it is an enemy or a friend, when I see someone act wrongly, I should simply think, "This came about because of certain conditions," and stay calm about it. ^6-33

If things happened just because we wanted them to, no one would ever suffer, since no living sentient being actually wants to suffer. ^6-34

Yet out of carelessness, people harm even themselves with thorns and the like. And driven by desire, some go so far as to starve themselves just to get what they want. ^6-35

Some people even jump off cliffs, harm themselves in reckless ways, eat poison or spoiled food, or act in unwholesome ways that hurt only themselves. ^6-36

When people, overpowered by kleśas, even go so far as to harm the very self they cherish most, how could they possibly avoid causing harm to others too? ^6-37

Since kleśas arise and drive people even to the point of harming themselves, how could I not feel compassion for them, instead of anger? ^6-38

If it is simply the nature of ordinary people to harm others, then getting angry at them makes no more sense than getting angry at fire for having the nature to burn. ^6-39

And if the fault is only a passing flaw, while sentient beings are naturally gentle at heart, then getting angry still does not make sense — like getting angry at smoke for briefly filling an otherwise clear sky. ^6-40

If I get angry directly at the stick that hits me, rather than at the person who swung it, it would make more sense to get angry at that person's anger itself, since the anger is what is really driving the stick. ^6-41

In the past, I myself have caused this kind of harm to other sentient beings. So it makes sense that sentient beings who harm me now are simply the natural result of that. ^6-42

Both the enemy's weapon and my own body are causes of this suffering. The enemy brought the weapon, and I brought the body — so really, who deserves my anger here? ^6-43

If, out of pure craving and confusion, I grab onto a painful, infected wound shaped like a human body, and then it hurts when touched, who is really there to blame for that pain? ^6-44

Ordinary people do not want suffering, yet they crave the very things that cause it. So when they end up harmed by their own mistakes, why should I blame them for it? ^6-45

The guardians of hell and the forest of sword-like leaves, for example, are things that arise from one's own past actions. In the same way, who is really there to be angry at, when what is causing me harm is simply my own karma ripening? ^6-46

Driven by my own past actions, those who harm me now have appeared. And if this leads them into the hell realms, would I not actually be the one responsible for their ruin? ^6-47

By depending on them, and by practicing patience, my own faults are worn away. Meanwhile, by depending on me, they end up experiencing prolonged suffering in the hell realms instead. ^6-48

In truth, I am the one harming them, while they are actually helping me. So why, unruly mind, do you get this so completely backwards and grow angry? ^6-49

If I actually had the good quality of a truly compassionate mind, I would not end up in the hell realms myself. But if all I do is protect myself from anger, what benefit does that bring to them? ^6-50

And if I retaliate and harm them in return, then not only will I fail to protect them from a bad rebirth, my own practice of patience will fall apart too. ^6-51

The mind itself has no physical body, so it can never actually be destroyed by anyone. It is only because of our strong attachment to the body that the body ends up suffering harm. ^6-52

Since insults, harsh words, and unpleasant talk do not actually harm the body at all, why, mind, do you get so furious about them? ^6-53

If other people's dislike of me does not actually consume me — not in this life, and not in future ones either — then why would I refuse to accept it calmly? ^6-54

If I refuse to accept it simply because it gets in the way of some material gain, then really I am throwing away my future good in exchange for keeping my present faults firmly in place. ^6-55

It would be better to die today than to live a long life the wrong way; even if someone like me lives a very long life, the suffering of death eventually comes anyway. ^6-56

Consider someone who enjoys a hundred years of happiness in a dream and then wakes up, compared to someone who enjoys just a moment of happiness and then wakes up. ^6-57

For both of them, once they wake up, that happiness never returns. In the same way, whether life is long or short, when death finally comes, it feels exactly the same. ^6-58

Even after gaining great wealth and enjoying happiness for a very long time, in the end we leave empty-handed, stripped bare, as if robbed by a thief. ^6-59

Someone might say: "If I survive because of the wealth I gain, I can use it to reduce misdeeds and build merit." But if I get angry just to gain that wealth, does that not wear away my merit and add to my misdeeds instead? ^6-60

If the very purpose I am living for gets ruined anyway, then what is the point of a life spent doing nothing but misdeeds? ^6-61

If I get angry at someone speaking badly about me because it might turn people away from goodness, then why do I not get equally angry when that same person speaks badly about someone else? ^6-62

Since others' lack of faith depends on them, and you already tolerate that patiently, why can you not tolerate unpleasant talk the same way, since kleśas also arise naturally, simply from conditions? ^6-63

Even when people mock or damage sacred images, monuments, and the true Dharma, it does not really make sense for me to get angry, because the buddhas themselves can never actually be harmed by that. ^6-64

Even toward those who harm my teachers, relatives, and friends, I should recognize, as before, that it comes about through conditions, and let go of anger. ^6-65

Since sentient beings cause harm either through conscious intention or without it, it does not make sense to single out only those who act with intention — I should simply learn to bear the harm either way. ^6-66

Some act wrongly out of confusion, and some, out of that same confusion, get angry at them for it. Who among them can really be called faultless, and who can really be blamed? ^6-67

Why did I, in the past, ever act in a way that caused others to harm me now? Since everything depends on past actions, why should I hold a grudge about it now? ^6-68

Seeing things this way, I should make every effort to develop a mind of loving-kindness toward everyone, and dedicate myself wholeheartedly to building good qualities. ^6-69

For example, when a house catches fire and it starts to spread to another house, it makes sense to immediately clear away anything flammable nearby, like dry straw. ^6-70

In the same way, whatever I am attached to that lets the fire of hatred spread, I should let go of it right away, out of fear that it will burn away my own merit. ^6-71

If a man about to be executed instead only loses a hand and is set free, is that not a good outcome? And if I am freed from the sufferings of hell through some human suffering now, is that not a good outcome too? ^6-72

If I cannot even bear this small amount of suffering right now, then why do I not get rid of anger, which is the real cause of the far greater suffering of hell? ^6-73

For the sake of trivial desires, I have already experienced being burned and worse, thousands of times over, in hell. Yet through none of that suffering did I ever accomplish anything meaningful, for myself or for anyone else. ^6-74

This present suffering is not nearly as bad, and it can accomplish something truly great. So I should feel nothing but joy at suffering that removes harm from other sentient beings. ^6-75

If other people feel joy when they are praised for their good qualities, why, my own mind, do you not feel that same kind of joy when someone else is praised? ^6-76

This joy of yours would be a genuine source of happiness, entirely free of fault. It is something praised by people with good qualities, and it is also the very best way to draw others toward you. ^6-77

On the other hand, if you do not want that same happiness for others, then — since you would also refuse to reward good service — you will end up losing out, both in this life and in future ones. ^6-78

When you talk about your own good qualities, you want others to feel happy for you. But when someone else talks about their good qualities, you refuse to feel happy for them. ^6-79

Having generated bodhicitta for the sake of every sentient being's happiness, why would you get angry when sentient beings actually find happiness on their own? ^6-80

If you claim you want all sentient beings to become enlightened and honored throughout the world, then why does it trouble you so much just to see them receive a little ordinary respect and support right now? ^6-81

If someone you are responsible for supporting instead gets support from their own relatives, and you get upset instead of happy about it, who is really raising them properly? ^6-82

If you do not even want that small good thing to happen for them, then how could you possibly want them to reach enlightenment? Someone who gets angry when others gain something clearly does not really hold bodhicitta at all. ^6-83

Whether that gift came from you or was already sitting in a generous donor's home, in both cases, it was never really yours to begin with — so what does it matter whether it is given away or not? ^6-84

Why would you throw away your own merit, faith, and good qualities? Why would you not simply let go of claiming what was never yours to keep, instead of getting angry about it? ^6-85

Not only do you feel no remorse for misdeeds you commit yourself, you even seem to want to compete with others who have done good deeds! ^6-86

Even if an enemy is unhappy, what good does that actually do you? Your mere wish for it will not actually cause them any harm. ^6-87

Even if your wish for their suffering came true, what good would that really do you? If you say it would satisfy you, what could possibly be worse than that kind of satisfaction? ^6-88

This hook, cast by the fisherman of kleśas, is truly unbearably sharp. Once caught by it, I will be thrown into the cauldrons of hell and cooked by its guardians. ^6-89

Being honored with praise and fame brings no real merit and no real strength; it does not cure sickness, and it does not bring real physical comfort either. ^6-90

If I actually understood what is truly good for me, what real benefit would fame bring? If all I want is a happy mind, then I would have to rely on things like drink and distraction instead. ^6-91

For the sake of fame, people lose their wealth and even risk their own lives. What real good do words do? Once you are dead, who is even left to enjoy that fame? ^6-92

When children's sandcastles collapse, they cry in despair. In the same way, when my praise and reputation collapse, my own mind acts just as childish. ^6-93

Since a mere sound has no mind of its own, it cannot possibly intend to praise me. Whatever true joy exists is simply because someone else feels genuinely happy about me — that is the real cause for joy. ^6-94

Whether it is someone else's joy, or even my own, what good does someone else's happiness actually do for me? That joy belongs entirely to that person alone — I do not get any share of it. ^6-95

If I were happy simply because someone else is happy, then I should feel that way about everyone's happiness equally. So why am I not happy when someone finds happiness through liking someone else instead of me? ^6-96

So this joy that arises just from thinking, "I am being praised," does not actually make logical sense at all — it is really nothing more than childish behavior. ^6-97

Praise and the like just distract me, and wear away my sense of urgency in practice. They breed envy toward those with good qualities, and they can even destroy real excellence. ^6-98

So are not those who work to undermine my praise and reputation actually helping to protect me from a bad rebirth? ^6-99

I am seeking real freedom, and I do not need to be bound up by wealth and honor. So how could I possibly be angry at those who are actually freeing me from those very chains? ^6-100

I want to throw myself into suffering, yet, as if blessed by the Buddha himself, some door remains firmly shut against that. So how could I possibly be angry at whoever is doing that for me? ^6-101

Someone might say, "This person is blocking my chance to build merit," and think it is reasonable to be angry at them for that. But there is no hard discipline equal to patience — so should I not actually settle right into practicing it instead? ^6-102

If it is really my own fault that I fail to be patient with this person, then it is I myself who am blocking the very cause of merit sitting right in front of me. ^6-103

If one thing cannot arise without another, and it does arise whenever that other thing is present, then that other thing is truly its cause — so how could it possibly be called an obstacle instead? ^6-104

A beggar who arrives at just the right time is not really an obstacle to generosity. And someone who helps a person become a monk should not be called an obstacle to ordination either. ^6-105

In this world, beggars are common, but people who actually cause us harm are rare. Because if I never wronged others in the past, then truly, no one would ever wrong me either. ^6-106

So, like an unearned treasure suddenly appearing in one's own house, without any effort at all, I should feel genuine joy toward my enemy, since they have become an ally in my own spiritual practice. ^6-107

Since this good result was accomplished together, by both of us, the fruit of my patience should rightly be offered to my enemy first, since they are truly the cause of my patience. ^6-108

Someone might say, "But my enemy had no intention of helping me develop patience, so they do not deserve to be honored for it." But then, why do we honor the true Dharma itself, even though it too is simply a fitting cause, without any intention behind it? ^6-109

Someone might say, "But my enemy actually intends to harm me, so they should not be honored." But if my enemy instead tried to help me, the way a doctor does, how would I ever have the chance to develop patience at all? ^6-110

So it is precisely because of a mind full of intense hostility that patience is able to arise in the first place. That is exactly why my enemy is the true cause of my patience, and deserves to be honored just like the sacred Dharma itself. ^6-111

That is why the Buddha himself said there are two kinds of "fields" in which goodness grows: the field of sentient beings, and the field of the buddhas. Many people, by pleasing sentient beings in this way, have reached the very height of accomplishment. ^6-112

Since buddhahood is attained equally through sentient beings and through the buddhas, why is it that we show the buddhas such respect, but not sentient beings, when the process depends equally on both? ^6-113

The quality of an intention is not something inherent by itself — it is known by its results. In that same way, sentient beings also share in this quality, and so deserve to be regarded as equal to the buddhas in this respect. ^6-114

Whatever offering is made to those with a loving mind is itself due to the greatness of sentient beings. And whatever merit comes from faith in the Buddha is due to the greatness of the Buddha. ^6-115

Since sentient beings also share, in part, in the process of accomplishing buddhahood, they deserve to be considered equal in that respect — though truly, nothing is fully equal to the buddhas, whose good qualities are like a boundless ocean. ^6-116

Even if just a small portion of that single, supreme collection of good qualities appeared in someone, it would be worth more to make offerings to that one sentient being than to offer up the three worlds themselves. ^6-117

Since sentient beings share in what gives rise to the Buddha's supreme qualities, on that basis alone, it makes sense to treat them as worthy of real honor. ^6-118

What is more, other than pleasing sentient beings, how else could anyone possibly repay those genuine, unwavering friends who bring us such immeasurable benefit? ^6-119

Since it is by giving up their very own bodies and entering the depths of the worst suffering that the buddhas repay that kindness, then even if sentient beings cause me great harm, I should still treat them, in every way, as something truly precious. ^6-120

My own teacher and guide does not even spare his own body for the sake of these sentient beings — so how, out of sheer confusion, could I possibly look down on them as if they were mere servants? ^6-121

When sentient beings are happy, the buddhas themselves rejoice; when sentient beings are harmed, the buddhas feel sorrow. So pleasing sentient beings brings joy to all the buddhas, and harming them brings sorrow to the buddhas as well. ^6-122

Just as no amount of pleasure could ever satisfy someone whose whole body is engulfed in flames, there is no way to please the greatly compassionate ones while sentient beings are being harmed. ^6-123

So, for whatever harm I have caused to sentient beings — which has displeased all the greatly compassionate ones — I confess that wrongdoing today, one by one, and I ask the buddhas' forgiveness for whatever has displeased them. ^6-124

From now on, in order to please the buddhas, I will become a servant to the world. Let crowds trample over my head, or even kill me — I will never respond with anger. May the Guardian of the World always be pleased. ^6-125

There is no doubt at all that these compassionate ones regard every sentient being in this world as their very own self. So how could I possibly fail to show respect to sentient beings, when to see them is to see the Guardian himself? ^6-126

This alone is what truly pleases the buddhas; this alone is what truly accomplishes my own good; this alone is what removes the suffering of the world. So I should always devote myself to this practice. ^6-127

For example, though a single official might cause harm to many people, far-sighted citizens do not retaliate against him even if they could, ^6-128

because he is not acting alone — the power of the king stands behind him. In the same way, I should never look down on or harm even a weak person who causes me harm. ^6-129

In the very same way, the guardians of hell, and all those endowed with great compassion, stand behind every sentient being. So, just as ordinary people appease a fierce king, I should work to please every sentient being. ^6-130

Even if a king became truly furious, could he inflict on me the kind of suffering I would experience in hell, simply for having displeased sentient beings? ^6-131

And even if a king were greatly pleased with me, could he ever give me what I would gain simply by pleasing sentient beings — buddhahood itself? Certainly not. ^6-132

Never mind the future buddhahood that comes from pleasing sentient beings — do we not already see, right in this very life, that it brings great glory, fame, and happiness? ^6-133

Through patience, one gains beauty and more while still wandering through this life, along with good health, a good reputation, an extremely long life, and the vast happiness of a great and rightful ruler. ^6-134

*Thus ends Chapter Six: "Patience."* ^6-a

## Chapter 7: Diligence (*vīrya*)

Having cultivated patience (*kṣānti*) like this, I should apply diligence (*vīrya*). Awakening rests on diligence, as nothing moves without wind. Without diligence (*vīrya*), merit does not arise. ^7-1

Diligence (*vīrya*) means taking joy in virtue. Its opposites are laziness — clinging to comfort and bad habits — discouragement, and putting yourself down. ^7-2

Laziness comes from enjoying comfort and idleness, craving sleep, and not feeling any weariness about the suffering of samsara. ^7-3

Caught in the net of kleśas, trapped in the cycle of rebirth, and already walking toward death — how can you still not see this? ^7-4

Have you not seen people just like you dying, one after another? And yet you still sleep on, unconcerned, like a water buffalo grazing without a care. ^7-5

With every escape route closed off, and death watching your every move, how can you still enjoy your meals and your sleep? ^7-6

Since death can come quickly, I should gather as much merit as I can while there is still time. If I only give up laziness once death has already arrived, what good will that do — it will be too late. ^7-7

Thinking, "I still have not done that," or "I have only half-finished this," death suddenly appears, and all I can think is, "Alas — it is over." ^7-8

With my eyes swollen and red from grief, tears streaming down, watching hope drain from the faces of those who love me, I will find myself staring at the messengers of Yama, lord of death. ^7-9

Tormented by memories of my own non-virtue, terrified at the sound of hell, and losing control of myself out of sheer panic — what will I even do in that state? ^7-10

If the ordinary fears of this life already make you feel like a fish gasping on dry land, why need we speak of the unbearable suffering of hell that comes from non-virtue? ^7-11

Even hot water is hard for this soft body to bear. Having already created the causes for the far greater burning of hell, how can I still feel this relaxed? ^7-12

Wanting results without diligence, being overly sensitive to hardship, acting as if you will live forever, even while death already has you in its grip — you are setting yourself up to be crushed by suffering. ^7-13

Relying on this precious human life — like a boat — I can cross the great river of suffering. Since a chance like this is so hard to find again, do not be foolish enough to fall asleep now. ^7-14

Why would you give up the greatest joy — delight in the sublime Dharma — in exchange for empty distractions and the very causes of suffering, like idle gossip and restlessness? ^7-15

Stay free of discouragement, build up your inner strength, take real charge of yourself, treat yourself and others as equally important, and be willing, when it counts, to put others before yourself. ^7-16

Do not get discouraged thinking, "How could someone like me attain awakening?" The Tathāgata, who never speaks anything but the truth, has declared this: ^7-17

Even tiny creatures like gnats, flies, bees, and worms can attain buddhahood, simply by generating genuine *vīrya*. ^7-18

Born as I am into the human race, capable of telling what helps from what harms, why would I not attain awakening too — as long as I never abandon this bodhisattva conduct (*caryā*)? ^7-19

Someone might say, "But I am afraid I might have to give up my hands or feet." That fear comes only from confusion — from failing to weigh what really matters against what does not. ^7-20

For countless eons I have been cut apart, burned, and torn to pieces, over and over — yet without ever attaining awakening through any of it, since I was not practicing this way. ^7-21

But the hardship involved in truly working toward awakening has a clear limit — like the brief pain of lancing a wound to clear out a much worse infection underneath. ^7-22

Every doctor cures illness through methods that are uncomfortable at first. To overcome much greater suffering later, I should be willing to bear a little discomfort now. ^7-23

But the Buddha, our supreme physician, does not rely on such harsh methods at all. Through the gentlest possible means, he heals even the deepest, most serious kleśas. ^7-24

The Buddha starts us off by having us practice giving away small, simple things, like vegetables. Once we are comfortable with that, we are gradually able to give away even more, bit by bit. ^7-25

Once we truly understand that our own body is not something to cling to any more than a vegetable is, what real difficulty is there in giving even that away? ^7-26

Once non-virtue is given up, suffering fades. Once *prajñā* grows, unhappiness fades too. It is confused thinking and non-virtue that harm both mind and body. ^7-27

Through building merit, the body becomes comfortable; through *prajñā*, the mind becomes at ease. So even while staying in samsara for the sake of helping others, why would someone compassionate ever feel discouraged? ^7-28

Through the power of bodhicitta, past non-virtue is worn away, and vast oceans of merit are gathered instead — that is why this path is declared supreme by the śrāvakas. ^7-29

Riding the horse of bodhicitta, which removes all weariness, and moving happily from one joy to the next — what wise person would feel discouraged on a journey like that? ^7-30

The forces needed to truly help beings are: strong aspiration, steadfastness, joy in the work, and the willingness to step back and rest when needed. Aspiration grows by reflecting on the dangers of samsara, and on the real benefits of this path. ^7-31

Having let go of everything that gets in the way, I should increase my diligence (*vīrya*) using aspiration, healthy self-confidence, joy, and the willingness to rest, along with genuine dedication and self-command. ^7-32

I still have countless faults to overcome, both my own and others'. For even a single fault, it could take an ocean of eons just to fully work through it. ^7-33

If I cannot even see myself making progress on a fraction of my own faults, why does my heart not break at the thought of how much suffering still lies ahead? ^7-34

I still have many good qualities left to develop, both my own and others'. For each one, it might take an ocean of eons to truly master. ^7-35

And yet I have not even become familiar with a fraction of these good qualities. It is astonishing how much of this precious life I have wasted on nothing at all. ^7-36

I have not made real offerings to the buddhas. I have not brought joy to others in a meaningful way. I have not done my part for the Dharma, or truly helped those in need. ^7-37

I have not given comfort to the fearful, or real relief to the suffering. All I have managed to do is cause pain — even to my own mother, starting from the moment I was in her womb. ^7-38

Because I have lacked real interest in the Dharma, both in the past and even now, I have ended up in this state of spiritual poverty. Who in their right mind would give up something so valuable? ^7-39

The Buddha taught that real interest and enthusiasm is the root of every kind of virtue. And that interest, in turn, grows from constantly reflecting on how our actions bring about real consequences. ^7-40

Suffering, unhappiness, every kind of fear, and losing the things we want — all of it comes about because of non-virtue. ^7-41

By doing good with a wholehearted mind, wherever that person is reborn, they will be met there with the merit and its results. ^7-42

But someone who does wrong, even while still hoping for happiness, will be struck down, wherever they are reborn, by the very suffering their own non-virtue brings about. ^7-43

Through the power of merit, one can be born inside a vast, fragrant, cooling lotus, nourished there by the buddha's beautiful words like food. Growing radiant as the flower opens under the buddha's light, one emerges with an extraordinary body, becoming an heir of the Jinas, standing right in their presence. ^7-44

But through non-virtue, the opposite happens: one is helplessly skinned alive, doused in molten metal boiled by raging fire, cut into pieces by blazing swords and spears, and left to fall onto a burning iron ground — all because of countless non-virtuous deeds. ^7-45

So I should build real enthusiasm for doing good, and train myself to hold it in genuine respect. And I should cultivate a healthy, steady confidence in my own ability to follow through with this practice. ^7-46

First, I should honestly check whether I am really ready for something, and only then decide whether to start or not. It is better not to begin at all than to start something and quit halfway. ^7-47

If I get into the habit of quitting halfway through, that pattern carries into future lives too, and only causes more trouble and suffering. And even the task I quit does not get properly finished either. ^7-48

I should apply self-confidence to these three: action, kleśas, and ability. To think, "I alone should do it" — that is the pride of action. ^7-49

This world, so often controlled by kleśas, is not really able to accomplish its own deeper good. Since others may not be able to do what I can, I should be the one to step up and take this on. ^7-50

When even others are willing to take on humbler tasks, how could I sit back and do nothing? I do not say this out of arrogance — having no arrogance at all is exactly the point. ^7-51

When a snake is already dead, even a crow can peck at it like a mighty eagle would. If I let myself feel weak and defeated, even small setbacks will end up hurting me. ^7-52

Is there any way out of hardship for someone who simply gives up and stops trying? But by building real, steady confidence in one's own effort, even great difficulties become workable. ^7-53

So, with a steady and determined mind, I should work through any setbacks or failings. If I let myself be defeated by them instead, my hope of overcoming real challenges becomes laughable. ^7-54

I should aim to rise above every challenge, and never let myself be defeated by one. As an heir of the Victorious Lion, I should hold onto exactly this kind of steady confidence. ^7-55

People overcome by arrogance are not the ones with real confidence at all — that kind of pride is just another kleśa in disguise. Genuinely confident people are never ruled by an enemy; it is the arrogant who end up ruled by the very enemy of their own pride. ^7-56

Puffed up with unhealthy pride, a person gets led straight into a bad rebirth; robbed of any real, lasting joy in this life, becoming, in a sense, dependent on scraps from others. ^7-57

Foolish, unappealing, and weak — that is how such a person ends up seen everywhere they go. Even someone with real discipline, if puffed up with unhealthy pride, falls into this same trap — so imagine how much worse it is for someone with even less going for them. ^7-58

Whoever uses real, healthy confidence to overcome unhealthy arrogance is truly confident, and a genuine hero. And whoever fully overcomes that enemy of arrogance, even when it tries to creep back in, brings about a genuine victory — one that truly benefits others as much as themselves. ^7-59

Living right in the middle of kleśas, I should stand firm against them in every way I can, like a lion standing unbothered among jackals — never letting the whole pack of kleśas get the better of me. ^7-60

Just as we instinctively protect our eyes when something dangerous comes close, I should protect myself just as carefully from ever falling under the control of kleśas, even in difficult situations. ^7-61

Just as someone throws themselves into a game they love, fully absorbed and wanting more, I should approach my own tasks with that same kind of genuine enthusiasm, never feeling like I have had enough. ^7-62

We work so hard hoping for happiness, though there is never any guarantee we will find it. But if the work itself is truly the cause of real happiness, why would doing it not bring joy in itself? ^7-63

If people are never satisfied chasing after pleasures that are really like honey smeared on the edge of a blade, why would I ever grow tired of doing good, whose result is nothing but real, peaceful happiness? ^7-64

So, in order to see any task through to the end, I should throw myself into it fully — like an elephant, worn out by the midday heat, joyfully diving into a cool lake. ^7-65

But if my strength runs low partway through, it is fine to pause, planning to return to it later. And once something is genuinely finished, I should set it aside and move on to what matters next. ^7-66

Like a seasoned warrior meeting an old enemy on the battlefield, I should skillfully dodge the weapons of kleśas, while staying ready to strike back and overcome them myself. ^7-67

Just as a warrior who drops his sword in battle rushes to grab it back out of fear, I should just as quickly recover my mindfulness if it slips, by remembering the real dangers of losing my way. ^7-68

Just as poison spreads through the whole body once it enters the bloodstream, non-virtue can spread through the whole mind the moment it finds even a small opening. ^7-69

Just as someone carrying a brimming bowl of oil, with an armed guard right behind threatening to kill them if they spill a drop, would stay intensely focused — a serious practitioner should hold that same kind of careful attention. ^7-70

So, just as I would jump up immediately if a snake landed in my lap, I should push away sleepiness and laziness with that same urgency, the instant I notice them. ^7-71

Whenever I make a mistake, I should take time to really reflect on it, and firmly resolve: "I am going to make sure this does not happen again." ^7-72

Thinking, "How can I make mindfulness a real, lasting habit in every situation?" I should actively seek out the right circumstances, and the right company, to help make that happen. ^7-73

Before taking on any task, I should remind myself of everything I have learned about being careful and mindful, and get myself properly prepared and energized first. ^7-74

Just as a wisp of cotton drifts wherever the wind carries it, I should let myself be carried along by real enthusiasm and joy. That is exactly how things actually get accomplished. ^7-75

*Thus ends Chapter Seven: "Diligence (*vīrya*)."* ^7-a

## Chapter 8: Meditative Concentration (*samādhi*)

Having cultivated diligence (*vīrya*) like this, I should settle my mind in *samādhi*. Someone whose mind is scattered lives constantly between the jaws of kleśas. ^8-1

Through physical and mental solitude, distraction does not arise. So I should step back from the busyness of ordinary life, and let go of unnecessary, scattered thinking. ^8-2

The reason I do not step back from busy, ordinary life is attachment — craving for gain and the like. So, to truly let go of that, a wise person should reflect carefully, like this: ^8-3

Knowing that *prajñā*, combined with calm abiding (*śamatha*) endowed with higher insight (*vipaśyanā*), overcomes kleśas, I should first seek calm abiding (*śamatha*) — and that itself comes from genuinely letting go of worldly attachment. ^8-4

Why would anyone who is impermanent hold onto real attachment for someone else who is just as impermanent? Because of that attachment, you might not even see them again for a thousand lifetimes. ^8-5

Without seeing that person, there is no joy, and the mind cannot settle down. And even when you do see them, you are never really satisfied — the craving just keeps burning, exactly like before. ^8-6

Being attached to other people completely clouds our ability to see things clearly. It kills any real sense of urgency about samsara, and in the end, it just leads to suffering and grief. ^8-7

Simply obsessing over that one person can cause this precious life to slip away, wasted. And because of that fleeting friendship, even our lasting connection to the Dharma gets damaged. ^8-8

Following the same path as people caught up in short-sighted, worldly concerns will surely lead somewhere bad. And if they are pulling you in a completely different direction from your own, what is really the point of relying on them? ^8-9

In one moment they are a friend, and in the next, suddenly an enemy; they get angry even about things that should make them happy. Ordinary people, caught up like this, are genuinely hard to please. ^8-10

Try to say something genuinely helpful, and they might get angry at you for it — even try to talk you out of doing what is actually good. And if you do not go along with them, they get angry, and that anger can lead them straight into trouble. ^8-11

Jealous of those doing better, competitive with equals, arrogant toward those doing worse when praised, and furious when criticized — when has real good ever come from associating with someone caught in this cycle? ^8-12

Associating with people caught up like this inevitably leads to self-praise, putting others down, and endless chatter about worldly pleasures — some kind of non-virtuous habit tends to follow. ^8-13

In this way, this kind of relationship really just ends up harming both people involved. It does not help me, and I do not end up helping them either. ^8-14

So I should keep a healthy distance from people caught up in this kind of pattern. When we do meet, I can be warm and friendly, but without becoming too close — just staying pleasant, at arm's length. ^8-15

Like a bee taking only the nectar it needs from a flower, I should take only what is genuinely useful, and stay unattached to the rest — as if meeting these situations for the very first time, without getting entangled. ^8-16

"I have got plenty of wealth and honor, and lots of people who like me" — holding onto this kind of pride is exactly what sets someone up for a terrifying shock after death. ^8-17

So, whatever a confused mind becomes attached to, it obsesses over endlessly — and along with that obsession, suffering just keeps coming back, again and again. ^8-18

That is why a wise person should not get attached in the first place — because fear itself grows out of attachment. Understanding that everything naturally slips away eventually, I should stay steady, and really see this clearly. ^8-19

Even if I gain plenty of wealth, fame, and popularity, there is no telling where — if anywhere — I will be able to carry it all when I die. ^8-20

If someone out there is criticizing me anyway, what real good does anyone else's praise do me? And if someone else is praising me anyway, what real harm does someone else's criticism do? ^8-21

Since even the Tathāgata himself cannot please every single being, with their endlessly different preferences, what hope does someone as ordinary as me have? So I should just let go of worrying about what everyone thinks. ^8-22

People criticize those without wealth, and they speak badly even of those who have it. Given how naturally difficult it is to please anyone this way, how could real, lasting happiness ever come from worrying about it? ^8-23

Since people caught up in short-sighted self-interest cannot really find joy except by serving themselves, the Buddha taught that such a person can never really be counted on as a true friend. ^8-24


[Sanskrit variant:] When pleasure arises only through self-interest, that pleasure is self-interest itself; as when property is lost, distress follows the loss of happiness. ^8-24x1
The deer, birds, and trees in the forest never speak an unkind word. When will I get the chance to simply live in that kind of peaceful company? ^8-25

Staying in a cave, an empty temple, or under a tree, when will I finally reach the point where I never look back, completely free of attachment? ^8-26

When will I finally live freely in vast, open spaces that belong to no one in particular, moving about as I please, without needing to hold onto any one place? ^8-27

Carrying just a simple bowl and a few basic things, wearing clothes no one else would even want, when will I finally live fearlessly, without needing to hide or protect this body at all? ^8-28

Visiting a charnel ground, where the dead are left, when will I finally look at the scattered bones there and truly recognize that my own body is just as subject to decay? ^8-29

This very body of mine will eventually become something so unpleasant that even jackals will not want to come near it, put off by the smell. ^8-30

Even this one body, born all at once, ends up decaying, its own flesh and bones eventually falling apart from each other — so why even mention how much more easily other friendships fall apart? ^8-31

We are born alone, and we die alone too. Since no one else can actually share our suffering with us, what real good do clingy friendships even do? ^8-32

Just as travelers on a long journey take shelter wherever they happen to stop for the night, in the same way, beings traveling through samsara take up temporary lodging in one life after another. ^8-33

As long as I have not yet been carried away, mourned by others, weighed down in grief — that is exactly how much time I still have left. I should use it wisely, and head toward a life of real solitude. ^8-34

Free of both close attachments and resentments, living alone in solitude, and having already accepted, ahead of time, that I will die anyway — so that when death actually comes, there is no real grief left to feel. ^8-35

Since no one would be nearby to cause grief or distraction, nothing would pull me away from keeping the Buddha in mind and staying focused on genuine practice. ^8-36

So, in a forest full of natural beauty and calm, needing very little to sustain me there, bringing real peace and happiness, and settling every kind of distraction, I should choose to live there, on my own. ^8-37

Letting go of every other worry, and focusing only on training my own mind, I should work diligently to bring it to rest, and truly tame it. ^8-38

In this life and beyond, chasing after sensory pleasures brings nothing but trouble: in this life, it can lead to violence and imprisonment; in future lives, it leads to hell and worse. ^8-39

For the sake of these desires, people have gone to great lengths in the past — sending messages back and forth, willing to do wrong without hesitation, even risking their own reputation. ^8-40

I have thrown myself into danger, and used up everything I have, all chasing after these desires — and yet it is exactly these desires that people end up clinging to with the most joy, again and again. ^8-41

Since a body, when you really look at it, is nothing more than a skeleton — with no independence or true self at its core — why do I still cling to it so fully, refusing to move toward real, lasting peace? ^8-42

Someone who was once eagerly pursued and courted might, upon finally seeing the truth clearly, feel embarrassed enough to cover their face — whether they had glimpsed it before or not. ^8-43

Imagine that same face, now picked clean by vultures, exactly as desire has already stripped it bare in truth. Seeing this clearly, why would I still run from facing it? ^8-44

If, even while others can see it plainly, someone still jealously guards a body as if it were theirs to keep, then why not guard it against the one who really consumes it in the end — death itself? ^8-45

Seeing that this body is really just a heap of flesh, eventually eaten by vultures and scavengers, why decorate it now with garlands and perfume, as if honoring a meal meant for someone else? ^8-46

If just seeing a still, lifeless skeleton frightens you, why are you not even more frightened seeing that same skeleton walking around, alive, like something reanimated? ^8-47

Since both waste and saliva come from the very same food, why does one disgust you while the other does not? ^8-48

Someone caught up in desire, confused about what real impurity even means, will not enjoy a soft, cotton-filled pillow just because it does not smell nice — yet happily embraces a body that constantly does. ^8-49

If you are attached to a covered-up body, why would you not also want to see it uncovered? And if there is really no need to see it that way, why cling to it so tightly even while it stays covered? ^8-50

If you are really not drawn to something impure, then why would you embrace another person at all — really just a cage of bones held together with sinew, covered in a layer of flesh and blood? ^8-51

You yourself are already made up of plenty of impurity, and you rely on that very body constantly — yet you still crave another bag of impurity too, driven by that same confused desire. ^8-52

If you say, "I enjoy this body, and want to touch and look at it," then how could you truly desire flesh that, by its very nature, has no mind or awareness of its own? ^8-53

The mind you are actually drawn to can never be touched or seen directly. And whatever you can touch or see has no awareness of its own. So really, what is the point of the whole embrace? ^8-54

It is not that surprising that people fail to see the impure nature of someone else's body — but it really is astonishing that they somehow fail to see that same nature in their own. ^8-55

Why, with a mind caught up in attachment to impurity, would someone ignore a fresh, dew-covered lotus opening in clear sunlight, and instead take delight in what is really just a basket of impurity? ^8-56

If you would not want to touch ground that has been soiled, then why would you want to touch the very body that impurity actually comes from? ^8-57

If you are truly not drawn to impurity, then why would you embrace another body, which grew, quite literally, from an impure source? ^8-58

A small worm, born from impurity, does not appeal to you at all — yet you still desire a full human body, made up mostly of impurity, and born the very same way. ^8-59

Not only do you fail to see your own impurity clearly, you go further and crave someone else's bag of impurity too, driven by the very same craving. ^8-60

Even pleasant things, like fragrant camphor, rice, or vegetables, once chewed and spit back out, make even the ground beneath them seem dirty and unpleasant. ^8-61

If you still doubt that the body is really this impure, even after seeing it directly, go look at other bodies left behind in a charnel ground, and see for yourself. ^8-62

Knowing that simply peeling back the skin would reveal something deeply unsettling underneath, how is it that people still find themselves drawn back to that same body, again and again? ^8-63

Even the pleasant scent on a body is really just perfume, like sandalwood — it is not something the body itself produces. So why would anyone become attracted to a person just because of a scent that actually comes from somewhere else? ^8-64

If it is really better not to be attached to the body because of its natural odor, then why do people pointlessly burn incense on it, just trying to make it seem more appealing than it really is? ^8-65

And if that pleasant scent really is just sandalwood, what does it actually have to do with the body wearing it? So again, why be attracted to a person over a scent that is not even theirs? ^8-66

If the body's natural, unadorned state — long hair and nails, yellowing teeth, its natural smell — is honestly something a little unsettling to look at plainly, then ^8-67

why put so much effort into grooming and polishing it, almost like sharpening a weapon that will only harm you in the end? This kind of confused effort, spent obsessing over the body, has caused so much needless turmoil in the world. ^8-68

If seeing a bare, still skeleton in a charnel ground unsettles you, how could you take pleasure in a whole town full of these same moving skeletons, walking around you every day? ^8-69

This kind of impure indulgence is not even free — pursuing it brings real exhaustion in this life, and it can even lead to serious suffering in future ones. ^8-70

A child cannot grow wealth on their own, so it would be nice to simply reach maturity and finally enjoy life. But if someone spends their entire life just accumulating wealth, what is even left to enjoy by the time they are old? ^8-71

Some people, driven relentlessly by desire, wear themselves out completely with a full day's work, and come home each night, collapsing into bed as if already dead. ^8-72

Some people, worn down by the hardships of traveling far from home for work, suffer through long separations — and even while longing to see their family, they sometimes never get the chance again. ^8-73

Confused into thinking it is for their own good, some people sell their very freedom for the sake of wealth — and even then, without actually gaining what they hoped for, they just end up pushed around by other people's demands. ^8-74

Some people sell their own labor and end up completely under someone else's control — and even when their wives give birth, it might happen with nowhere better to go than under a tree, out in the open. ^8-75

People fooled by desire, thinking, "I just need to make a living," willingly risk their lives in dangerous work, and even become someone else's servant, all chasing after profit. ^8-76

Some people caught up in these desires end up having their bodies mutilated; some face terrible violence; some are struck down; some are even burned — all driven, ultimately, by desire. ^8-77

Understand clearly that wealth brings endless trouble — through the effort of earning it, the anxiety of protecting it, and the grief of eventually losing it. People distracted by attachment to wealth like this never really find a way out of the suffering built into samsara. ^8-78

For people caught up chasing desires, there are so many drawbacks, and only the tiniest of rewards — like a cart-pulling animal that only occasionally gets a mouthful of grass along the way. ^8-79

And even that small reward is not hard for a beast of burden to get — yet for the sake of that same tiny reward, people caught up in endless work end up wasting this rare and precious human life. ^8-80

Sense pleasures inevitably fall apart in the end, and can even lead to a bad rebirth. Since they are not even that valuable to begin with, chasing them brings constant, exhausting trouble for very little real return. ^8-81

If buddhahood could actually be reached with just a fraction of that same effort, then someone caught up chasing desires suffers even more than someone practicing this path — yet without ever reaching enlightenment at all. ^8-82

Thinking carefully about the suffering that awaits, sense pleasures are actually worse than weapons, poison, fire, cliffs, or even enemies — nothing really compares to how much damage they can cause. ^8-83

So, having grown genuinely tired of chasing sense pleasures, I should cultivate real joy in solitude instead — in peaceful, quiet places, free of conflict and kleśas. ^8-84

In a spacious, simple dwelling, cooled by moonlight and the scent of sandalwood, walking through the quiet, gently stirring forest breeze, thinking only about how best to help others. ^8-85

Living freely in an empty house, under a tree, or in a cave, for as long as I like, letting go of the exhausting burden of owning and protecting things, I can finally live with real ease, worry-free. ^8-86

Living freely, without attachment, and without being tied down to anything — this kind of contented, easy life is genuinely hard to find, even for the wealthiest and most powerful people. ^8-87

Having reflected on the benefits of solitude in these and other ways, I should calm all my scattered thinking, and turn my attention to meditating on bodhicitta. ^8-88

First, I should work hard at truly seeing myself and others as equal. Since everyone equally wants happiness and wants to avoid suffering, I should protect every being just as carefully as I protect myself. ^8-89

Although the hand and other body parts are all different, they are still protected together as parts of one single body. In the same way, even though different people each have their own happiness and suffering, we are all equally united by that same simple wish to be happy. ^8-90

Even though my own suffering does not harm anyone else's body, it still feels completely unbearable to me — simply because I identify so strongly with myself. ^8-91

In the same way, even though someone else's suffering does not directly affect me, it should feel just as hard to bear, once I really learn to identify with them the same way I do with myself. ^8-92

I should work to relieve others' suffering, simply because it is suffering, exactly like my own. And I should work to help others, simply because they too are living beings, exactly like I am. ^8-93

Since both I and everyone else equally want to be happy, what real difference is there between us, that would justify me working only toward my own happiness? ^8-94

Since neither I nor anyone else wants to suffer, what real difference is there between us, that would justify me protecting only myself, and not everyone else too? ^8-95

If I do not bother protecting others from suffering simply because it does not harm me directly, then why do I protect myself from suffering that has not even happened yet — which, right now, also does not harm me at all? ^8-96

The idea that "I am the one who will experience that future suffering" does not really hold up — because in a real sense, the person who dies and the person reborn afterward are not exactly the same continuous person. ^8-97

If the idea is that only the person actually experiencing suffering should be the one protected from it, then, using that same logic, since the foot's pain does not belong to the hand, why does the hand still reach out to help the foot? ^8-98

Someone might object, "True, that reasoning is not perfectly logical either, but we protect our own foot because we feel like one unified self." But then, whatever kind of thinking is not fully logical — for self or for others — should be let go of, wherever we reasonably can. ^8-99

What we call a person or a self over time is really just a convenient label — like calling a string of beads a rosary, or a group of soldiers an army — not some solid, unchanging thing. Since there is no single, fixed owner of suffering, whose suffering, exactly, is it supposed to be? ^8-100

Since suffering does not really belong exclusively to any one fixed owner, there is no fundamental difference between my suffering and someone else's. Suffering should simply be relieved because it is suffering — there is no real point insisting on rigid boundaries here. ^8-101

Why should not everyone's suffering be addressed equally? There is really no good reason to argue otherwise. If suffering deserves to be relieved at all, it deserves to be relieved for everyone — and if not, well, that includes me too, since I am just one being among countless others. ^8-102

Someone might ask, "Why deliberately cultivate compassion, when it brings its own kind of suffering?" But when you really consider the vast suffering of all beings, how could the discomfort of compassion possibly compare? ^8-103

If accepting a little suffering myself could genuinely reduce the greater suffering of many others, then someone with real compassion should be willing to take that on, for everyone's sake. ^8-104

That is exactly why the bodhisattva Candraprabha, even knowing about the danger a cruel king posed, did not avoid his own suffering — because he understood that accepting it would end the suffering of many others. ^8-105

In this way, once I have truly grown used to feeling joy at relieving others' suffering, I would even be willing to enter the worst suffering myself, the way a swan happily glides into a beautiful lotus pond. ^8-106

Would not the immense joy of helping beings become free from suffering be more than enough reward on its own? What more could someone who genuinely wants real freedom possibly need? ^8-107

So, even while working hard to help others, there is no need for pride or self-congratulation. Taking joy purely in the act of helping, there is no need to hope for some personal payoff later. ^8-108

Just as I naturally protect myself even from something as small as an unkind remark, I should cultivate that same protective, caring attitude toward everyone else too. ^8-109

Through pure habit, we have come to feel a sense of me even in relation to something as basic as the biological material that formed this body — something with no real, solid self in it to begin with. ^8-110

In that same way, why could I not train myself to feel that same sense of connection toward other people's bodies too? It is really no harder than how I already came to feel connected to this body of mine. ^8-111

Recognizing that I myself have plenty of faults, while others have so many good qualities, I should practice letting go of self-centeredness, and instead work on genuinely embracing others. ^8-112

Just as we naturally accept that the hands and feet are simply parts of one body, why not, in that same way, come to see all beings as parts of one greater whole? ^8-113

Just as, through sheer habit, a sense of me developed around this body — which actually has no independent self at its core — why could not that same sense of connection develop, through habit, toward other beings too? ^8-114

In this way, even while helping others, there is really no reason to feel proud or self-congratulatory — just as I do not expect a reward simply for feeding myself when I am hungry. ^8-115

So, just as I naturally protect myself even from something small like an unkind word, I should train myself, through practice, to hold that same caring, protective attitude toward every being. ^8-116

This is exactly why the compassionate bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, out of a wish to remove fear from beings, even blessed his own name with the power to bring comfort. ^8-117

I should not shy away from this challenging practice, because through the power of habit, even something whose very name once seemed frightening can eventually become something we genuinely miss if it is gone. ^8-118

Anyone who wants to quickly protect both themselves and others should practice this profound, secret method: exchanging places between self and other. ^8-119

Because of my attachment to this body, even small threats bring on real fear. So really, who would not feel some resentment toward a body that causes this much anxiety, almost like it is an enemy? ^8-120

Wanting simply to satisfy the body's hunger and thirst, some people end up killing birds, fish, and deer, and lying in wait along their trails. ^8-121

For the sake of profit and status, some people go as far as harming even their own parents, or taking what rightfully belongs to sacred, communal purposes — and for that, they end up facing terrible consequences. ^8-122

What truly wise person would desire, protect, and honor the body the way we usually do, without any clear-eyed perspective? Who would not instead relate to it with a balanced, healthy attitude, without extreme attachment? ^8-123

"If I give this away, what will I have left for myself?" — thinking this way, focused only on self-interest, is the mindset of a truly stingy, hungry-ghost-like mind. "If I use this for myself, what will I have left to give others?" — thinking this way, focused on others' benefit, is the mindset of the truly generous. ^8-124

If I harm others for my own sake, I will end up suffering the consequences later. But if I am willing to accept some hardship myself for the sake of others, I open the door to every kind of real excellence. ^8-125

Wanting to put myself above everyone else just leads to a bad rebirth, confusion, and misery. But redirecting that same energy toward wanting others to succeed instead leads to a good rebirth and genuine respect. ^8-126

If I make others serve my own interests, I will eventually find myself in a position of servitude too. But if I put myself in service of others, I will end up experiencing real leadership and genuine respect instead. ^8-127

All the happiness that exists in this world comes from genuinely wanting others to be happy. And all the suffering that exists in this world comes from wanting only one's own happiness. ^8-128

Why say more? Just look at the clear difference between someone caught up in pure self-interest and the buddha, who worked entirely for the benefit of others. ^8-129

If I do not genuinely learn to trade my own comfort for taking on others' burdens, I will never truly attain awakening — and I will not find real, lasting happiness in this life either. ^8-130

Setting aside future lives for a moment — even just in this life, someone who will not put in real effort, and an employer who will not pay people fairly, never really succeed at their own goals either. ^8-131

By giving up the real happiness that comes from benefiting both this life and future ones, and instead causing suffering to others, a confused person just ends up bringing terrible suffering down on themselves. ^8-132

All the violence, fear, and suffering in this world really comes down to clinging to a sense of self. So what good is this great demon of self-clinging really doing for me? ^8-133

Without truly letting go of self-centeredness, there is no way to actually escape suffering — just like there is no way to avoid being burned while you are still holding onto the fire. ^8-134

So, in order to end the harm I bring on myself, and to ease the suffering of others, I should give myself fully to others, and hold them just as dear as I hold myself. ^8-135

"I now belong to others' care" — mind, you need to really accept this. From now on, do not think about anything except the benefit of every being. ^8-136

It is not right to use my eyes and other abilities only for my own self-interest, when really they exist to serve everyone. And it is certainly not right to use those same abilities to harm others. ^8-137

So, I should put others first, and whatever I find of value in myself, I should offer it up and put it to real use for their benefit. ^8-138

Treating someone in a humbler position as if they were me, and treating myself as if I were someone else, I should try, as a training exercise, seeing what it is like to feel envy, rivalry, and pride directed at myself this way. ^8-139

"That person over there gets honored — not me. I do not have what they have. They get praised, while I get criticized. They are happy, while I struggle." ^8-140

"I am the one doing all the hard work, while that person just relaxes. They are seen as important, while people say I have nothing to offer at all." ^8-141

"What is the point of saying I have no good qualities? I have plenty! Sure, some people surpass me, but I surpass others too." ^8-142

"My decline in discipline or *prajñā* is not really my fault — it is just the power of kleśas at work. I should do whatever I can to help myself, and even accept some harm along the way if that is what it takes." ^8-143

"But if I am not even worth helping, then why is that other person looking down on me? What good do their fancy qualities even do for me? Fine, they are the one with all the qualities, apparently." ^8-144

"This person, teetering right on the edge of a terrible rebirth themselves, has no real compassion for suffering beings at all — yet they are the one showing off their supposed good qualities, hoping to impress people who actually know better." ^8-145

"Comparing themselves to me as if we were equals, just to make themselves look better, they will go out of their way — even starting arguments — to make sure they end up with more wealth and recognition than I have." ^8-146

"How can I make sure everyone knows about my own good qualities, while making sure no one ever hears a word about this other person's?" ^8-147

"My own faults should stay hidden, and I should be the one receiving praise and offerings, not that person. I should be the one who gets ahead, while they get nothing." ^8-148

"For a long time, I will actually enjoy watching this person go through some misfortune, becoming a target of ridicule and criticism from everyone around." ^8-149

"And this troublesome person even claims to rival me? Do they really match up to me in knowledge, intelligence, looks, family background, or wealth?" ^8-150

"Hearing my own good qualities announced and celebrated by everyone like this fills me with real joy, and I thoroughly enjoy this happy feeling." ^8-151

"Even if this person has some wealth, if they are really supposed to be working for me, I should just give them the bare minimum to survive on, and take the rest for myself by force." ^8-152

"I should make sure this person never gets to be truly happy, and stays constantly exposed to harm from me instead — after all, this is the very person who has caused me trouble in hundreds of ways, throughout this endless samsara." ^8-153

So, mind, even though countless lifetimes have already passed with you working only for your own benefit, all that tremendous effort has produced nothing but ongoing suffering. ^8-154

So now, truly commit yourself to working for the benefit of others instead. Since the Buddha never taught anything false, you will come to see for yourself, later on, exactly how valuable this really is. ^8-155

If you had actually taken up this practice sooner, you would not be stuck now in this state, still lacking the complete happiness of buddhahood. ^8-156

So, just as you already learned to feel "this is me" about a body that originally came from someone else's biological material, you should learn, in that same way, to feel that same closeness toward other people too. ^8-157

Having truly become devoted to others' interests, take whatever you find of value in your own life and body, and put it directly to use helping them. ^8-158

"I am happy, while that other person is not. I am respected, while they are overlooked. I am the helpful one, while they contribute nothing." Given all that, mind, why would you not feel a bit of jealousy toward your own self-centered habits? ^8-159

I should be willing to give up some of my own comfort, and take on some of others' suffering instead, constantly asking myself, "What is this self-centeredness actually doing for me?" and really look honestly at my own faults. ^8-160

I should be quick to take responsibility, even for others' mistakes, treating them almost as my own, while openly admitting even the smallest of my own faults to others. ^8-161

I should let go of chasing my own reputation, and instead speak well of others, putting myself humbly to work, serving whatever others actually need. ^8-162

This self-centered mind of mine is naturally full of faults, so I should not go around praising it for some occasional good quality. In fact, I should be perfectly fine if no one ever finds out about whatever good it does. ^8-163

In short, whatever harm this self-centered mind has caused others in the past, purely for my own benefit — now let that harm come back onto me instead, for the sake of all beings. ^8-164

Do not let this mind grow forceful and unruly. Instead, keep it gently restrained, the way a shy newcomer might hold back, with real humility and caution. ^8-165

"Do this. Stay this way. Do not do that." This is exactly how I should keep this mind of mine in check — and if it steps out of line, I should firmly bring it back. ^8-166

But if, even after all this guidance, you, mind, still refuse to cooperate — since you are really the root cause of every fault — you deserve to be firmly reined in. ^8-167

"That earlier time when you managed to get away with harming me is over now. I see you clearly now — there is nowhere left for you to hide. I am going to break down all of your arrogance." ^8-168

"Give up, once and for all, this idea that you still have some separate interest of your own to protect. I have already committed you to serving others — so stop complaining, and just get on with it." ^8-169

"If, out of carelessness, you fail to genuinely dedicate yourself to beings, I will hand you over instead to the guardians of hell." ^8-170

"You have caused me so much long-lasting suffering in the past, through this exact same self-centered pattern. Remembering all of that now, I am going to break down this self-centeredness of yours, once and for all." ^8-171

If I genuinely want happiness, I should not chase it through self-centeredness. And if I genuinely want to protect myself, I should instead focus on always protecting others. ^8-172

The more I pamper and indulge this body, the more delicate and overly sensitive it becomes, falling into the very same trap, again and again. ^8-173

And once caught in that pattern, even everything on this whole earth would not be enough to satisfy its endless wants. So really, who could ever hope to fulfill that kind of craving? ^8-174

Impossible desires just breed more kleśas and a growing sense of discouragement. But for someone who is not dependent on getting anything in particular, their sense of real contentment never runs dry. ^8-175

So I should not give this body's constant desires any extra room to expand. Someone who does not cling to appearances and pleasant sensations is really living the good life. ^8-176

In the end, this body just becomes ashes — completely still, moved only by outside forces. So why would I cling to this uncomfortable, impure form as if it were really me? ^8-177

Whether alive or already as good as dead, what real use is this machine of a body to me? What real difference is there between it and a simple clump of earth? Why can I not just let go of this misplaced pride? ^8-178

By constantly pampering this body, I have only piled up pointless suffering for myself — through attachment and frustration over something that is really no different from a block of wood. What is the actual point? ^8-179

Whether I pamper this body carefully, or it ends up eaten by vultures someday, the body itself feels no attachment or resentment either way. So why am I still so attached to it? ^8-180

If this body itself has no idea whether it is being criticized or praised, then really, who have I been working so hard to please and protect, all this time? ^8-181

Someone might say, "Whoever finds my body appealing is truly my friend." But since literally everyone naturally cares about their own body, why would not that same logic make me feel warmly toward everyone, equally? ^8-182

So, without clinging to it, I should offer this body up entirely for the benefit of others. Even though it has plenty of flaws, I can still put it to good, meaningful use, like any other tool. ^8-183

That is more than enough of behaving like an ordinary, self-centered person. I should follow the example of the truly wise instead, remembering everything about staying careful and alert, and pushing back against sleepiness and mental fog. ^8-184

So, in order to clear away these mental obstacles, I should turn my mind away from unhelpful patterns, and constantly settle it, through meditation, on what is genuinely true and worthwhile. ^8-185

*Thus ends Chapter Eight: "Meditative Concentration (*samādhi*)."* ^8-a

## Chapter 9: Wisdom (*prajñā*)

The Buddha taught all these practices — patience, diligence, and the rest — for one real purpose: *prajñā*. So, since I want to be free from suffering, I need to develop genuine *prajñā*. ^9-1

There are said to be two ways of understanding truth: the conventional way (*saṃvṛti*) and the ultimate way (*paramārtha*). The ultimate truth (*paramārtha*) is something ordinary thinking cannot fully grasp — and ordinary thinking itself is what we call conventional truth (*saṃvṛti*). ^9-2

Within this everyday world, there are two kinds of people: those who have real insight, and ordinary people. And the understanding of ordinary people gets challenged and corrected by those with deeper insight. ^9-3

And even among those with insight, people with a deeper level of understanding correct those at an earlier stage. Both sides often use the same kinds of examples, without digging into their full implications. ^9-4

Ordinary people see things and assume they're solidly, truly real. Someone with real insight sees the same things, but recognizes they're more like an illusion. This is the heart of the disagreement between ordinary and awakened understanding. ^9-5

Even something that seems totally obvious, like the shape of an object, is just something we've all agreed to see a certain way — not something proven by honest examination. It's a bit like how something unclean might get casually called "clean," just by habit, even though it's not accurate. ^9-6

Someone might object: "But the Tathāgata himself described things directly, in ordinary terms, to help people understand. Surely he wasn't saying these things don't even exist for a single moment — wouldn't that even go against what's normally accepted, in everyday terms?" ^9-7

There's actually no problem with how someone with real insight understands everyday reality — they simply see it clearly, exactly as it truly functions on that level. The problem would be following ordinary assumptions blindly — for instance, mistakenly thinking a human body is truly "clean" — which contradicts what careful examination actually shows. ^9-8

Someone might ask: "Just as good merit comes from honoring even an illusion-like buddha, exactly as it would a real one, then if beings themselves are also like illusions, how could anyone actually be reborn after death?" ^9-9

For as long as the right conditions keep coming together, an illusion-like appearance can keep showing up, again and again. Just because something lasts a long time doesn't actually mean it's solidly, permanently real. ^9-10

There's no real non-virtue involved in "killing" a mere illusion, since an illusion has no mind. But for a being who does have a mind — even if that mind, in the deepest sense, is also illusion-like — real consequences, both good and bad, can still arise. ^9-11

Since a magician's spell can't actually create a real mind, no illusion can ever have a mind of its own. But illusions that arise from various different causes can still appear in all sorts of different forms. ^9-12

There's no single cause anywhere that alone has the power to produce absolutely everything. Someone might object: "If nirvana is truly, ultimately real, while samsara is only conventionally real—" ^9-13

"—then even a buddha would technically still be wandering in samsara on the conventional level, so what's even the point of practicing this whole path? And since causes and conditions never stop flowing, the illusion-like appearance of things should never actually end either." ^9-14

But once the flow of causes and conditions truly comes to an end, nothing further arises, even on the conventional level. And once confusion itself has completely ended, there's no one left to perceive any illusion in the first place. ^9-15

So when no illusion at all is appearing to you anymore, what exactly is there left to see? And if you say some other "form" is still there at that moment, well — that so-called form is nothing more than the mind itself. ^9-16

But if the mind itself is also illusion-like, in that same way, then who is there left to perceive anything at all? The Tathāgata taught exactly this: "The mind cannot directly see itself." ^9-17

Just as a sword's blade can't cut itself, the mind can't perceive itself in that same direct way either. But someone might object: "Doesn't a lamp illuminate its own light directly, though?" ^9-18

But actually, a lamp doesn't quite "illuminate itself" in a meaningful sense — it was never covered in darkness to begin with. Saying it "illuminates itself" is a bit like saying the color blue is "blue by depending on nothing at all" — the concept doesn't add anything. ^9-19

In that same way, we notice that some things depend on other things, and some don't. But the color blue was never "non-blue" first, only to somehow turn itself blue afterward. ^9-20

If we can only say, "the lamp illuminates," because some awareness recognized that fact, then by what other awareness could we possibly know and declare that "the mind is inherently self-aware and clear"? ^9-21

Since nobody can actually observe this "self-awareness" directly, debating whether it's clear or unclear is pointless — a bit like arguing over how gracefully the daughter of a woman who's never had children might dance. There's simply nothing real there to discuss. ^9-22

Someone might object: "But if there's no such thing as the mind being aware of itself, how could we ever remember a past experience? Memory happens because of a real connection formed when we actually experienced something — like remembering a mouse bite because of the poison that later shows up." ^9-23

Awareness of an object, arising together with the right causes and conditions, absolutely can be recalled clearly later — but that doesn't require the awareness to have somehow directly perceived itself at the time. It's like how a magic ointment lets someone see a hidden treasure vase, without the ointment itself needing to be seen in that process. ^9-24

None of this denies our everyday experience of seeing, hearing, or knowing things. What's being challenged here is the habit of grasping onto these experiences as solid, ultimately real things — because that grasping is the true root of suffering. ^9-25

An illusion isn't completely separate from the mind experiencing it, but it isn't exactly identical to the mind either, since it's constantly changing. If it were truly identical, it couldn't be considered a separate, real object at all. And if it's separate, how could it be considered solidly, truly real either? ^9-26

Just as an illusion appears without being ultimately real, the mind observing it should be understood in that same way — something that appears, without needing to be ultimately, solidly real. If this whole samsara had some solid, unchanging basis behind it, then it would end up being just as empty and unchanging as open space. ^9-27

How could something without any real, solid existence still function or act, just by depending on something else equally lacking real existence? In this view, your own mind would end up completely alone, with no real object out there to relate to at all. ^9-28

If simply having a mind free of any grasped object automatically made everyone a buddha, then what real value would there even be in this whole analysis of reducing everything down to just "mind alone"? ^9-29

Even understanding, intellectually, that something is like an illusion — how could that alone actually stop kleśas from arising? After all, even a magician can end up feeling desire for the very illusion he himself creates. ^9-30

That's simply because the magician hasn't yet let go of the underlying habits of desire connected to that particular object. So even while creating the illusion, his familiarity with emptiness itself is still too weak to override that old habit. ^9-31

By becoming familiar with the habit of understanding emptiness (*śūnyatā*), we can gradually let go of our deep habit of seeing things as solidly real. And by getting used to the idea that "nothing at all truly exists," we eventually let go of even that idea too, later on. ^9-32

When we carefully examine something and conclude it "doesn't exist," and we can't actually find it anywhere under that analysis, then how could its "non-existence" — which has nothing solid to depend on either — still somehow remain as an object in front of the mind? ^9-33

When neither "it exists" nor "it doesn't exist" remains in front of the mind as something to grasp onto, then, since there's no other possible way left to conceptualize it, the mind simply comes to rest, completely at peace, without grasping onto anything. ^9-34

May all of us, sharing this same nature, come to truly understand that everything is, in this deep sense, just like open space. And yet, people who chase only their own selfish happiness, driven by the countless causes behind conflict and excitement— ^9-154

—end up swinging between agitation and excitement, going through grief, exhausting struggles, arguments, and even real violence against each other — surviving, ultimately, through non-virtue, and facing tremendous, exhausting hardship along the way. ^9-155

Even after being reborn again and again into fortunate circumstances, and enjoying plenty of happiness there, people still end up, after death, falling into long, unbearable suffering in a bad rebirth. ^9-156

Within this samsara, there are countless dangerous pitfalls waiting. And the true nature of things there never quite matches how it actually appears to us. Since different traditions and philosophies even contradict each other about what that "true nature" even is, there's no single, solid, consistent version of reality to be found within samsara at all. ^9-157

And this same samsara contains a boundless, unbearable ocean of suffering, beyond any real comparison. And within it, our own strength is so limited, and even our own lifespan is painfully short. ^9-158

And even while chasing after a long, healthy life within samsara, our time gets eaten up by hunger, exhausting labor, sleep, being harassed by others, and getting entangled in pointless relationships with immature, self-centered people. ^9-159

Life slips away pointlessly, so quickly, and real insight and wisdom remain extremely hard to develop. And in the middle of all this, what real way is there to overcome our deeply ingrained habit of constant distraction? ^9-160

And within samsara, negative influences work relentlessly to push us toward a truly severe, difficult rebirth. And within it, there are so many misleading paths, and it's hard to work through all the doubt and confusion. ^9-161

And it's difficult, once lost, to find this kind of rare, fortunate opportunity again. It's extremely rare for a buddha to even appear in the world at all. And it's hard to overcome the powerful current of kleśas. It's heartbreaking how relentlessly this suffering just keeps repeating, over and over. ^9-162

In this way, even while going through such intense suffering, beings often don't even recognize it clearly for what it is. For these beings, caught helplessly in this endless river of suffering, we should feel deep, genuine compassion. ^9-163

It's a bit like someone who repeatedly bathes themselves, only to immediately jump into fire afterward, over and over again. In that same way, even while living through truly intense suffering, some people still, strangely, imagine themselves to be perfectly happy and fine. ^9-164

In this way, living as if old age and death will simply never come for them, people carry on recklessly — until, quite suddenly, they're struck down, and fall straight into an unbearable, difficult rebirth. ^9-165

When, then, will I finally be able to bring real relief to these beings, burning in the fire of such intense suffering — offering them, like a gentle, soothing rain from clouds of genuine merit, a real share of my own happiness and goodness? ^9-166

When will I finally be able to build up a great store of goodness, with real sincerity and care, in a way that's completely free from grasping (*abhiniveśa*) onto things as solid and fixed — and then, for the sake of those harmed by that very habit of grasping, finally help them discover the truth of *śūnyatā* for themselves? ^9-167

*Thus ends Chapter Nine: "Wisdom (*prajñā*)."* ^9-a

## Chapter 10: Dedication

Through whatever goodness I've gained from writing this guide to the bodhisattva's way of life, may every being come to enter this same path. ^10-1

May everyone suffering in body or mind, everywhere, find, through this goodness, an ocean of real joy and happiness. ^10-2

May they never lose that happiness for as long as they remain in samsara. May the world find the same unbroken happiness that bodhisattvas experience. ^10-3

For every hell realm that exists throughout the universe, may the beings suffering there instead find the joy and happiness of a pure land (*buddhakṣetra*). ^10-4

May those suffering from bitter cold find warmth. And may boundless, cooling rivers, flowing like rain from the great compassion of the bodhisattvas, soothe those suffering from unbearable heat. ^10-5

May the forest of blade-like leaves in hell transform into a lush forest of fragrant sandalwood. May the terrible thorny trees there become wish-fulfilling trees instead. ^10-6

May the regions of hell become beautiful instead, filled with lotus-scented lakes, and made lovely by the sweet calls of geese, swans, and ducks. ^10-7

May the heaps of burning coals become heaps of jewels instead. May the scorching ground become smooth crystal. May the crushing mountains of hell become beautiful palaces, filled with buddhas receiving offerings. ^10-8

May the rain of burning stones and weapons become, from this moment on, a gentle rain of flowers instead. And may all the fighting there, striking one another with weapons, turn into a playful game of tossing flowers. ^10-9

May those drowning in that terrible river, their flesh gone and their bones white as jasmine flowers, gain, through this goodness, a beautiful new body, and rest peacefully by the riverbank, surrounded by joyful company. ^10-10

"Why do the terrifying servants of death — the crows and vultures — no longer frighten anyone here? What wonderful power has cleared away all this darkness and brought such joy?" — and then, looking up, may they see a radiant, protective figure in the sky, and, filled with overwhelming joy, become free of non-virtue and join him. ^10-11

Seeing the burning coals of hell suddenly put out by a shower of flowers mixed with fragrant water, and wondering, "What is this sudden happiness filling me so completely?" — may the beings there see the compassionate bodhisattva appear right in front of them. ^10-12

"Friends, let go of your fear, and come this way, quickly! Through whose power have we suddenly become free of suffering, filled instead with this wave of joy?" — a young, radiant protector has arrived, bringing fearlessness and safety to everyone. ^10-13

"Look — hundreds of gods bow their crowned heads to honor him; a shower of flowers falls gently from above; and in a beautiful palace, a thousand joyful voices sing his praise." Seeing such a gentle, radiant protector, may all the beings in hell now cry out with joy instead of pain. ^10-14

In this way, through the roots of my own goodness, may the beings suffering in hell see clouds — like great, compassionate bodhisattvas — bringing happiness, coolness, and a wonderful fragrance, and may the gentle rain falling from these clouds bring them real, deep joy. ^10-15

May animals be freed from the fear of being hunted and eaten by each other. And may hungry ghost (*preta*)s (*pretas*) find the same ease and happiness enjoyed in the most fortunate of human lands. ^10-16

May the compassionate hands of Avalokiteśvara pour forth a stream of nourishing milk, satisfying every hungry ghost (*preta*), and giving them a cooling, soothing bath, always. ^10-17

May the blind be able to see, and may the deaf always be able to hear. And may pregnant women give birth safely and without pain, just as smoothly as in the old stories of miraculous births. ^10-18

May those without clothes find something warm to wear, and may the hungry find nourishing food. May the thirsty find clean water, and refreshing drinks to enjoy. ^10-19

May the poor find real wealth. May those weighed down by grief find genuine joy. And may those who feel hopeless find fresh encouragement, and a true, lasting sense of stability. ^10-20

May every sick being be quickly healed of their illness. And may no kind of sickness ever afflict any being again. ^10-21

May the frightened become fearless. May the imprisoned be set free. May the powerless gain real strength, and may everyone's hearts grow gentle and kind toward one another. ^10-22

May all travelers, everywhere, find their journeys safe and pleasant. And may whatever they set out to do be accomplished easily, without struggle. ^10-23

May those traveling by boat or ship have their wishes fulfilled, and, arriving safely on shore, may they be joyfully reunited with their families. ^10-24

May those lost on a dangerous, empty road encounter kind fellow travelers, and continue their journey without exhaustion, safe from thieves and dangerous animals, along an easy and peaceful path. ^10-25

May the gods watch over and protect children, the elderly, and anyone without protection who finds themselves lost in deserts or other dangerous, pathless places — and also those who are asleep, unconscious, or intoxicated, and unable to protect themselves. ^10-26

May all beings be freed from every kind of hardship, blessed with faith (*śraddhā*), *prajñā*, and compassion (*karuṇā*), provided with good food and a good way of living, and may they always be able to remember their past lives clearly. ^10-27

May everyone have access to endless resources, as vast and inexhaustible as the sky itself, and may they enjoy them freely, in peace, without any conflict or harm toward one another. ^10-28

May beings who feel small or overlooked gain real presence and influence instead. And may those whose bodies have been worn down by hardship become healthy and strong instead. ^10-29

May all the women in the world become men (reflecting a historical Indian Buddhist assumption that highest spiritual status required male rebirth). May those of lowly birth attain high status, and may pride be overcome everywhere. ^10-30

Through this goodness of mine, may every single being, without exception, give up all non-virtue, and always act with real goodness instead. ^10-31

May they never lose touch with bodhicitta, and may they stay devoted to this way of life. May the Buddhas watch over and care for them fully, and may they give up anything that works against this path. ^10-32

May every being enjoy an immeasurably long life. May they always live happily, and may even the very sound of the word "death" never need to be heard. ^10-33

May every direction be filled with beautiful, wish-fulfilling groves, echoing everywhere with the sweet sound of the buddha's teachings. ^10-34

May the ground everywhere be free of gravel and rough patches, and may it become as smooth and level as the palm of a hand, gentle and beautiful like polished stone. ^10-35

In gatherings as vast as can be imagined, may many bodhisattvas remain present, adorning the whole earth simply through their own wonderful qualities. ^10-36

May every being constantly hear the sound of the true teaching, coming ceaselessly from birds, from trees, from beams of light, and even from the open sky itself. ^10-37

May they always have the chance to encounter the Buddhas and their spiritual heirs (*bodhisattvas*). And may they honor this Guide of the World with endless, generous offerings. ^10-38

May the rains come at just the right times, so that crops grow abundantly everywhere. May those in power govern fairly and with integrity, and may the whole world flourish and thrive. ^10-39

May medicines truly heal, and may prayers and mantras carry real power when recited. May all spirits, even fierce ones, be filled with genuine compassion. ^10-40

May no being ever suffer, may no being ever feel afraid or disrespected, and may no being's heart ever feel unhappy. ^10-41

May monasteries be filled with study and recitation, and remain well-kept and beautiful. May monastic communities always live in harmony, and may their genuine goals be fulfilled. ^10-42

May monks who want to train find real solitude to support their practice. And, letting go of all distractions, may their minds become calm and ready for deep meditation. ^10-43

May nuns be well provided for, and free from conflict and harm. And may everyone who has taken up a spiritual life stay true to their discipline, without ever losing their way. ^10-44

May those who have broken their discipline feel genuine remorse, and work continually to make things right. May they attain a fortunate rebirth, and there too, keep their commitments strong, without slipping. ^10-45

May the wise be honored, and may they always have what they need to sustain themselves. May their conduct remain pure, and may their good reputation spread far and wide. ^10-46

Without ever needing to experience the suffering of a bad rebirth, and without unnecessary hardship, may they quickly attain full awakening, in a body more excellent even than that of the gods. ^10-47

May every being, again and again, make heartfelt offerings to the Buddhas in countless ways, and may they always experience the boundless happiness that comes with full awakening. ^10-48

May the bodhisattvas' heartfelt wish to help all beings be fulfilled. And may whatever the great Guardians intend for the good of beings actually come to pass. ^10-49

And in that same way, may those following other paths within the Buddhist tradition also find true and lasting happiness. ^10-50

Through the kindness of Mañjuśrī, may I too, all the way until I reach a deep level of realization, always remember my past lives clearly, and always have the chance to live a meaningful, spiritual life. ^10-51

May I be content sustaining myself with whatever simple food is available, and always stay healthy and capable. And in every lifetime, may I find the right conditions for genuine, peaceful solitude. ^10-52

And whenever I wish to see him, or ask even a small question, may I always be able to see the compassionate Mañjuśrī directly, without any obstacle in the way. ^10-53

In order to accomplish real good for every being everywhere, throughout the whole universe, may my own way of life come to match the example set by Mañjuśrī. ^10-54

For as long as space itself exists, and for as long as any being remains, may I too continue to exist, working to relieve the suffering of the world. ^10-55

Whatever suffering exists for any being, may it come and ripen on me instead. And through the compassionate community of bodhisattvas, may all beings come to know real happiness. ^10-56

May this teaching — the one true remedy for the suffering of beings, and the source of all real happiness — remain in the world for a very long time, valued and supported. ^10-57

I bow to Mañjuśrī, through whose kindness this wholesome intention arose in me in the first place. And I bow, too, to my spiritual preceptor (*guru*), through whose kindness I have been able to grow and flourish on this path. ^10-58

*Thus ends Chapter Ten: "Dedication."* ^10-a
