## 1. Chapter One: The Good Things About Bodhicitta ^1-0


I bow down, with a respectful heart, to the Buddhas
who have the truth inside them, and to all their followers,
and to everyone else worth bowing to.
Now I will explain, simply and briefly, following the old teachings,
how to start living the way a follower of the Buddha lives. ^1-1


I am not going to say anything new here
that no one has ever said before.
And I am not very good at writing beautiful words either.
So I am not writing this to help other people.
I am writing it just to make these ideas grow stronger in my own mind. ^1-2


But maybe, by writing it,
my own faith in doing good will grow a little.
And if someone else who is a bit like me
happens to read this too, maybe it will help them as well. ^1-3


It is very, very hard to be born with the freedom and the good luck
to be able to learn things like this.
Now that I have that chance,
if I do not use it well, when will I ever get such a chance again? ^1-4


On a dark night, thick with clouds,
a flash of lightning lights up everything for just a moment.
In the same way, because of the Buddha's power,
good, wise thoughts appear in the world only once in a great while. ^1-5


That is why goodness is always so weak,
while doing wrong is so strong and so terrible.
Nothing can overcome that wrongdoing —
except the mind that wants to wake up fully for everyone's sake. ^1-6


The wise Buddhas thought about this for a very, very long time,
and they saw that this one thing helps the most.
Because of it, countless beings everywhere
can reach true happiness easily. ^1-7


If you want to end the many kinds of suffering in this world,
if you want to take away the unhappiness of others,
and if you want everyone to enjoy many kinds of happiness —
then never let go of this wish to wake up for others, called bodhicitta. ^1-8


The very moment bodhicitta is born in someone's heart,
even the saddest person, trapped in the prison of endless rebirth,
becomes someone we call a child of the Buddha,
someone worth bowing to, even for gods and humans. ^1-9


Like a wonderful potion that turns ordinary metal into gold,
bodhicitta takes this impure body of ours
and turns it into something as precious as a Buddha.
So hold on tightly to this thing called bodhicitta. ^1-10


The one guide of every living being, with endless wisdom,
looked closely at bodhicitta and found it truly precious.
So everyone who wants to leave behind
the endless wandering of life after life should hold it firmly too. ^1-11


All other good deeds are like banana trees —
once they give their fruit, they wither away and that is the end.
But the tree of bodhicitta keeps giving fruit
again and again, forever, without ever running out. ^1-12


Even someone who has done truly terrible things
can be saved instantly by relying on a brave protector, escaping great danger.
So why wouldn't a careful person also rely
on bodhicitta, which can free anyone in a single moment? ^1-13


Bodhicitta burns away great wrongdoings in an instant,
like the fire that ends an entire age of the world.
Its endless benefits
were explained by the wise and loving Maitreya to Sudhana. ^1-14


In short, this bodhicitta
can be understood as two kinds:
the wish to wake up and help everyone,
and actually doing something about it. ^1-15


Just as we can tell the difference
between wanting to go somewhere and actually going there,
in the same way, wise people should understand
the difference between these two, one step at a time. ^1-16


The wish to wake up and help others
already brings great rewards, even while we are still going through life after life.
But it does not bring
a constant, unbroken stream of goodness the way actually acting on it does. ^1-17


But from the moment someone truly takes up that mind,
determined never to turn back,
wishing to free every single living being,
without end, ^1-18


from that moment on, even while sleeping
or even when not paying attention,
a huge, endless stream of goodness
keeps growing, as vast as the sky. ^1-19


The Buddha himself taught this,
along with the reasons for it,
when a boy named Subāhu asked him,
so that it would help people who were still not sure of themselves. ^1-20


If just thinking, "I wish I could take away
the headaches of every living being,"
already gives someone with that kind, caring wish
an enormous amount of goodness, ^1-21


then think how much more goodness there must be
in someone who wishes to take away
the endless suffering of every single being,
and wants each one of them to have endless good qualities too. ^1-22


Does any father or mother
have a wish as kind as that?
Do even the gods, or the wise sages,
or even the great god Brahmā have such a wish? ^1-23


If these very beings never once
even dreamed of having such a wish for their own sake,
how could such a wish ever arise
for the sake of someone else? ^1-24


This precious, extraordinary mind,
one that others do not even have for themselves,
is a wonder that has never happened before —
and now it is born, for the sake of every living being. ^1-25


How could anyone possibly measure
the goodness of this precious mind,
which is the cause of joy for every wandering being,
and the true cure for their suffering? ^1-26


If simply wishing to help others
is already better than worshipping all the Buddhas,
then how much better must it be
to actually work hard for the happiness of every single being? ^1-27


People want to escape suffering,
yet they run straight toward it.
They want happiness, but because of confusion,
they destroy their own happiness as if it were their enemy. ^1-28


But someone who fills with every happiness
those who have no happiness at all
and who suffer so much,
and who cuts away all of their suffering, ^1-29


and who also clears away their confusion —
where could you ever find goodness equal to that?
Where could you find a friend like that?
Where could you find goodness like that? ^1-30


If even paying back a small kindness
is worth some praise,
then what more is there to say
about a bodhisattva, who helps without even being asked? ^1-31


People call someone kind and good
if they give a little plain food to a few people once,
feeding them for just a moment,
even if they look down on them while doing it, filling their stomachs for half a day. ^1-32


So what more is there to say
about someone who, for a very long time, gives countless beings
the very best happiness of the Buddhas,
fulfilling every wish they could ever have? ^1-33


The Buddha said that anyone
who has an unkind thought toward such a generous child of the Buddhas
will stay in a hell realm
for as many ages as they had unkind thoughts. ^1-34


But if someone instead has a mind full of trust and kindness toward them,
the good result of that grows even greater still.
Even when children of the Buddhas face great trouble,
wrongdoing does not arise in them, and goodness keeps growing naturally. ^1-35


I bow down to the body of anyone
in whom this precious, wonderful mind has been born,
and I go for refuge to this source of happiness,
who brings happiness even to those who try to hurt them. ^1-36


This is the end of the first chapter, called "The Good Things About Bodhicitta," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^1-a
