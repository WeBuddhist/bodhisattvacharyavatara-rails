## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 27 to 29 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. The afflictions are an enemy with no hands or feet, yet they harm us from inside our own mind. Let us look again and again at our own mind and ask why we practice patience with them and let them make us their slave.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a story that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Kyabje Ling Rinpoche once had a teacup. One day strong anger arose in him, and he threw the teacup to the ground and broke it. When the anger had calmed and his mind was at ease again, he gathered up the broken pieces of the cup, and deep regret arose in him. The affliction of anger had completely broken down his good sense and his self-control.

This story shows us that even though the afflictions have no body or weapon, they live inside our own mind and use us like their slave. It is not right for us to practice patience with an affliction that is harming us. So when the afflictions take control of our mind, we must resist them.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, the moment an affliction arises in me, I will recognize it as an enemy and not welcome it.

**Explanation:** *(Recognizing the afflictions)* The afflictions are the true enemy of our mind. If we practice patience with our own anger and craving, this is a mistake. If we give them free rein, we ruin ourselves. So the moment an affliction arises, we must recognize it as an enemy and stop it.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: this basis, so rare to find, is like ground that makes it easy to accomplish one's temporary and ultimate aims once it is obtained. Having now obtained it through favorable circumstance, and while possessing sound judgment able to distinguish what to adopt and reject, if I go on giving way to procrastination and laziness, failing to store up good karma and instead letting my present conduct run loose in just this way, so that I am led to the lower realms again later — is this not like being bewitched by a spell? Or, when we come to scorn ourselves, saying "this is exactly like having no mind at all," does the thought not arise in each of us that facing that head-on is truly painful? Yet even though it may be difficult in the moment to cut the continuity of one's faults, one can at least reduce how often one engages in the basis of fault. This is what is meant by "I do not even know what deludes me; what is within me?" — scorning oneself by asking, what is this trunk-like thing of delusion obscuring my own insides? What could possibly answer for it?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-27]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Have I, perhaps, become like one bewitched by a mantra cast by someone to confuse me? For at this juncture I simply have no mind able to recognize what is beneficial and what is harmful. As the Omniscient Great One has said: "One who does not understand though it is explained, does not realize though it is taught — my heart is like a great lump of iron, or, like the nature of stone, is left without any mind" — is it that I have no mind, as he says? On careful examination, neither of those two descriptions quite fits, and yet I myself do not know by what cause I have become so deluded and confused. Even when I think, within this body of mine, "let me accomplish some holy Dharma — guarding ethical discipline, engaging in study and reflection" — some power, not my own, keeps me from actually doing it. What agent of delusion could this be? There must certainly be one; since I have not yet identified it, I must examine again and again just what this is.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-27]]

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Gyaltsab's explanation of 4-27 continues directly from his explanation of 4-26, under the heading "second" for this verse's second couplet.)*

The freedoms and endowments are extremely hard to find, and of great benefit once found — so having obtained, by some favorable turn, this beneficial basis, if I, even while possessing the wisdom to distinguish benefit from harm, am led back to that same hellish state, then one should reflect: is this not just like being bewitched by a spell, so that I simply have no mind here to accomplish what is beneficial?

If one asks what deludes me: I myself do not know by what cause I have been so bewitched and deceived — one should reflect that there must certainly be some cause of delusion within me — and even if one falls under the sway of laziness at this, one should still reflect in just this way.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

*(Sabzang's explanation of 4-27 is the same combined passage given on 4-26's page, covering both verses at once.)*

Moreover, this basis of the freedoms and endowments — extremely hard to find, and, like a vast ground, the very basis and support for accomplishing benefit and happiness — having been obtained, by the favorable turn of merit, if I, while possessing the intelligence of critical examination, once again commit wrongdoing and am thereby led back into the hells, then it is just as if someone had been bewitched and rendered senseless by a spell: I simply have, on this occasion, something equivalent to no mind at all. I myself do not know by what cause I have been so deluded and confused; and if one examines what could be the agent of delusion within this body of mine, there is nothing but the afflictions, so it is fitting to strive at abandoning them.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

This special basis of the freedoms and endowments, extremely hard to find, is like a fine field that, once found, serves as the very ground and support for accomplishing all benefit and happiness — immediate and ultimate — for oneself and others, comparable to good soil that is the ground and support for crops and the like to grow. If I, having obtained it, by some favorable turn, through the merit and virtue previously accumulated, while now possessing the discriminating wisdom that comes from study and the like — able to know as they truly are the causes of benefit and harm such as virtue and wrongdoing — nevertheless abandon what should be done, accumulate much unwholesome karma through what should not be done, and am thereby led once again, after having failed for so long to escape and suffered so greatly, back into the fearful state of the hells and the like —

then, just as someone bewitched by a spell might be led into fire without recognizing it, so too I, at this present time, become like one whose mind of critical examination is simply absent, like a madman or a corpse. For if I had a mind at all, how could it be right to be led into ruin in this way? This illustrates that, having circled for so long through the many narrow straits of the unleisured states — the hells and so forth — since beginningless time, unable to escape and experiencing so much suffering, if I now squander this one respite of freedom, obtained through the power of merit and virtue, for nothing, and go back once more to those unbearable realms, then it is just as if, through the power of a bewitching spell, I had lost the mind that benefits myself. Reflecting thus, one is taught that one must strive on the path to liberation, relying on this excellent basis, in order to reverse taking rebirth in the cyclic existence bound by birth and death. As the Letter to a Friend says:

> Having found leisure free from the eight faults of the unleisured states, strive to reverse [further] rebirth.

And the Hero also says:

> Whoever, having obtained [this basis], cultivates the seed of the transcendent perfections, the supreme seed of the glory of awakening — surpassing even a wish-fulfilling jewel — who, among the continuum of such qualities, would let it bear no fruit here?

If one then asks, by what kind of spell was I bewitched? — I myself did not previously know the cause by which I deceived myself into delusion; there must certainly be some cause within me that deludes me in this way, and so it is fitting to examine it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-27]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: this very human body endowed with the Dharma is, by way of cause, example, and number, extremely hard to find; and having obtained it — a ground of the greatest benefit to oneself and others, serving as the support for perfectly pure ethical discipline and the like — if I, this very bodily basis obtained through some favorable turn, by good interdependence or by the blessing of the buddhas, while yet possessing the wisdom that engages with what is good and bad, still fall under the sway of others through laziness, distraction, and carelessness, and engage in the paths of unwholesome action, thereby leading myself once again into that same hellish state — this is like being bewitched by a spell: just as being bewitched by a spell cast by another leaves one without control over one's own thinking, so too, in this case or in the real purpose of this life, I am as if without any mind — so it is simply as if I had no mind.

As for "I do not even know what deludes me; what is within me?" — this means: I myself do not know by what cause this very mind has been deluded; and if one examines what is within my own mind, the afflictions are there.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Even Having Found the Freedoms and Endowments, Falling Back into Wrongdoing Is Like Having No Mind Able to Tell Benefit from Harm

All the commentaries agree in explaining that, just as a magic spell can bewilder someone else's mind, so the power of the afflictions leads even oneself, against one's own will, into wrongdoing — leaving one, in effect, without any mind for what is truly beneficial.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Cites the Omniscient Great One to Illustrate the Danger of a Mind Too Stone-Like to Recognize Its Own State

He explains that even when I mean, within this very body, to accomplish some holy Dharma, some power not my own keeps me from actually doing it — the cause of this delusion must be sought again and again.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites the *Letter to a Friend* and the Hero's Verses to Urge That the Freedoms and Endowments Be Applied to the Path of Liberation

He shows that having circled through the unleisured states since beginningless time and suffered greatly, one must not waste today's rare opportunity for liberation, but strive on the path.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Identifies the Afflictions as the Very Cause That Deludes One from Within

Examining what is within one's own mind that could delude it in this way, he finds that it is the afflictions that are present there.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Sets Out the Manner of Self-Reproach When One Falls Under the Sway of Laziness

Based on the pattern of letting one's present conduct run loose instead of storing up good karma, he shows how one comes to scorn oneself, asking what trunk-like thing of delusion could be obscuring one's own insides.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that even having obtained the rare freedoms and endowments, to be led back into the hells is just like being bewitched by a spell — so one must examine again and again what cause of delusion lies within one's own mind.

**Key points.**

- Even having obtained the excellent, hard-to-find basis of the freedoms and endowments, if one still, despite possessing the wisdom to distinguish benefit from harm, accumulates further wrongdoing and is led into the hells, this is said to be just like being bewitched by a spell.
- Just as someone bewitched by a spell can be led into fire without recognizing it, one loses control over one's own thinking and becomes, in effect, without any mind for the real purpose of this life.
- On careful examination of what within one's own body keeps one from accomplishing the holy Dharma, one finds nothing but the afflictions — so one must strive to abandon them.
- The Omniscient Great One's saying that explanation and teaching alike fail to bring understanding to a mind like stone or iron, and the *Letter to a Friend*'s counsel to strive to reverse rebirth once the eight faults of the unleisured states are left behind, both point to the same task.
- Falling under the sway of laziness leads one to scorn oneself, asking what trunk-like thing of delusion could be obscuring one's own insides — a reflection one must return to again and again.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He asks: who is it that keeps our own mind from accomplishing virtue with ease? The afflictions are doing it. Naming outward anger toward others, craving-attachment, and — summarized under "and so forth" — pride and doubt as "enemies" is a point of great importance. Most of us identify our enemy as something outside ourselves, while we are apt to see our own inner attachment and aversion almost as good qualities of the mind. Even among those who profess a religion, it is said there are formless gods and spirits that cause harm, and, identifying these as if they existed externally, people make offerings to the deities and nāgas who are said to protect against them, and worship the sun, moon, and fire. Within the Buddhist teaching itself, though such harm-doers exist, dealing with them only clears away the immediate trouble — it is not a permanent solution. Whoever the human or non-human harm-doer may be, since, like ourselves, they all want happiness and do not want suffering, there is no one among them who is only to be discarded rather than an object worthy of some regard; and being kind to such ordinary "enemies" can even bring benefit in return — so they are not real enemies in the true sense. Even the body produces various illnesses, so the body too becomes our enemy in that sense, and when the mind's own unhappiness makes us unhappy, that too could be called an enemy by that logic — yet since these do not harm us all the time, we do not, as a rule, say "I myself am my own enemy." Likewise, an external enemy causes harm only occasionally, never permanently — whereas the afflicted enemy causes permanent harm. Is that not, then, the real enemy? What truly and constantly does harm at the beginning, middle, and end is the real enemy. It is held that only by conquering the afflicted enemy of one's own mindstream does one attain liberation. This, then, is why it says here "enemies such as hatred and craving."

Under the power of the afflictions, on one side doubt arises, and on one's own account — for example, when someone claims there is no past or future life, no karmic cause and effect — if one traces such claims to their final proof, there is nothing beyond "it seems to me there isn't." Pushed to the very end, it comes down to pride: "I know roughly what's what; I don't see it, so it must not exist" — there is no proof beyond that bare assertion. And the root of this, in turn, is the deep-seated attitude in one's own mind, "I am the most important," from which follows "it exists if I accept it, and does not exist if I do not" — this way of thinking has pride as its very foundation. When such disruptive claims come along nowadays, at first one may say something like "yes, that must be so"; but when pressed further — "well, what is it actually like, then?" — in the end one simply says "I suppose it isn't so." Like an arrow shot at random with no target and no basis, coming to a final conclusion with nothing to back it up — this is exactly what happens: when pride arrives in our minds, either we see things as non-existent, or we are led into a state of doubt.

Furthermore, as for how attachment and aversion are themselves so painfully difficult to deal with directly: for example, when anger arises, it has almost the appearance of protecting oneself; when someone else does one an injury, there arises something like an immediate readiness to retaliate — is that not exactly how anger operates? Attachment has the appearance of being one's own trustworthy friend, a mental habit of cherishing and being kind to oneself. So although attachment and aversion each present the appearance of protecting one or caring for one with kindness, in the end they lead to harm for oneself. The reason anger is the worst enemy for someone practicing the bodhisattva's trainings is that anger directly leads to harming others, and so runs contrary to the training that a bodhisattva must strive hard to prevent. Attachment does not directly harm others, so, relative to that, anger is taught to be the greater object to be abandoned. Even so, in the end attachment lays the ground from which anger is drawn out — attachment is bad in the way that an oil stain [on paper] is bad [spreading invisibly].

Anger, pride, attachment, doubt, envy, and competitiveness — among these extremely destructive states, the term "affliction" is explained in the sense of suffering or unhappiness, and the reason such states of mind are named "afflictions" is that the moment such a thought arises, it makes the person or being in whom it arises unhappy, or disturbs their previous peace and ease. Whatever state of mind arises in someone's mindstream immediately disturbs whatever calm and settledness was there before. For example, an ordinarily very gentle, unassuming person, once overcome by anger, will say anything at all without hesitation, and show any expression at all without hesitation. Likewise, even in an ordinarily harmonious and affectionate family, once anger arises, there is a real danger that they might strike or even kill one another. What is responsible for that? Clearly, it is the affliction of anger that has done it.

Furthermore, if someone who ordinarily practices the four qualities of a renunciate falls under the power of intense anger, they may wrap their robe around their waist and become as fierce as a tiger's fangs toward other beings; and, in general, once under the power of affliction, one may even end up taking one's own life. Anger and what is called "affliction" are nothing but faults from every angle. Though there are contexts specific to advanced levels of practice, here it is said that one must not give the afflictions any opening — that is, no chance at all should be given to them. Why? Because our real enemy is not some foreign invader; the real enemy is the affliction within their own mindstream — anger, craving-attachment (and, under "and so forth," doubt, envy, and competitiveness).

Such enemies as anger and craving are not something that exists in bodily form, wielding limbs and weapons to dominate a person; nor are they brave and skilled — yet somehow, overwhelmed by their own weight, one ends up living as the afflictions' slave.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

*(Kunpal's explanation for 4-28 opens a new outer topic, "the third: reflecting on the afflictions to be abandoned, cultivating heedfulness," and its first sub-heading; the outline heading itself is trimmed below, keeping only the explanation proper.)*

Having examined in this way, if one asks what deludes me from within, it is the parents of cyclic existence — hatred and craving-attachment — and, within, the afflicted enemies such as delusion, that are present. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Afflicted Enemies Have No Body, Yet by an Unaccountable Power They Make Me Their Slave

All the commentaries agree in showing that hatred, craving, and the rest possess neither limbs nor weapons, nor the qualities of bravery or cleverness, yet since beginningless time have kept me, without any freedom, obeying their every command like a servant.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Sets Out the Defining Characteristics of the Ten Afflictions in Detail, Following the Abhidharma

Drawing on the *Abhidharmasamuccaya* and the *Pramāṇavārttika* to explain the defining characteristics, divisions, causes, and functions of the six (or ten) root afflictions, he counsels that abandoning them by relying on their antidotes is of great importance.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Explains That the Afflictions, Though Not an Embodied Self, Subjugate Me as a Slave Without Any Cause for Harm

Since the afflictions have no bodily form such as limbs, there is no genuine reason for them to cause harm — yet subjugating oneself as a servant to them is utterly improper.

###### 4. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Counsels That Recognizing Why Hatred, Craving, and the Rest Are Named "Enemies" Is a Point of Great Importance

He explains that whereas an external enemy causes harm only occasionally, the afflicted enemy in one's own mindstream causes harm constantly, at the beginning, middle, and end — making it the true enemy.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Shows How Attachment and Aversion Appear to Protect or Care for Oneself, Yet Lead to Harm in the End

Although anger seems, in the moment, almost like self-protection, and attachment like a trustworthy friend, both are shown, on examination, to be like an oil stain that in the end draws one toward harm.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse examines how the afflicted enemies — hatred, craving, and the rest — though possessing no limbs or weapons of the body and no quality of bravery or cleverness, nevertheless subjugate me without any freedom, making me their slave and yoking me to suffering — and teaches that the afflictions must therefore be abandoned.

**Key points.**

- Unlike other external enemies, the afflicted enemies — hatred, craving, and the rest — possess no bodily form such as a head or limbs, and are neither brave nor clever.
- It is utterly improper that the afflictions should, since beginningless time, subjugate me without the slightest freedom, making me their slave and burdening me with manifold suffering.
- An external enemy causes harm only occasionally, never constantly; but the afflicted enemy in one's own mindstream causes harm at the beginning, middle, and end — and although attachment and aversion may seem to protect or care for oneself, they are, like an oil stain, ruinous in the end.
- Following the Abhidharma treatises, the root of existence — attachment, aversion, pride, ignorance, view, and doubt (the six or ten afflictions) — is set out in detail, with the teaching that it must be abandoned.
- Malice arises from nine causes, occasioned by unwanted circumstances toward oneself, one's friends, and one's enemies across the three times; since it subjugates one without freedom and causes suffering, it is fitting to abandon these afflictions.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: I have fallen under the power of the afflictions and come to dwell as if beneath their tyranny, oppressed by them. From now on there is simply no room whatsoever to boast or preen oneself as an antidote that regards the afflictions as faults and sees their danger. The afflictions alone have won out, and I have come to a state in which they may harm me with delight as they please. If one asks what benefit or harm ultimately results from this: as just explained, when anger, attachment, pride, and the like arise, they immediately produce bad behavior. For example, take a household: first a strong sense of "I" arises in the mind, and, taking that as the foundation, one divides things into "mine" — "my relative, my acquaintance, my friend" — and, having divided things into one's own side, one then says "my enemy," or "my friend's enemy" — meaning someone who is not one's own enemy but is said to be one's friend's enemy — dividing everyone into the camp of relatives and the camp of enemies. Then, toward those on the unwelcome side, even when there is nothing else to call them, some bad name is given anyway; occasioned by envy and competitiveness, one calls them "beggar" or "cheat" and the like — such bad words are spoken. Those bad words are, as it were, carried on the wind of gossip, and when they reach the other party's ears, that person too puts on a show of pride; he replies with something even more cutting. At some point, when the two happen to meet, their very way of looking at each other becomes somehow different. Short of actually drawing knives at each other's waists — and if knives are indeed drawn, then it is simply a matter of grabbing the blade by the hilt — one puts on the air of having a brave heart, cries out "ki-ho-ho!" and, whenever the moment allows, does nothing but try to kill. What is responsible for that? The root condition is the affliction that has done it. For example: a household is at peace and ease, everyone in high spirits, all the family happy and content, wearing new clothes, clean and well cared for; everyone comes together in one room, laughing "ha-ha" in a mood of joy, playing such games, drinking chang and sitting together. Then, at some point, if one wrong word spoken well or badly gives rise to a thought of attachment or aversion, that bad state of mind, as its condition, instantly dissolves the joyful, happy, smiling expressions of a moment before. The pleasant conversation and the singing of a moment ago likewise vanish. The moment one bad word — "you" and "I" — is used, along with it a bodily expression appears on one face that looks unpleasant to the other; and even without actually coming to blows or bloodshed, the peace within that household is destroyed root and branch. Whereas a moment ago a familiar friend was sitting close by, and one thought perhaps to leave, seeing the friends looking so happy — that same trusting mind, on hearing afterward a cutting word like "you" and "I," may well end up feeling that a quarrel has broken out today between the two, of the kind one must avoid even if one had nothing to do with it. All of that chain of consequence was made by nothing but the untamed mind called attachment and aversion. It is the same one person, the very one who was there that same morning; no harm was inflicted on him by the Three Jewels, nor was he harmed by spirits or demons. When one's mind is thoroughly at peace, peace and ease come; and at some point, when unpeaceful attachment or aversion arrives as a condition, disturbance results from it. It is with respect to this that it is said: "not to be angry at them, but to endure — this is inappropriate patience, a cause for scorn" — treating such afflictions as if they were one's own close friend, not seeing their fault when attachment or aversion arises, and accommodating them — this is exceedingly ill-advised. Even an ordinarily gentle person, on a day when anger arises, becomes somewhat different and even changes their facial expression; in the end it is oneself who is ruined. Kyabje Ling Rinpoche used to tell a story: it seems there was, in the presence of the previous incarnation, a man in Lhasa skilled at serving tea for the Chinese community, who had a fiery temperament. One day, shortly before an attendant of Yongzin Rinpoche arrived at his place on some errand, he had flown into a rage and smashed an old teacup he owned. When, at some point, his anger had calmed, he picked up the shards of the broken cup in his hand and sat there sighing at length, it is said. In any case, when anger arose, the perception that the cup was something precious had simply vanished; only afterward, when he came to his senses, did he realize how excessive it had been. In the moment anger arises, some kind of mental capacity remains present, but it is nothing more than the capacity of a fool or an idiot. If, examining the actual facts afterward and seeing the reasons, one gives a reply: if another has genuinely wronged or been unfair to oneself, and, speaking of this in terms of the Dharma, one lets loose an account of that unfairness driven purely by arrogance and pride, that too brings harm to oneself; and yet, if one does not reply at all, out of an absence of any wish to harm, there is a fault in the bodhisattva's conduct — whereas if one does reply, there is a virtue in doing so; so one must reply. But this should be done relying only on the actual need for such a reason, never while under the sway of anger — replying instead from a relaxed and settled state of mind gives the best kind of reply one can give.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that to endure, without becoming angry, the afflicted enemy that dwells in one's own mind and does one harm with delight, at every moment and without hesitation, is patience applied to what is not a proper object for patience — and is therefore a matter for the utmost scorn by the heirs of the Victorious Ones and by the noble ones. All the commentaries agree on this basic ground.

**Key points.**

- To endure, without becoming angry, an affliction that dwells in one's own mind and does one harm is patience applied to what is not a proper object for patience, and is a cause for scorn by the heirs of the Victorious Ones.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam, through the Kadam mind-training image of a thief residing right at one's own head yet still preying on the eye, illustrates how the afflictions do their harm from within, dwelling constantly with the mind.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama, through the image of a family celebration, shows how afflictions destroy happiness in an instant.
- Through the story of Kyabje Ling Rinpoche's tea server, His Holiness the Dalai Lama illustrates how, when anger arises, the mind that recognizes things becomes like that of a fool, harming oneself.
- One must understand the afflictions' defining characteristics, divisions, causes, and functions as set out in the Abhidharma treatises, and strive above all to cultivate an awareness of their dangers.
