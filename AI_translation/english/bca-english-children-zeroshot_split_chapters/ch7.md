## 7. Chapter Seven: Teaching Joyful Effort ^7-0


Having built patience like this, I should now build joyful effort,
because true awakening depends on this kind of effort.
Just as nothing moves without wind,
no goodness happens without effort. ^7-1


Joyful effort means finding real delight in doing good.
Its opposite should also be explained:
laziness, clinging to unhelpful habits,
feeling discouraged, and putting yourself down. ^7-2


Enjoying the taste of doing nothing,
craving sleep and comfort,
and never feeling tired of the sufferings of endless rebirth —
these are exactly what cause laziness to grow. ^7-3


Caught in the net cast by disturbing emotions,
already trapped once again in the snare of rebirth,
and now walking right into the mouth of death —
how is it that I still do not understand this? ^7-4


Do you not see how death is already
killing off, one by one, all those around you?
And yet you still rely on sleep,
just like a lazy water buffalo. ^7-5


With every road for escape already blocked,
while the Lord of Death watches you closely,
how can you possibly still enjoy your food,
and how can you possibly still enjoy your sleep? ^7-6


Since death will come quickly no matter what,
I should gather as much goodness as I can while I still have time.
Giving up laziness only once death has already arrived
would come far, far too late to do any good. ^7-7


For something not yet begun,
or something left only half finished —
when death suddenly arrives,
I will think, "Oh no, it's over," with real regret. ^7-8


Swollen with grief, eyes red,
tears streaming down their face,
close friends losing all hope for me —
I will see faces that look just like the face of death's own messenger. ^7-9


Tormented by the memory of my own wrongdoing,
and hearing the sounds of hell in the distance,
terrified, my body soiled and trembling out of fear —
what will I be able to do in a moment like that? ^7-10


If you are already this frightened, like a fish
flopping helplessly on dry land, even now, in this very life,
then what need is there to even describe
the unbearable suffering of the hell realms that wrongdoing leads to? ^7-11


Even the touch of warm water
can feel unbearably painful on tender, delicate skin.
So having created the causes for a hell
of unbearable heat, how can I still sit here so comfortably? ^7-12


Wanting good results without any effort,
being overly sensitive to hardship,
being caught in the grip of death though seeming as fine as a god right now —
oh no, such a person will be utterly overwhelmed by suffering. ^7-13


Relying on this human life, like a sturdy boat,
I can cross the great river of suffering.
Since a boat like this is so hard to find again later,
I should not, like a fool, fall asleep now, at this crucial time. ^7-14


Turning away from the true teachings, the greatest source of joy,
a joy with no limits at all,
why would I instead find delight
in distractions and mere entertainment, which only lead to suffering? ^7-15


To sustain my effort, I need:
freedom from discouragement, a strong support,
real enthusiasm, letting go of what is not helpful,
treating myself and others as equally worth caring for, and exchanging myself for others. ^7-16


I should never let myself feel discouraged,
thinking, "How could someone like me ever reach awakening?"
Because the Tathāgata himself,
who always speaks the truth, has taught this truth: ^7-17


that even small creatures like flies, mosquitoes,
bees, and various insects,
if they generate the power of real effort,
can attain even the highest, most difficult awakening. ^7-18


So if I, born human by good fortune,
already able to tell what helps from what harms,
do not give up the practice of a bodhisattva,
why should I not be able to reach awakening too? ^7-19


Someone might say, "But I am afraid,
because I might have to give up my hands, feet, and other such things."
That fear only comes from confusion,
from not properly weighing what is truly serious and what is not. ^7-20


For countless hundreds of millions of ages,
I have already been cut, pierced, burned, and killed,
over and over again, many times,
and yet none of that ever brought me any closer to awakening. ^7-21


But the difficulty involved in achieving awakening
actually has a real limit and a real purpose —
it is like the pain of having a wound properly treated,
in order to remove a deeper sickness and clear away real harm. ^7-22


Every doctor uses some form of treatment,
even an uncomfortable one, to cure a painful illness.
In the same way, to defeat the many kinds of suffering,
I should be willing to bear a little discomfort now. ^7-23


But even that ordinary kind of treatment
is not what the supreme doctor, the Buddha, actually uses.
Instead, through methods that are wonderfully gentle,
he heals even the greatest, most serious sicknesses. ^7-24


At first, the guide gently starts people off
with simple acts of generosity, like giving away vegetables.
Once they grow accustomed to that,
they gradually learn, step by step, to give away even their own flesh. ^7-25


Once someone's mind has grown so used to their own body
that it feels no more precious than a simple vegetable,
what real difficulty is there left
in giving away flesh and other such things? ^7-26


Since wrongdoing is what truly needs to be avoided, giving it up brings no real suffering at all.
And since wisdom brings clarity, it never brings true unhappiness.
It is really wrong understanding
and wrongdoing itself that harm the mind and body. ^7-27


Since goodness brings comfort to the body,
and wisdom brings comfort to the mind,
why would someone filled with compassion, who works for others' sake
while still living within the cycle of rebirth, ever feel discouraged? ^7-28


Through the power of bodhicitta,
past wrongdoing is used up and cleared away,
and an ocean of goodness is gathered instead —
this is why bodhisattvas are said to be even greater than those who seek liberation only for themselves. ^7-29


So, mounted on the horse of bodhicitta,
which clears away all weariness and discouragement,
traveling smoothly from happiness to happiness,
what wise person would ever feel discouraged on such a journey? ^7-30


To accomplish the aims of all beings, the forces needed are:
sincere aspiration, steadiness, joy, and the readiness to set things aside when needed.
Aspiration is built up by thinking carefully
about the suffering of endless rebirth and its true dangers, and about the benefits of practice. ^7-31


Having given up these obstacles in this way,
I should build sincere aspiration, steady confidence, real joy, and the readiness to let things go when needed,
and I should work hard to increase my joyful effort
through enthusiasm and genuine mastery over my own mind. ^7-32


I should work to overcome countless faults
in myself and in others.
Overcoming even just one single fault
could take an ocean of ages of effort. ^7-33


Yet in myself, I do not even see the beginning
of overcoming even a fraction of my own faults.
So why does my heart not break,
knowing I face such an immeasurable source of suffering? ^7-34


I should also work to develop
countless good qualities in myself and in others.
Yet developing even one single good quality
could take an ocean of ages of practice. ^7-35


Even so, I have never actually managed
to fully develop even a portion of any of these good qualities.
It would be a shame to waste
this precious human life I have found, doing nothing worthwhile with it. ^7-36


I have not made offerings to the Buddha,
I have not given the joy of a great celebration,
I have not done anything to help spread the teachings,
and I have not fulfilled the wishes of those in need. ^7-37


I have not given fearlessness to the frightened,
and I have not given comfort to the suffering.
All I have really done
is cause suffering, like the pain felt in a mother's womb before birth. ^7-38


In the past, and even now,
because I have lacked real aspiration for the true teachings,
I have ended up in this kind of poverty and hardship.
So who would ever want to give up their aspiration for the teachings, once they understand this? ^7-39


The Buddha taught that aspiration
is the root of every kind of goodness.
And the root of aspiration itself
comes from constantly meditating on the results of our actions. ^7-40


Suffering, unhappiness,
every kind of fear,
and losing what we want —
all of these come about because of doing wrong. ^7-41


But someone who has done good deeds with a sincere heart
will be honored with wonderful results
wherever they go,
because of that very goodness. ^7-42


Someone who does wrong, hoping for happiness,
will instead, wherever they go,
be struck down by the weapon of suffering,
because of that very wrongdoing. ^7-43


Resting in the heart of a great, sweetly-scented lotus,
nourished by the pleasant fragrance of the Buddha's own teachings,
that supreme and beautiful body, born from a lotus opened by the Buddha's radiant light,
becomes, through goodness, a child of the Sugatas, dwelling right before the Buddhas themselves. ^7-44


But someone struck down by Death's messengers, all their skin peeled away completely,
utterly overwhelmed with suffering, poured over with molten copper burning fiercely hot,
pierced again and again by blazing swords and spears, torn into a hundred pieces of flesh,
falling onto a burning iron ground — this is what many kinds of wrongdoing lead to instead. ^7-45


So I should feel real aspiration toward goodness,
and train myself with genuine respect and dedication.
Following the ritual of the "Vajra Banner,"
I should also build a healthy sense of confidence. ^7-46


Before beginning any task, I should first check carefully
whether I actually have the ability to complete it, and then either begin it or not.
It would be better not to begin at all
than to start something and then turn back partway through. ^7-47


If I get in the habit of quitting,
that same habit carries over into future lives too,
and wrongdoing and suffering only increase because of it.
Even future tasks, and their results, end up weaker and smaller because of this habit. ^7-48


I should feel healthy confidence regarding three things:
my actions, my ability to resist disturbing emotions, and my own capability.
Thinking, "I myself will accomplish this" —
that is confidence regarding one's actions. ^7-49


This world, overpowered by disturbing emotions,
is unable to truly accomplish its own real benefit.
So, since other beings cannot do what I, understanding this, can do,
I myself should take on this task. ^7-50


If other people are willing to do even lowly tasks,
why should I hold myself back?
Of course, I should never do this out of arrogance —
it is best to have no arrogance in me at all. ^7-51


When crows come across a dead snake,
even they can act as bold as eagles.
In the same way, if I let myself become weak,
even a small setback can end up harming me badly. ^7-52


If someone gives up trying, feeling discouraged and weak,
how could they ever truly escape their difficulties?
But someone who builds real, healthy confidence and effort
becomes hard to defeat, even by great obstacles. ^7-53


So, with a firm and steady mind,
I should work to overcome any faults that arise.
If I instead let myself be defeated by my own faults,
my wish to overcome all difficulty everywhere would become laughable. ^7-54


I should aim to overcome every difficulty,
and never let anything defeat me.
As a child of the Buddhas, the Lion among beings,
I should hold onto this kind of healthy confidence. ^7-55


Beings overcome by pride
are not the same as those with this healthy confidence.
Those who are proud fall under the power of their enemies,
but those with real confidence overcome even their enemies. ^7-56


Someone puffed up with the pride of disturbing emotions
is led by that very pride toward difficult rebirths,
losing out on true human happiness,
becoming, in a sense, a servant living off others' leftovers, ^7-57


dull, unpleasant, and weak,
and looked down upon by everyone everywhere.
Even someone who has worked hard at difficult spiritual practices,
if they are still puffed up with pride,
falls into that same category of weakness — there is little more to say about it. ^7-58


But someone who builds healthy confidence in order to overcome the enemy of pride
truly deserves to be called confident, a real hero of victory.
Someone who thoroughly defeats the arising of pride, the true enemy,
brings about the full result of victory that all beings wish for. ^7-59


When surrounded by disturbing emotions on every side,
I should stand firm, undaunted, in a thousand different ways.
Like a lion among jackals,
I should never be overcome by any crowd of disturbing emotions. ^7-60


Just as someone in serious danger
instinctively protects their own eyes above all else,
in the same way, even in the midst of real danger,
I should never let myself fall under the power of disturbing emotions. ^7-61


Just as someone enjoys a favorite game and wants to keep playing,
whatever task needs doing,
I should become genuinely attached to doing it,
finding joy in it, never feeling satisfied or wanting to stop. ^7-62


People work hard for the sake of happiness,
yet there is no guarantee that happiness will actually follow.
But how could there be anything but happiness
in doing the very work that itself brings happiness? ^7-63


If people are never satisfied with ordinary desires,
which are as risky as licking honey off a razor's edge,
then why would I ever grow tired
of goodness, which brings true, lasting happiness as its result? ^7-64


So, in order to bring a task to completion,
I should throw myself into it fully,
just as a great elephant, scorched by the midday heat,
plunges eagerly into a cool lake. ^7-65


But if my strength begins to fail,
I should set the task aside for a while, planning to return to it later.
And once something is properly completed,
I should let it go and move on, ready for what comes next. ^7-66


Just as a skilled warrior in the thick of battle
deflects the enemy's blade,
I should skillfully avoid the weapons of disturbing emotions,
and stay ready to strike down these true enemies. ^7-67


If a sword falls to the ground during battle,
a warrior quickly and fearfully picks it back up.
In the same way, if I ever lose hold of the sword of mindfulness,
I should quickly remember the dangers of a bad rebirth, and pick it right back up. ^7-68


Just as poison, once it enters the blood,
spreads quickly throughout the whole body,
in the same way, if I ever let my guard down even slightly,
wrongdoing can quickly spread throughout my whole mind. ^7-69


Just as someone carrying a bowl brimful of oil
stays extremely careful and focused
when a sword-wielding guard walks beside them, threatening death if even a single drop spills,
I should keep that same intense, careful focus in my own practice. ^7-70


So, just as I would leap up quickly
if a snake suddenly appeared in my lap,
in the same way, whenever sleepiness or laziness arise,
I should quickly and firmly push them away. ^7-71


Whenever I make a mistake,
I should gently but firmly acknowledge it to myself,
and think for a long while, with real determination,
"I will make sure this does not happen again." ^7-72


To make sure this kind of mindfulness
becomes a natural habit in every situation,
I should seek out the right company
and engage in whatever activities help build this habit. ^7-73


To make sure I have real strength and readiness before starting any task,
I should first remember the teachings on being careful and mindful,
and use them to gently rouse and prepare myself
for the work ahead. ^7-74


Just as cotton fluff is carried along
wherever the wind blows,
in the same way, I should let myself be carried along
by genuine enthusiasm — this is truly how things get accomplished. ^7-75


This is the end of the seventh chapter, called "Teaching Joyful Effort," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^7-a
