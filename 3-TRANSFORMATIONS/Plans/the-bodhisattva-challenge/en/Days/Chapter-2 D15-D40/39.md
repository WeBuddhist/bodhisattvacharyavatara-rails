# Day 39 — Not bad, but not seeing

> **Notification**
> **Title:** Meet one past mistake with understanding, not a beating
> **Detail:** Today's verse says our wrongs come from not seeing clearly. Confession is clear-seeing, not self-punishment.

## Opening

Notice how Shantideva describes the source of his misdeeds: through my lack of understanding and ignorance. Not through wickedness. The verse traces our wrong turns back to a kind of blindness, not seeing clearly what leads where. That single word changes the whole feel of confession. It is not a courtroom where we are the guilty party. It is more like finally turning a light on.

## From the Tradition

Sazang Mati Panchen, a fourteenth-century teacher, describes the confessor as an ordinary person, ignorant of what to take up and what to leave, acting under the sway of confusion. That is the diagnosis: the trouble is not a rotten character but unclear sight. We rarely set out to cause harm; we misread the situation, we do not see where an action leads, and only later does the damage become obvious. This matters for how confession actually works. If your wrongs came from being bad, the only response is to feel bad, which changes nothing. But if they came from not seeing, then the cure is to see, and seeing is something you can do. So the honest question about a past mistake is not "how could I be so awful" but "what did I not understand at the time." That question softens the shame and, at the same time, is the very thing that keeps you from repeating it.

## Today's Practice

Bring to mind one thing you did that you regret. Instead of running the usual harsh commentary about it, ask a gentler and more useful question: what did I not see clearly at the time? Let the answer come without scolding. Understanding what you missed does more to keep it from happening again than any amount of beating yourself up ever will.
