## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three classical commentators agree that Verse 1-22 employs an *a fortiori* logical argument—specifically the "argument from the stronger" (*kaimutyanyāya* / *shos ci dgos*)—to contrast the limited merit of the headache analogy in Verse 1-21 with the infinite, boundless merit of generating the mind of enlightenment (Bodhicitta). The commentators systematically unpack this comparison by contrasting the objects, the types of suffering to be removed, and the positive qualities to be established.

### 1. The Logical Progression (The *A Fortiori* Argument)
*   **Consensus:** All three commentators emphasize the transition from a minor, localized act of benevolence to the universal, ultimate altruism of Bodhicitta. If wanting to relieve a simple headache for a few beings is so highly meritorious, then wanting to completely free all beings from all suffering and establish them in supreme qualities must yield limitless, immeasurable merit.
*   **Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen** explicitly contrasts the "small focus and aspect" (*dmigs rnam chung ngu*) of curing a headache with the infinite scope of Bodhicitta, framing it with the classic rhetorical question: *"If so, what need is there to mention the infinite merit..."*
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Ngulchu Thokme** mirror this structure, using the comparison to demonstrate that the merit of Bodhicitta is beyond any standard of measure, signs, or numbers, and is truly equal to space.

### 2. The Vastness of the Object (*ālambana* / *dmigs yul*)
*   **From Finite to Infinite:** The commentators point out that while the headache analogy focuses on a very limited number of beings, the mind of enlightenment focuses on *every single sentient being* without exception (*ekaikasya* / *sems can re re*).
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** describes the objects of Bodhicitta as *"every single countless sentient being equal to space,"* emphasizing the absolute universality of the Bodhisattva's vow. 
*   **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly focuses on the scope of *"all sentient beings"* as the ultimate object of the Bodhisattva's benevolent intention.

### 3. The Dual Aspect of the Aspect (*ākāra* / *rnam pa*)
The commentators align closely with the Sanskrit text's grammatical structure, which highlights two distinct drives of the Bodhisattva's mind: wishing to remove suffering (*jihīrṣataḥ* / *bsal 'dod*) and wishing to bring about supreme qualities (*cikīrṣataḥ* / *yonten bsgrub par 'dod pa*).
*   **Elimination of Suffering (Compassion):**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** defines this as wanting to *"dispel the boundless unhappiness"* of all beings.
    *   **Sazang** explains it as wishing to *"clear the boundless sufferings and unhappiness"* of every single being.
    *   **Thokme** focuses on wishing to *"clear the immeasurable unhappiness"* of each of them.
*   **Endowment of Qualities (Loving-kindness):**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** takes a strictly ultimate perspective, defining this as wanting to *"establish every single sentient being in the boundless qualities of a Buddha."*
    *   **Sazang** provides a more comprehensive scholastic categorization, stating that the Bodhisattva wishes to establish each being in *"immeasurable temporary and ultimate qualities"* (representing high rebirth and definitive goodness, or *abhyudaya* and *niḥśreyasa*).
    *   **Thokme** simply defines it as wishing to *"accomplish immeasurable qualities"* for each of them.
