## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 64 and 65 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Through not knowing, we have built up many mistakes and much wrongdoing. But we can feel regret for them. We can confess them in front of the buddhas and bodhisattvas, and promise not to make such mistakes again. Then every fault becomes clean. So from today we should take up the practice of confessing wrongdoing, and of holding back from it afterwards, with real courage.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give scripture and a comparison that are valuable, easy to understand, and encouraging.

Master Nagarjuna says in the *Letter to a Friend* that someone who was careless before but becomes careful later grows beautiful, like the moon once the clouds have gone. So if regret arises for the mistakes we have made, and we promise not to repeat them, every fault can be made clean.

Likewise, Khenpo Kunzang Palden says that we confess our wrongdoing to the buddhas and their heirs. They see it with the eye of wisdom. They hear it with ears that reach everywhere. They know it with minds that know everything. The confession is made in their actual presence.

Above all, he says, promising not to do wrong even at the cost of our own life is, among the four powers, the power of restraint. The four powers are regret, reliance, applying the antidotes, and restraint.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will admit my mistakes, and make a promise not to repeat them in future.

**Explanation:** *(Avoiding wrongdoing)* We feel honest regret for the wrongdoing and the mistakes we have made. Then we promise never to make that kind of mistake again. That is the power of restraint. If the promise is a firm one, the wrongdoing we did earlier is made clean, and bad actions will not stain us in future. That is why it matters so much.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

In the actual presence of the protectors, the Tathāgatas together with their heirs, I make whatever mass of wrongdoing and faults I have piled up until now rise clearly before my mind as the object of recollection. With my body I make the gesture of joining my palms; with an attitude of respect, and with my mind thinking, "What shall I do if the fruit of such faults — a suffering hard to bear — comes upon me?" — with a mind afraid of that suffering, I bow again and again, and in that way I confess it all. <!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

"Of the protectors" — that is, of the protectors, the victorious ones together with their heirs, who possess great compassion. In their actual presence: with the body, the hair standing on end, the eyes brimming with tears, the hands with palms joined; with the speech, words of lament and the words of confession; with the mind, afraid and terrified of the suffering of the moment of death, of the intermediate state, and of the lower realms in the life to come — with a mind of fierce shame and regret I bow again and again in reverence, and I acknowledge and confess every one of those faults and downfalls, one by one, hiding nothing and concealing nothing. "Again and again" is the utterance of a learned person of genuine experience: it is meant to point to a great regret. And although in this way the victorious ones and their heirs are hidden from us, we are not hidden from them — it is like being blind oneself while standing before someone who can see. So if we enumerate our faults and confess them, then, even from measurelessly far away, they see them with the eye of wisdom, hear them with the ear of miraculous power, and know them with the mind that knows; and therefore, he says, this too is "in their actual presence."

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Setting the protectors — those endowed with great compassion, the victorious ones together with their heirs — vividly before me in my mind, I bow down with my body and join my palms; and from the depths of my heart, with a mind afraid of the suffering that will be experienced as the fruit of wrongdoing, and with fiercely intense regret, I bow again and again. Acknowledging, "I, a fool, have done each and every one of those wrongs and downfalls," I confess them one by one. In this way the actual manner of confessing is shown: a confession made with a mind of regret expressed through the gestures of body and speech. Trusting that I myself am standing right there in their presence, I join the palms of my two hands; and with a mind utterly afraid, terrified, of the fruit of wrongdoing — the suffering of samsara and of the lower realms — I bow again and again to the buddhas and the rest, to the objects of homage, the Three Jewels. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Prostration of Confession Is Made in the Actual Presence of the Protectors

All the commentaries agree in the end that one imagines the victorious ones together with their heirs, who possess great compassion, vividly present before one in one's mind, and then bows and confesses with all three — body, speech and mind.

###### 2. The Meaning of "in Their Actual Presence" — That the Buddhas, by Their Knowing and Seeing, Really Do See, Hear and Know

Kunzang Pelden explains that even if one enumerates and confesses one's faults from measurelessly far away, the buddhas see them with the eye of wisdom, hear them with the ear of miraculous power and know them with the mind that knows, and that it is therefore "in their actual presence."

###### 3. Bowing Again and Again Is the Mark of Great Regret

Kunzang Pelden explains that "again and again" is the utterance of a learned person of genuine experience, and that it is meant to point to a great regret.

###### 4. Faults Must Be Acknowledged and Confessed One by One, Hiding Nothing and Concealing Nothing

Kunzang Pelden explains that every fault and downfall must be acknowledged and confessed one by one, with nothing hidden and nothing concealed; and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk says the same thing — "all those faults I confess one by one, each on its own."

###### 5. By Doing Virtuous Action as the Antidote to Wrongdoing One Is Freed From One's Faults

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the *Vinayaviniścaya*, which says that, just as the sun and moon emerge from the clouds, so too a wrongful action is blocked by virtue.

###### 6. This Verse Is Explained Together With Verse 63 as Part of a Single Act of Confession

Ngulchu Thogme, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and His Holiness the Dalai Lama all take verse 63 and this verse together and explain them as one single way of confessing.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows how one confesses: imagining the protectors, the victorious ones together with their heirs, vividly present before one in one's mind, one joins one's palms with the body, speaks words of lament and of confession with the speech, and with the mind, afraid of the suffering to come, bows again and again — and so confesses every fault and downfall, one by one, hiding nothing. Many of the commentaries explain verse 63 and this verse together as parts of a single act of confession.

**Key points.**

- Confession is an act performed in the actual presence of the protectors, with all three — body, speech and mind.
- "In their actual presence" is explained as holding good because the buddhas see with the eye of wisdom and know with the mind that knows.
- Kunzang Pelden explains that bowing again and again is the mark of great regret, and that every fault and downfall must be acknowledged and confessed one by one, hiding nothing; Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk agrees that each is to be confessed on its own.
- That doing virtuous action as the antidote to wrongdoing frees one from one's faults is shown by the image, from the *Vinayaviniścaya*, of the sun and moon emerging from the clouds.
- Many commentators take verse 63 and this verse together as parts of one single stanza of confession.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

So then: in the presence of the Tathāgatas together with their heirs, with fiercely intense regret for my earlier wrongdoing, I bring it out straight, concealing nothing, and confess it directly; and from now on I am to keep to a mind of restraint that will not do those things even if my life is at stake. "You holy guides, please bear with all that I have piled up until now. Please bless me, that my wrongdoing may be purified. In the future I will never in any way do those things again — those causes that give rise to such unhappy fruit." That is to say, it is a promise: "I will never do it again."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-65]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

"Guides, victorious ones together with your heirs: my having done wrong is a lapse and carries a fault — please accept that it is so." It is a request, a petition; and he says that, just as the one before whom the confession is made asks about one's faults, so one acknowledges one's own faults oneself and petitions them.

Fourth, the power of restoration. "This wrongdoing, this non-virtue, this bad conduct, leads from the light into the path of darkness, produces a ripening that is utterly unbearable, and is condemned by holy beings; it is not good conduct. So from this very day onward, from now on, even if my life is at stake I will never do it again — I hold back from it." As one says this, one imagines that light-rays of many kinds come from the bodies of those before whom the confession is made, the victorious ones together with their heirs, and that all the faults and downfalls, all the wrongdoing and obscuration, of oneself and of every other being are cleared away and purified in an instant, as when the sun rises into a dark room.

Furthermore, there are two kinds of person who raise the victory banner of the Dharma and bring down the victory banner of Māra: the one in whom no fault arises at all, and the one who, when a fault has arisen, purifies it by confession. That it can be cleansed if it is confessed is the one good quality of wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-65]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

For that reason: "Guides, since my having done wrong is a lapse, please accept that it is a lapse. This wrongdoing I did was not a good thing to do, and so from now on I will never do it again, even if my life is at stake." That is, from today onward one cuts the stream of it and holds back from it. Although the scriptures, together with the commentaries that draw out their intent, speak of many doors for purifying wrongdoing, the remedy that is complete in every respect is to confess by this confession that has all four powers; and the *Tarkajvālā* (Blaze of Reasoning), the *Great Commentary on the Eight Thousand [Line Perfection of Wisdom]* and other works say that by it even wrongdoing whose fruit is certain to be experienced is purified. As for ourselves: we are not skilled in distinguishing actions and their effects, and even where we do know a little we fail to take up and give up as we should — and so many doors of wrongdoing open every single day. Therefore one should enter the path of the four-power confession continually, and should make a special effort with an eye to purifying whatever obstructs the arising of bodhicitta.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

"Guides of the world, buddhas and bodhisattvas: please accept, and know, that the blameworthy wrongs I have done are a lapse and a fault. This non-virtuous conduct produces an unbearable ripening and is condemned by holy beings; it is not good conduct. So from this time on, from now until awakening, I will never knowingly do it again, even if my life is at stake — I hold back from it." If one does that, one comes to train in the holy Dharma.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-65]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

"Guides, victorious ones together with your heirs: whatever blameworthy wrongdoing there is in my own mindstream, whether proscribed or blameworthy by nature, is a lapse and a fault — so please accept that very lapse, that very fault, just as it is, and know it." Or else one may construe it as: "Accept that I, a lowly one, am indeed one who has lapsed, one who carries a fault." This is a petition: since one is confessing with regret, having seen the fault as a fault and with no wish to hide it, one asks them to regard it in that way. "Wrongs and downfalls such as these carry an unbearable ripening; they are ignoble actions, the lowest sort of deed, condemned by holy beings — not a good thing to do. So from today onward I will never in any way do them again, knowingly and with open eyes, even if my life is at stake: I cut the stream of them and hold back from them properly." In this way one promises to turn away from one's faults. This is also called the power of restraint, or the power of turning away from wrongdoing. As for that: the differences among the wrongs to be purified, and the differences in the way of purifying them, have been explained already; and as for the difference in the remedy that purifies, one must confess, as was explained above, by way of a confession complete in all four powers.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-65]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"You perfect buddhas, blessed ones, who lead the beings to be trained out of the ocean of samsara to the city of liberation, and you their heart-sons, the bodhisattvas: whatever wrongdoing, whatever non-virtuous action, I as a person have piled up with my body, speech and mind is a lapse, something that carries a fault, a cause of samsara and of the lower realms — please accept in your compassion that it is exactly that." So then: "those wrongs of body, speech and mind that I did before are not good, for they are just the sort of cause and condition that produces every suffering of samsara and of the lower realms." Recognizing them for what they are, one says: "From now on, from this point onward, even if it comes to dying, even if my life is at stake, I will most certainly never again do any of these wrongs, these non-virtuous actions, as I did before." This promise not to do them again is what is called the power of restoration — restoring what has been damaged. And he says that if one confesses wrongdoing in this way, by way of the four powers, that wrongdoing is cleansed, or purified, each according to its kind.

#### Scriptural Quotations

> Whoever was heedless before
> but afterwards became heedful
> is beautiful, like the moon freed from clouds —
> as were Nanda, Aṅgulimāla, Darśaka and Śaṅkara.

— *Letter to a Friend* (Suhṛllekha) (cited by Kunzang Pelden as the image showing that one who has confessed wrongdoing and become heedful grows beautiful, like the moon freed from clouds). ⚑ The four names in the last line are proper names of persons in the Tibetan (དགའ་བོ།, སོར་ཕྲེང་།, མཐོང་ལྡན།, བདེ་བྱེད།); the identifications given here are provisional.

> It gives rise to the mindful thought, "This mind of mine is not good";
> it makes one confess each fault and hold back from it afterwards;
> it takes no delight in it — such a one trains in this Dharma of the buddhas.

— Sabzang Mati Panchen cites this as "from the *Mother*" (the Perfection of Wisdom), while Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites it as "from the *Condensed*" (the *Verse Summary of the Perfection of Wisdom*), so the two attributions of this one passage differ. ⚑

> Maitreya, if a bodhisattva, a great being, possesses four things, the wrongdoing he has done and accumulated will be overpowered. What are the four? They are these: practising complete repudiation; practising the antidote thoroughly; the power of restoration; and the power of the support. Practising complete repudiation is having much regret for a non-virtuous action once one has done it. Practising the antidote thoroughly is striving hard at virtuous action even after one has done a non-virtuous action. The power of restoration is obtaining, by properly taking on a commitment, the restraint that does not do it again. The power of the support is going for refuge to the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, and not forsaking bodhicitta. Because such a person relies on what possesses that power, wrongdoing cannot overpower him.

— *Catuḥdharmanirdeśa* (the *Teaching of the Four Dharmas*) (cited by Minyak Kunzang Sonam as the source from which the names of the four powers are taken).

> Whoever has done
> utterly unbearable wrongdoing through a thousand aeons
> will, by one thorough confession,
> have all of it cleansed away.

— *Golden Light Sūtra* (གསེར་འོད་དམ་པ).

> Whatever wrongful action

> Utterly unbearable actions once done

> and by thorough confession and holding back

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Confessing Wrongdoing Is Done by Petitioning the Guides to Accept It as the Fault It Is

All the commentaries agree in the end that this is a petition addressed to the victorious ones together with their heirs: "Please accept that the wrong I have done is a lapse and carries a fault."

###### 2. The Promise "I Will Never Do It Again" Is the Fourth of the Four Powers, the Power of Restoration

Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that this promise — "even if my life is at stake I will never do it again" — is the fourth power, called the power of restoring what has been damaged; Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains the same commitment as the power of restraint, or the power of turning away from wrongdoing.

###### 3. A Confession Complete in All Four Powers Purifies Even Wrongdoing Whose Fruit Is Certain to Be Experienced

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains that the remedy which is complete in every respect is this confession that has all four powers, and that by it even wrongdoing whose fruit is certain to be experienced is purified.

###### 4. Identifying the Four Powers — Complete Repudiation, Thoroughly Practising the Antidote, the Power of Restoration, and the Power of the Support

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the *Catuḥdharmanirdeśa* (the *Teaching of the Four Dharmas*) and identifies each of the four powers in turn.

###### 5. One Should Enter the Path of the Four-Power Confession Continually, Aiming Especially at Purifying Whatever Obstructs the Arising of Bodhicitta

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains that since many doors of wrongdoing open every single day, one should keep to the path of the four-power confession continually, and should make a special effort to purify what obstructs the arising of bodhicitta.

###### 6. Two Kinds of Person Raise the Victory Banner of the Dharma — the One in Whom No Fault Arises, and the One Who Purifies a Fault by Confessing It

Kunzang Pelden divides those who raise the victory banner of the Dharma into these two: the one in whom no fault arises at all, and the one who, when a fault has arisen, purifies it by confession.

###### 7. One Who Has Confessed and Become Heedful Grows Beautiful, Like the Moon Freed From Clouds

Kunzang Pelden cites the *Letter to a Friend* to show, with the image of the moon freed from clouds, that one who was heedless before but became heedful afterwards grows beautiful.

###### 8. "Guides" Means the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas Who Lead Beings Out of the Ocean of Samsara to the City of Liberation

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that "guides" refers to the perfect buddhas who lead beings out of the ocean of samsara to the city of liberation, and to their heart-sons, the bodhisattvas.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** With this verse, at the very close of Chapter 2, one petitions the guides — the victorious ones together with their heirs — to accept that the wrong one has done is indeed a fault; and since it was not good conduct, one promises never to do it again. Many of the commentaries explain that this very promise is the fourth of the four powers, the power of restoration.

**Key points.**

- Confessing wrongdoing takes the form of a petition to the guides, asking them to accept it as the fault it is.
- The promise "I will never do it again" is the fourth of the four powers, the power of restoration — also called the power of restraint, or of turning away from wrongdoing.
- A confession complete in all four powers can purify even wrongdoing whose fruit is certain to be experienced, and it should be aimed especially at purifying whatever obstructs the arising of bodhicitta.
- The four powers, as given in the *Catuḥdharmanirdeśa* (the *Teaching of the Four Dharmas*), are: practising complete repudiation, practising the antidote thoroughly, the power of restoration, and the power of the support.
- Many scriptural passages make it clear that one who has confessed and become heedful grows beautiful, like the moon freed from clouds, and that a single thorough confession can cleanse away even the wrongdoing of a thousand aeons.
- This verse brings Chapter 2, the chapter on confessing wrongdoing, to its close; with the following verse, 3-1, Chapter 3 — fully taking hold of bodhicitta — begins.
