## 6. Chapter Six: Teaching Patience ^6-0


Every bit of good I have gathered
over a thousand ages —
generosity, offerings to the Buddhas, and other good deeds —
can all be destroyed by just one moment of anger. ^6-1


There is no wrongdoing worse than anger,
and no hard practice better than patience.
So I must work hard at patience,
practicing it in every way I can. ^6-2


If I carry around a mind stinging with anger,
my mind will never know real peace.
I will not find joy or happiness,
I will not sleep well, and I will feel unsteady. ^6-3


Even people who owe their comfort and respect
to someone who has been kind and generous to them
can turn against that very person
and even try to harm them, if that person becomes angry. ^6-4


Anger makes friends turn sad and unhappy;
even people won over by generosity stop wanting to be close.
In short, there is no such thing
as someone who is truly happy while feeling angry. ^6-5


Anger, like an enemy, causes
all of these sufferings and more.
But whoever works hard to defeat anger
finds happiness in this life and in future ones too. ^6-6


When I get what I don't want,
or something blocks what I do want,
my mind finds a kind of food in that unhappiness,
and anger grows strong and destroys me. ^6-7


So I must completely cut off
the food that feeds this enemy, anger.
This enemy has no other purpose at all
except to cause me harm. ^6-8


Whatever happens to me,
I should not let it disturb my happy mind.
Being upset does not get me what I want anyway;
it only makes my goodness disappear. ^6-9


If a problem can still be fixed,
what is there to be unhappy about?
And if it truly cannot be fixed,
what good does being unhappy do? ^6-10


I do not want suffering, harsh insults,
unkind words, or being spoken badly of,
for myself or for those I care about —
yet I am happy to wish exactly that on my enemies. ^6-11


The causes of happiness appear only now and then,
while the causes of suffering are very many.
Without suffering, there is no way to grow tired of endless rebirth and seek freedom.
So, my mind, you should stay firm and steady. ^6-12


People who deliberately harm themselves through austerities,
like the Karnapas' rituals, or through burning or cutting themselves,
endure such feelings for no true purpose at all.
So why should I, seeking real freedom, not be able to bear far less? ^6-13


There is nothing at all
that does not become easier with practice.
So, by getting used to bearing small harms,
I should train myself to bear even great harm patiently. ^6-14


Do I not see the pointless suffering
caused by snakebites, insect bites,
hunger, thirst,
and things like rashes and itching? ^6-15


I should not be overly sensitive
to heat, cold, rain, wind,
sickness, being bound, or being struck.
If I act that way, the harm I feel only grows worse. ^6-16


Some people, seeing their own blood,
actually grow braver and steadier.
But some people, seeing someone else's blood,
feel faint and even collapse. ^6-17


This difference comes from
how firm or how unsteady the mind already is.
So I should look past the harm I feel
and not let sufferings overwhelm me. ^6-18


Even when suffering arises, a wise person
should not let their mind become disturbed or unsettled.
When fighting against the disturbing emotions,
there will naturally be many hardships along the way. ^6-19


Looking past every kind of suffering,
and defeating enemies like anger and the rest —
that is what makes someone a true hero and victor.
Anyone else is merely killing something already lifeless. ^6-20


Suffering also has a good side:
through feeling weary of it, pride is cleared away,
compassion arises for those still trapped in endless rebirth,
wrongdoing is avoided, and goodness becomes something to delight in. ^6-21


I do not get angry at a great source of suffering
like bile or other bodily imbalances that cause sickness.
So why would I get angry at beings who have minds,
when they too are simply driven by conditions beyond their control? ^6-22


Just as sickness arises
even though we do not want it to,
in the same way, disturbing emotions arise forcefully in people
even though they do not want them to. ^6-23


People do not think, "Now I will get angry,"
and yet anger still arises suddenly in them.
And anger does not think, "Now I will arise,"
and yet it arises all the same. ^6-24


Every kind of fault
and every kind of wrongdoing
arises through the force of conditions;
none of it happens by its own free choice. ^6-25


Even the group of conditions that comes together to cause something
does not itself think, "Now I will cause this."
And what is caused by them does not think,
"I am causing myself to arise," either. ^6-26


Whatever people imagine as some kind of ultimate "controller,"
or whatever they think of as a fixed "self" —
none of that ever actually thinks,
"Now I will arise," and then arises that way. ^6-27


Since that "self" does not exist before it arises,
what exactly is it that wants to come into being?
And since that self is said to always be fixed on its object,
it could never actually stop existing either — which makes no sense. ^6-28


And if this "self" were truly permanent,
it would clearly have to be inactive, like the sky.
Even meeting other conditions,
something unchanging could not actually do anything at all. ^6-29


And if it still somehow acted while staying the same as before,
what exactly would that action have accomplished?
What connection could there really be
between "the self" and "its own action" in that case? ^6-30


So, everything actually arises depending on other things;
nothing has power over itself in this way.
Understanding this, I should not get angry
at anything at all, as if getting angry at a mere snake made of rope. ^6-31


Someone might object: "But if nothing has control over itself,
then it makes no sense to try to stop anything either."
Not true — relying on effort and the right conditions,
it makes perfect sense to want suffering to come to an end. ^6-32


So, when I see an enemy or even a friend
doing something clearly wrong,
I should think, "This came about because of certain conditions,"
and in that way, stay calm and at peace. ^6-33


If things always turned out exactly as everyone wished,
then no living being would ever suffer at all,
since certainly no one anywhere
truly wants to suffer. ^6-34


Yet out of carelessness, people harm even themselves,
stepping on thorns and the like without meaning to.
And driven by desire, for the sake of winning someone's affection or such things,
people starve themselves and cause themselves other kinds of harm. ^6-35


Some people even hang themselves, jump off cliffs,
eat poison or harmful food,
or live in ways that go against their own good,
harming themselves through their own actions. ^6-36


When someone, overpowered by disturbing emotions,
even goes so far as to kill someone they themselves love,
how could such a person possibly
avoid causing harm to the bodies of others? ^6-37


Since it is the arising of disturbing emotions that leads people
to do things like harm even those they love,
if I do not feel deep compassion for them because of this,
then what is the real meaning of being angry at them at all? ^6-38


If harming others were truly
the basic nature of foolish, immature people,
it would make no sense to be angry at them for it,
just as it makes no sense to be angry at fire for its nature of burning things. ^6-39


And even if their fault is only a temporary flaw,
while beings themselves are naturally good,
it still makes no sense to be angry at them,
just as it makes no sense to be angry at smoke for rising into a clear sky. ^6-40


If I get angry at the stick that actually strikes me,
when it was really thrown by a person,
then, since that person too was driven by their own anger,
it would make more sense to be angry at the anger itself instead. ^6-41


In the past, I too have caused
this kind of harm to other beings.
So it makes sense that this harm to beings
would eventually come back around to harm me too. ^6-42


Both the enemy's weapon and my own body
are, together, the actual cause of my suffering.
Since the enemy brought the weapon and I brought the body,
which one, exactly, should I be angry at? ^6-43


This human body of mine is like an open sore,
so sensitive it can hardly bear to be touched, always causing suffering.
Since I myself am the one who grasped at it out of blind craving,
whom, exactly, should I blame when it gets hurt? ^6-44


Immature beings do not want to suffer,
and yet they crave the very causes of suffering.
So when they are harmed because of their own mistakes,
what use is there in resenting someone else for it? ^6-45


It is just like the guardians of hell,
or a forest of leaf-blades sharp as swords —
this suffering was brought about by my own actions,
so who exactly should I be angry at for it? ^6-46


Those who harm me appear
because they were stirred into motion by my own past actions.
Because of this, they themselves may end up going to hell.
Am I not the one, in truth, who has harmed them? ^6-47


By relying on beings like this to practice patience,
I purify a great deal of my own past wrongdoing.
But because of me, they, in turn,
may endure long and terrible suffering in hell. ^6-48


So really, I am the one harming them,
while they, in truth, are helping me.
So why, my wrongheaded mind,
do you get this so backwards and feel angry at them? ^6-49


If I truly had good qualities within me,
I would not go to hell in the first place.
And if I had already protected myself well,
what real harm would these beings have done to me at all? ^6-50


But if, in return for harm, I cause harm back,
that does not actually protect me at all.
It only ruins my own good conduct,
and it destroys the value of the difficult practice I have undertaken. ^6-51


The mind has no physical body,
so nothing can ever truly harm the mind itself.
It is only because of clinging so strongly to the body
that the body ends up suffering harm. ^6-52


Insults, harsh words,
and unpleasant speech —
none of these can actually harm the body.
So why, my mind, do you become so furious over them? ^6-53


If others' dislike of me
does not actually eat away at me,
whether in this life or in future ones,
why do I resist it so much? ^6-54


If I dislike it only because it gets in the way of my own gain,
I should notice this instead:
that dislike throws away my chance for real gain right here,
while wrongdoing stays firmly rooted in me. ^6-55


It would be better for me to die today
than to live a long life through wrong livelihood.
Even if someone like me lived a very long time,
the suffering of death would still be waiting all the same in the end. ^6-56


Consider someone who dreams a hundred years of happiness
and then wakes up,
compared to someone who enjoys just a moment of happiness
and then wakes up. ^6-57


For both of them, once they wake,
that happiness never comes back again.
Whether the dream was long or short,
in the end, at the moment of waking, it is exactly the same. ^6-58


Even if I gain a great deal of wealth
and enjoy comfort for a very long time,
in the end I will leave empty-handed,
just like someone robbed by a thief. ^6-59


Someone might say, "If I stay alive through wealth,
I can burn away wrongdoing and build up merit."
But if I get angry for the sake of gaining wealth,
will I not just burn away my merit and build up wrongdoing instead? ^6-60


If the very purpose for which I stay alive
ends up being ruined by my own anger,
then what real point is there
in staying alive only to do wrong? ^6-61


If I get angry at someone speaking unkindly about me
because it harms other beings' faith in me,
then why do I not feel that same anger
when I hear that same person speaking unkindly about someone else? ^6-62


I can tolerate other people's lack of faith,
since it depends on causes beyond my control.
So why can I not tolerate unkind words about myself,
when they too arise simply because someone's disturbing emotions have taken hold? ^6-63


Even when sacred images, stupas, and the true teachings
are mocked, ruined, or destroyed,
it does not make sense for me to feel anger about it,
because the Buddhas themselves cannot truly be harmed. ^6-64


So too, when someone harms
my teachers, relatives, or friends,
I should see, as before, that this too arises from conditions,
and let go of my anger. ^6-65


Beings that can feel, and things that cannot feel,
both can cause harm to me.
So why single out only those who can feel and hold a grudge against them?
Instead, I should simply learn to bear the harm patiently. ^6-66


Some do wrong out of confusion;
some become angry out of that same confusion.
Which of them should we say is truly without fault?
And which one should we say is truly at fault? ^6-67


Why did that person, in the past,
create the causes that now lead them to harm others?
Since everything ultimately arises from prior causes,
why should I resent them for it now? ^6-68


Understanding all of this,
I should work hard, in every way I can,
to feel a kind, loving heart
toward every being, and direct all of this toward true goodness. ^6-69


For example, if a fire breaks out in one house
and spreads to another,
it makes sense to remove and throw away
whatever fuel — grass and the like — might let it spread further. ^6-70


In the same way, whenever attachment to something
causes the fire of anger to start spreading,
I should, fearing that my own goodness will burn away,
throw that attachment aside immediately. ^6-71


If a person condemned to death has only their hand cut off instead,
and is thereby set free, is that not fortunate?
And if, through the suffering I experience as a human being,
I am spared from the sufferings of hell, is that not fortunate too? ^6-72


If I cannot even bear
this small amount of suffering right now,
then why do I not work to stop anger,
which is the very cause of the suffering of hell? ^6-73


For the sake of ordinary desires, I have already endured
thousands of experiences like being burned in hell,
and yet through all of that,
I accomplished nothing — neither for myself nor for others. ^6-74


This present suffering, by comparison, is nowhere near as bad,
and it can actually accomplish something truly great.
So I should feel only real joy
at this suffering, which clears away harm for the whole world. ^6-75


If I feel joy and happiness
when someone else is praised for their good qualities,
then why, my mind, do I not feel that same joy
when I praise those very same qualities myself? ^6-76


This joy and happiness of yours
is a source of happiness with nothing wrong in it at all.
It is something praised by those with good qualities themselves,
and it is also the very best way to bring others together. ^6-77


But if you say, "I don't want that kind of happiness
for others too," then, because you refuse to give even simple things like a fair wage,
you will lose out both in ways you can see and in ways you cannot. ^6-78


When my own good qualities are praised,
I want others to feel happy about it too.
So when someone else's good qualities are praised,
why do I refuse to feel happy about it myself? ^6-79


Since I have given rise to the wish for awakening
wanting every being to be happy,
if beings themselves happen to find happiness on their own,
why would I possibly feel angry about that? ^6-80


If I say I want beings to become Buddhas,
worthy of worship throughout the three worlds,
why do I feel upset
just seeing them receive a small bit of ordinary respect now? ^6-81


Someone you are responsible for caring for,
someone you yourself would want to support —
if that person instead receives support from a relative,
would you feel happy about that, or would you feel angry instead? ^6-82


If you would not even wish that much for them,
then how can you claim to want full awakening for them?
Someone who gets angry at another person's good fortune —
how could such a person really have bodhicitta at all? ^6-83


Even if a gift comes to someone else instead of you,
whether it stays in the donor's house
or is given away to that person,
either way, it was never yours to begin with — so what does it matter to you? ^6-84


Why would I throw away
someone else's good qualities, faith, and merit?
Someone who does not feel angry
at another person's own good fortune — why not simply act that way yourself? ^6-85


Not only do you feel no remorse
for the wrongs you yourself have done,
but you also want to compete
with those who have done good deeds instead? ^6-86

If an enemy is unhappy,
what is there for you to be happy about?
Simply wishing for their unhappiness
does not actually cause them any harm at all. ^6-87


Even if your wish causes them suffering,
what real joy is there for you in that?
And if you say, "It satisfies me,"
what could possibly be worse than that kind of satisfaction? ^6-88


This sharp, terrible hook, cast by the fisherman of disturbing emotions,
is unbearably cruel.
Once caught by it, I will surely be boiled
by the guardians of hell in their great cauldrons. ^6-89


Being praised and made famous
brings me no real merit, no real greatness.
It does not make me stronger, does not keep me from sickness,
and does not bring real comfort to my body. ^6-90


If I truly understood what benefits me,
what real benefit is there in fame at all?
If all I want is a happy feeling,
I might as well rely on things like drinking and gambling for that. ^6-91


People lose their wealth and even risk their lives
just to gain fame.
But what real use are mere words?
And once I am dead, who exactly enjoys that fame? ^6-92


When a child's sandcastle collapses,
the child cries in real distress.
In the same way, when praise and fame collapse,
my own mind behaves just like that child. ^6-93


A mere sound has no mind of its own,
so it cannot possibly intend to praise me.
People say, "Others feel joy because of me" —
and that, perhaps, could be a real reason for joy. ^6-94


But whether that joy belongs to someone else
or even to me, what real benefit does another person's joy bring to me?
That joy and happiness belong entirely to that other person;
I myself gain no share of it at all. ^6-95


If I am happy simply because someone else is happy,
then I should feel that way about everyone equally.
So why is it that when someone feels happy through praising another person,
I do not feel happy, but instead I feel unhappy? ^6-96


So the joy that arises in me
just from being praised myself
does not actually make sense in that way either.
It is simply the behavior of an immature mind. ^6-97


Praise and the like actually distract me;
they also destroy my healthy sense of urgency for real change.
They create jealousy toward those with good qualities,
and they can even ruin real excellence when it does appear. ^6-98


So those who stand nearby, ready
to undermine my praise and reputation,
are they not actually helping to protect me
from falling into the lower realms? ^6-99


Since what I truly seek is freedom,
I have no need to be bound by wealth and respect.
So how could I possibly feel angry
at those who are actually helping to free me from those chains? ^6-100


Just as the Buddha's blessings work,
these people, without even meaning to,
become like an open door for me, keeping me from entering suffering.
So how could I feel angry at them? ^6-101


Someone might say, "That person is blocking my good fortune,"
but even then, anger is not the right response.
There is no hard practice equal to patience —
so why would I not simply devote myself to that instead? ^6-102


And if it is my own fault
that I fail to be patient with this,
then it is truly I myself
who am blocking my own cause of goodness. ^6-103


Whatever would not exist without a certain cause,
and exists only because that cause exists —
if that cause is truly the reason something exists,
how could it be called an obstacle to that very thing? ^6-104


A beggar who happens to arrive at the right time
is not blocking someone's chance to be generous.
And someone who helps another person become a monk or nun
cannot rightly be called an obstacle to that ordination either. ^6-105


Beggars are common enough in this world,
but those who actually cause harm are rare.
After all, if I have not harmed others in the past,
hardly anyone would want to harm me now. ^6-106


So, just as one might be delighted to find
an unexpected treasure appear at home without any effort at all,
I should feel real delight toward my enemies,
since they help me on the path to awakening. ^6-107


Since this fruit of patience comes about
because both my enemy and I worked together to bring it about,
it makes sense that the credit for this fruit
should go first to my enemy, since they are the very cause of my patience. ^6-108


Someone might say, "But my enemy never intended to help me practice patience,
so they don't deserve to be honored for it."
But then, since the true teachings themselves are also just a fitting cause
for spiritual progress, why would we honor them either? ^6-109


Someone might say, "But this enemy actually intends to harm me,
so they should not be honored."
But if my enemy were instead trying to help me, like a doctor,
how would I ever get the chance to practice patience at all? ^6-110


So it is precisely because of someone's hostile intentions
that patience is able to arise in me.
Since they are the true cause of my patience,
they deserve to be honored, just like the true teachings themselves. ^6-111


That is why the Buddha himself said
that beings are a field of merit, just like the Buddhas themselves.
Many people have gained the perfection of true excellence
simply by making beings happy. ^6-112


Since both beings and the Buddhas
play an equal part in bringing about a Buddha's enlightened qualities,
it does not make sense to show respect only to the Buddhas
and not to beings in the very same way. ^6-113


The greatness of an intention is not measured by itself alone,
but by what it actually produces — in that same way,
beings also have real greatness,
and so, in that sense, beings and Buddhas are truly equal. ^6-114


Making an offering to someone filled with loving kindness
is itself a way of honoring the greatness of beings.
And the merit that comes from having faith in the Buddha
is also a way of honoring the greatness of the Buddha. ^6-115


Since beings share in bringing about
a Buddha's awakened qualities,
it makes sense to consider them equal in this way.
But of course, nothing can truly equal the Buddhas,
whose ocean of good qualities has no limit at all. ^6-116


Even if only a small portion
of that single, unmatched treasury of good qualities
appeared in a being,
offering all three realms would still not be enough to properly honor it. ^6-117


Since beings hold within themselves the potential
for these supreme qualities of a Buddha,
it makes sense, considering this shared potential,
to treat beings themselves as worthy of honor. ^6-118


Besides this, who else could ever
repay those who, with sincere and steady kindness,
bring immeasurable benefit to others,
except by working to make beings happy? ^6-119


Since even giving up their own body and entering the deepest hell for the sake of beings
is how the Buddhas repay their kindness,
whatever great harm beings might do to me,
I should still respond to all of it with only kindness. ^6-120


Even the Buddhas, who are like great lords to me,
are willing to give up even their own precious bodies for the sake of beings.
So how could I, foolish as I am,
feel proud and unwilling to serve beings myself? ^6-121


Whatever brings beings happiness brings joy to the Buddhas;
whatever harms beings brings the Buddhas sorrow.
So by bringing joy to beings, I bring joy to all the Buddhas;
and by harming beings, I harm the Buddhas themselves. ^6-122


Just as nothing can bring comfort to a mind
while the whole body is engulfed in flames,
in the same way, when beings are harmed,
there is no way for those with great compassion to feel joy. ^6-123


So today, for all the times I have harmed beings
and thereby caused sorrow to those of great compassion,
I confess every one of those wrongs individually,
and I ask the Buddhas to be patient with whatever has displeased them. ^6-124


From now on, in order to bring joy to the Buddhas,
I will make myself a servant to the whole world.
Even if crowds of beings trample on my head
or even kill me, I will not turn back — please, protectors of the world, be pleased with me. ^6-125


There is no doubt at all that these compassionate protectors
regard every being as their very own self.
So, seeing that beings themselves appear in the very form of these protectors,
why would I not show them the same respect? ^6-126


This alone is what truly pleases the Buddhas.
This alone is what truly accomplishes my own deepest purpose.
This alone is what clears away the suffering of the world.
So I should always devote myself to this, without exception. ^6-127


For example, if one of a king's officials
causes harm to many people,
even those with the power to strike back
usually hold themselves back, seeing the bigger picture. ^6-128


This is not because that official is acting alone —
it is really the power and authority of the king behind them.
In the same way, I should never look down on
or harm any weak being who causes me harm. ^6-129


Because behind them stand the guardians of hell
and the compassionate Buddhas themselves,
just as ordinary people show respect to a fierce king,
I should work to make all beings happy. ^6-130


Even an angry king
could never inflict on me
the kind of suffering
that comes from displeasing beings and ending up in hell. ^6-131


And even a king's favor, however great,
could never give me
what comes from making beings happy —
namely, complete Buddhahood itself, which nothing else can grant. ^6-132


Setting aside the future attainment of Buddhahood,
which comes from making beings happy,
do I not already see, even in this very life,
great glory, fame, and happiness coming from it? ^6-133


Through patience, one gains beauty and other such qualities
throughout one's life,
along with good health and a good reputation,
a long life, and even the vast happiness of a universal ruler. ^6-134


This is the end of the sixth chapter, called "Teaching Patience," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^6-a
