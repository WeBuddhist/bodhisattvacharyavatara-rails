## Combined Commentary Summaries
The commentators provide a highly structured, philosophically rich, and unified analysis of Verse 1-4. This verse marks the beginning of the actual explanation of the stages of the path, focusing on urging the practitioner to extract the essence of a precious human life of leisure and opportunity (*kṣaṇa-sampad* / *dal 'byor*). The commentators organize their explanations around three major thematic lines: the definition and structure of this precious support, the extreme difficulty of its obtainment (causes and analogies), and its ultimate purpose—accomplishing the goals of a person (*puruṣārtha* / *skyes bu'i don*).

### 1. The Definition and Structure of "Leisure and Opportunity" (*kṣaṇa-sampad*)
All three commentators agree that this precious support consists of eighteen distinct qualities: the eight freedoms (leisures / *dal ba*) and the ten endowments (fortunes / *'byor pa*).
- **The Eight Freedoms (Leisures):** According to **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Ngulchu Thokme**, leisure means being free from the eight non-leisures (*mi khom pa'i gnas brgyad*): birth in hell, as a hungry ghost, as an animal, as a barbarian (in an uncivilized borderland), as a long-lived god, holding wrong views, birth in an era without a Buddha, or being mute (and mentally incapable). **Sazang Mati Panchen** attributes this standard list to Prajñākaramati's *Great Commentary* (*Pañjikā*), while **Gyaltsab Je** emphasizes that these states are called "non-leisures" because they afford no opportunity to practice virtue.
- **The Ten Endowments (Fortunes):** The commentators divide these into five personal fortunes (*rang 'byor*) and five circumstantial fortunes (*gzhan 'byor*).
  - *Five Personal Fortunes:* Being human, born in a central land (where the Dharma is available), having complete/intact faculties, having an unreversed end of actions (not committing or causing the five heinous crimes of immediate retribution), and having faith in the source (the three baskets of scriptures). Both **Gyaltsab Je** and **Sazang Mati Panchen** cite the *Śrāvakabhūmi* (*Hearer Grounds*) as the source of this classification.
  - *Five Circumstantial Fortunes:* A Buddha has appeared, he teaches the holy Dharma, the teaching remains, there are followers who practice it, and there is loving compassion from others (patrons and spiritual mentors who support practitioners). **Gyaltsab Je** and **Sazang Mati Panchen** also attribute this list to the *Śrāvakabhūmi*.

### 2. The Extreme Difficulty of Obtainment: Causes and Analogies
The commentators emphasize that this eighteen-fold support is exceptionally rare because the causes required to secure it are incredibly difficult to cultivate.
- **Gyaltsab Je's Causal Formula:** **Gyaltsab Je** provides a precise scholastic breakdown of the causes required to obtain this support:
  1. *Ethical discipline* (*tsul khrims*) as the foundation (noting that simply obtaining a basic human body requires guarding at least one type of moral discipline).
  2. *Generosity* (*sbyin pa*) and other perfections as helpers or companions.
  3. *Stainless aspiration* (*smon lam*) as the linking connection.
  Because accomplishing this combination is extremely difficult, the support of leisure and fortune is exceptionally rare.
- **The Blind Turtle Analogy:** To illustrate this rarity, **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** cite Nagarjuna's *Letter to a Friend* (*Suhṛllekha*), which compares the difficulty of obtaining a human birth from a lower realm to a blind turtle putting its neck into the yoke of a single wooden yoke drifting on a vast ocean. Since the turtle rises only once every hundred years and the yoke is tossed by the wind, the chances of their meeting are incredibly slim—yet obtaining a human birth is even more difficult.
- **Other Scriptural Evidence:** 
  - **Sazang Mati Panchen** quotes the *Sūtras on the Perfections* (Prajñāpāramitā) to show that even a basic human life is rare, let alone one endowed with all leisures and opportunities. He also cites Candragomin's *Letter to a Disciple* (*Śiṣyalekha*), asking who would make such a state—which excels even a wish-fulfilling jewel—fruitless.
  - **Ngulchu Thokme** cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* (Compendium of Training) quoting the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* (Biography of Noble Maitreya) to substantiate the extreme difficulty of finding free and pure leisure and opportunity.

### 3. The Ultimate Purpose: Accomplishing the Goals of a Person (*puruṣārtha*)
All three commentators explain that having obtained this rare support, its primary purpose is to accomplish the goals of a person (*puruṣārtha* / *skyes bu'i don*).
- **Defining the Goals:** The commentators define these goals as twofold:
  1. *High rebirth* (*增上生 / mngon mtho*): Temporary happiness in higher realms of gods and humans.
  2. *Definitive goodness* (*決定勝 / nges legs*): Ultimate liberation (nirvana) and complete Buddhahood.
- **The Urgency of Practice:** The commentators issue a stark warning: because we mostly reside in states of non-leisure (such as lower realms) where the opportunities to practice virtue are non-existent, failing to practice the holy Dharma now will make it virtually impossible to obtain such a perfect support again in future lifetimes.
  - **Ngulchu Thokme** quotes the sūtras to urge: "Therefore, do not make this leisure and opportunity in vain!"
  - **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Gyaltsab Je** agree that the wise must make great effort (*tsang sgrub*) to extract the essence of this life, using this favorable opportunity to accomplish the supreme fruit of awakening before death or decay deprives them of it.
