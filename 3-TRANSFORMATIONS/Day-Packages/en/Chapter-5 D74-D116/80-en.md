## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth verses of the fifth chapter. Let us look together at this point: running away with the body alone does not give up desire. Guarding the mind is the root of every training.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an explanation that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Even the perfection of wisdom, in the end, comes down to our own mind. The main point of all the teachings is what is called "the secret of the mind." This means that the mind's nature is empty, with no independent self. A sutra also says, "The mind has no mind; the nature of mind is clear light." This is called a secret because ordinary people, hearers, and solitary realizers have not fully understood the selflessness of phenomena. So it stays hidden from them. If we do not know this secret nature of the mind, then even if we wish to remove suffering and gain happiness, we will wander about with no purpose. All the conduct of bodhisattvas — the six perfections, skillful means, aspiration, strength, wisdom, and so on — arises based on our own mind.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will not let my attention scatter toward outer objects. Instead, I will guard my own mind well, with mindfulness and awareness.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the Mind)* Guarding our own mind well with mindfulness and awareness is the root of every discipline. Outer discipline alone does not destroy the afflictions. For example, a novice monk may run away from desire with the body, but this does not give up the desire in the mind. We must give up desire in the mind itself. So today, we will not run away only from outer situations. Instead, we will guard our own mind continuously with mindfulness and awareness.

### Verse 5-16

> **Rail source:** `2-RAILS/Verses/5-16-summary.md` &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Rail status:** `draft`

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-16`):*

> Recitation, ascetic practice, and the rest — even if pursued for a long time — are called pointless by those who know, when done with a distracted mind.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

This teaches that the perfection of meditative concentration, too, is precisely the mind. Even though one practices, for a long time, all manner of verbal recitation and physical ascetic practice, if this is done while the mind is scattered elsewhere, the Blessed One, who knows reality as it is, has declared it to be pointless. Why is this taught? Because the *Samādhi-saṃgraha Sūtra* says: "Monks, ascetic practice and recitation done with a mind distracted toward desire become fruitless," and so forth. Here as well, the term "fruitless" is applied to an inferior result, not to the complete absence of any result.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-16]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that if recitation, ascetic practice, and the rest are performed for a long period while the mind remains scattered, the Buddha, who knows reality as it is, has declared this to be pointless. The arising of the great power of virtue from recitation, ascetic practice, and so forth must likewise be accomplished on the basis of a one-pointed mind, and this too depends on the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Meditative concentration, too, is in general a cultivation of the mind. Yet even if one grows accustomed to, and practices for a long period, all manner of extensive ascetic practice that supports or serves as a method for producing that concentration in one's own mind-stream — verbal recitation, or bodily practices such as going without food or without a change of clothing — if, while the mind is thinking of desirable objects and the like, one is distracted elsewhere, then, like chewing on a strip of wood or on one's tongue, whatever recitation, ascetic practice, and so forth one has performed is declared by the Buddha, the Blessed One who directly knows or realizes the reality of all phenomena, not to accomplish the intended purpose.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

The completion of the perfection of meditative concentration depends on a mind that is free from mental sinking and excitement, and that is not overtaken by an attitude of either eagerness or discouragement. Even if one practices, for a long time, all manner of ascetic practice — such as the recitation of mantras and dhāraṇīs, and fasting retreats — if this is done while the mind is scattered elsewhere, the Buddha, who knows reality as it is, has declared it pointless in the sutras, because it does not produce the intended result.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Even though one practices, for a long time, all manner of physical ascetic practice and the recitation of secret mantras and the like, if this is done while the mind is scattered elsewhere, the Buddha declares that knowing the reality of phenomena in this way is pointless — that is, of little value. For the *Samādhi-saṃgraha Sūtra* says: "Monks, ascetic practice and recitation done with a mind distracted toward desire become fruitless," and the Mother [the *Prajñāpāramitā*] likewise says: "Through a distracted mind, even one's own purpose will not be accomplished."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Even though one grows accustomed to, and practices for a long time, all manner of ascetic practice — verbal recitation of mantras and the maintenance of awareness-mantras, and bodily practices such as fasting retreats and restraining the sense faculties — if, through sleepiness, dullness, and the like, the mind's engagement becomes weak and unclear, or the mind becomes distracted elsewhere from its object of focus, then whatever has been done is declared by the Tathāgata to fail to accomplish the intended purpose of directly realizing the reality of all phenomena. For, as the Mother says: "If, through a distracted mind, even one's own purpose is not accomplished, how much less another's purpose? Therefore, not even the smallest opportunity for the mind to be distracted should be allowed," and so forth. This shows that meditative concentration, too, is accomplished only by the mind resting one-pointedly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Even though one practices and grows accustomed, for a long period of time, to all manner of ascetic practice — the recitation of secret mantras and the like, dwelling respectfully in a state of humility, and restraining the sense faculties — if, not being caught by mindfulness and introspection, the mind falls into sleepiness, agitation, restlessness, and the like, and becomes distracted from a virtuous object of focus toward something else, then whatever one has done is declared by the Omniscient One to be without purpose or result, in that it does not directly realize the abiding nature of phenomena exactly as it is. Here, "without purpose" means that the intended purpose, or the needed result, is merely inferior or slight — it does not mean there is no result at all — just as one might say of a son who cannot perform his duties, "he is no son," or apply the word "woman" pejoratively to someone incapable.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

By "recitation" is meant such things as brahmins reciting the Vedas — reading many texts aloud — and by "ascetic practice" is meant such things as naked non-Buddhist ascetics who abstain from food and clothing, going about naked in the practice of liberation, or restricting one's diet, and other such practices in which the body rises up under the force of severe austerity. Even if one practices such things for a long time, in brief, if the mind is distracted toward all the objects of desire — form, sound, smell, taste, and touch — then whatever has been done is declared by the fully perfect Buddha, the Blessed One, to be pointless with respect to knowing reality — that is, knowing the true nature of reality without error. The main point is that, since all phenomena depend on the mind, if that mind cannot be brought onto the genuine path, then physical and verbal recitation and ascetic practice of that kind are, by nature, pointless.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-16]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree That Recitation and Asceticism Are Pointless When the Mind Is Distracted

All the commentaries agree that recitation and ascetic practice, however long sustained, are declared pointless by the Buddha when performed with a mind scattered elsewhere; only practice undertaken with a gathered, one-pointed mind bears fruit.

###### 2. "Pointless" Means an Inferior Result, Not No Result At All

Khenpo Zhenga's gloss and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both clarify that "pointless" here is a term applied to an inferior or slight result — not to the total absence of any result — just as one might call someone incapable of a task "no son," or apply the word "woman" pejoratively to someone incapable.

###### 3. The Mother Sutra Teaches That a Distracted Mind Fails to Accomplish Even One's Own Purpose

Ngulchu Thogme and Sabzang Mati Panchen both cite the *Prajñāpāramitā*'s teaching that a distracted mind fails to accomplish even one's own purpose, let alone another's — so not even the smallest opportunity for distraction should be allowed.

###### 4. Meditative Concentration Itself Requires a One-Pointed Mind Undistracted from Its Object

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the perfection of meditative concentration consists in a one-pointed mind undistracted from its object of focus, brought about through habituation to placing the mind primarily on that object.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** However much verbal recitation and physical austerity one performs, if the mind is scattered elsewhere it fails to yield the intended result and so is called pointless; the great power of virtuous practice arises only in dependence on one-pointed meditative concentration.

**Key points.**

- **A distracted mind renders practice pointless.** However long one sustains recitation and physical austerities, if the mind is not held by mindfulness and introspection but is distracted toward the five sense-objects and elsewhere, this is declared pointless by the one who knows reality, the Buddha.
- **What "pointless" means.** "Pointless" is not a denial that any result whatsoever occurs; it is a term applied, by way of negation, to an inferior or very slight result.
- **The Mother's teaching.** The *Prajñāpāramitā* teaches that through a distracted mind, even one's own purpose is not accomplished, let alone another's.
- **The nature of meditative concentration.** The perfection of meditative concentration is held to be a one-pointed mind, free from mental sinking and excitement, undistracted from its object of focus.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

This teaches that the perfection of wisdom, too, is precisely the mind. Whoever is frightened by the terror of saṃsāra, if they do not know this — the supreme sovereign of all phenomena, the secret of the mind, which lies beyond the domain of the childish and bears the character of the selflessness of all phenomena — then even though they wish to attain happiness and to overcome suffering, they will wander pointlessly through the night, their actions bearing no fruit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-17]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that "the supreme sovereign of phenomena, the secret of the mind" can, from the conventional standpoint, be understood as follows: since the agent behind saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, or the ultimate determinant of happiness and suffering, is the mind — which, whether tamed or untamed, accumulates good and bad karma and thereby experiences the resultant phenomena of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa — the root comes down to the mind. As for why the adventitious defilements upon the mind are removable, if this is put in terms of the Mantra Vehicle alone, one might wonder whether it can be explained simply in terms of a conventional clarity-and-awareness, calling the root nature of the mind "clear light." The main point, though, is that one must establish that the adventitious defilements upon the mind are removable by taking as the reason the mind's ultimate nature — the mind's final abiding reality, its lack of inherent existence, its being empty of true existence. He states that if one does not know this crucial point — the secret of the mind, this manner of abiding conventionally and ultimately — then even while wishing to attain the happiness of liberation and to overcome the suffering of cyclic existence, however much ascetic practice one undertakes, the effort becomes pointless, and one wanders through the night in vain.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Wisdom, too — whatever supreme sovereign or essential point of the objects of knowledge a person may take hold of, namely the luminosity, empty of self, that is the nature of the mind — exists in all beings, yet is not known or realized by all; hence it is secret. Since all outer phenomena arise, like reflections in a mirror, upon the mind, and being fashioned by mind do not go beyond one's own mind, they are merely the play of the mind; for this reason, this mind must chiefly be settled and ascertained. If one does not know this secret of the mind — that it is empty and selfless — then even though one wishes to attain the happiness of nirvāṇa and to uproot all the suffering of saṃsāra, these wishes will be pointless and one will wander through the night, that is, without any essence. Even hearers and solitary realizers, though they realize the mere selflessness of the person, have not realized the selflessness of phenomena; hence this too remains secret even from them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Wisdom, too, depends on the mind. Since the supreme sovereign or essential point of phenomena for a person — namely the secret of the mind, this ultimate truth — is not to be taught to an unfit vessel, if this is not known, then even though one wishes to attain unsurpassed happiness and to overcome the suffering of saṃsāra, these wishes will be pointless, and one will wander through the night as one pleases. Hence wisdom, too, depends on the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

That which a person takes as the essential point — namely the supreme sovereign, the secret of the mind, that is, emptiness of inherent nature — is, according to the great commentary, called "secret" because it lies beyond the domain of the childish; and according to the detailed explanation, it is called so because the reality of selflessness is to be concealed within one's own mind. If one does not know this, then even though one wishes to attain happiness and overcome suffering, these wishes will be pointless, and one will wander in saṃsāra — as the sutra says: "Knowing that beings wander confused like a deer caught in a snare, one endowed with wisdom roams freely through the sky like a bird." Hence wisdom is chiefly the mind itself, for the *Gaṇḍavyūha* likewise says: "All the conduct of bodhisattvas depends on one's own mind." Since skillful means, aspiration, strength, and wisdom are also differentiations of wisdom itself, these too have the mind as their chief.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

That which is, for a person, the supreme sovereign or essential point of all phenomena, mundane and supramundane — as the Mother says, "the mind has no mind; the nature of mind is clear light" — if one does not know this secret of the mind, this ultimate truth, the sphere of reality, and does not possess the correct view, then even though one has the discriminating intention that wishes to attain the happiness of liberation and to overcome the suffering of saṃsāra, and strives at practice, such confused persons as non-Buddhists engage in pointless austerities — such as relying on the five fires — and merely wander in saṃsāra, like a kāśi flower in the sky driven by the wind, without essence. For the ultimate attainment of liberation comes only from knowing the abiding nature of all phenomena; hence wisdom, too, is accomplished only through the mind knowing the true nature of the mind. Therefore, as the *Gaṇḍavyūha* says: "All the conduct of bodhisattvas depends on one's own mind. All the maturing and taming of sentient beings likewise depends on one's own mind" — thus all virtue, too, is accomplished through guarding the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For a person wishing to be free from the suffering of existence and to attain the undefiled peace and happiness of liberation, that which is the supreme sovereign or essential point of all phenomena, mundane and supramundane, is the secret of the mind — that is, the abiding nature of the mind. It is called "the secret of the mind" because it lies beyond the domain of ordinary conceptual engagement, or because it is not to be taught to an unfit vessel. This reality, so difficult to realize, is the treasury of all worldly and supramundane phenomena — the mind's ultimate reality, free from the extremes of existence, non-existence, and so on. If one does not know and has not grown accustomed to this genuine abiding nature, then even though one has the intention wishing to attain the peace and happiness of nirvāṇa and to overcome the suffering of saṃsāra together with its causes, and strives in the methods for this, those confused about the method will, however diligently they strive, accomplish none of their wished-for aims — liberation, omniscience, and the rest — and will merely wander through existence at will, like a kāśi flower in the sky driven by the wind. This shows that, without realizing the meaning of this abiding nature, and while still grasping at things as real, no amount of virtuous practice or ascetic effort will bring about the peaceful happiness of nirvāṇa that has abandoned suffering together with its cause.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The perfection of wisdom, too, must be brought to completion through the mind. That which, among all objects of knowledge, is the supreme sovereign for a person is the mind itself; its nature — emptiness, selflessness, luminosity, the union of clarity and emptiness — being unrealized by those of lesser intelligence, is an extremely secret and profound point that must be known. If one does not know this, then whatever aspirations one holds in mind — liberation, the happiness of nirvāṇa, the state of omniscience, the overcoming of the suffering of saṃsāra in the three realms — none of these can be attained, nor can the suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death in saṃsāra be overcome; and so, he says, one wanders about pointlessly, that is, at one's own whim, without essence. Hence the chief point, the root or essential nexus of all phenomena, is one's own mind itself; and it is taught that all the practices of the six perfections of virtue are brought to their culmination or completion on the basis of this very mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-17]]

#### Scriptural Quotations

> "The mind has no mind; the nature of mind is clear light."
> — the *Mother* (*Prajñāpāramitā*)

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Wisdom Too Depends on the Mind: Not Knowing the Secret of the Mind Makes the Wish for Happiness Fruitless

All the commentaries agree that the perfection of wisdom depends on the mind, and that whoever does not know this supreme sovereign of phenomena — the secret of the mind, its emptiness and selflessness — wanders pointlessly through saṃsāra even while wishing for happiness and freedom from suffering.

###### 2. "Secret" Means Beyond the Domain of the Childish — Even Hearers and Solitary Realizers Do Not See It

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that "secret" means lying beyond the domain of the childish, or being unfit to teach to an unready vessel; even hearers and solitary realizers, though they realize the selflessness of the person, have not realized the selflessness of phenomena, so it remains secret even from them.

###### 3. Skillful Means, Aspiration, Strength, and Wisdom Are All Differentiations of Wisdom, So All Six Perfections Culminate Through the Mind

Ngulchu Thogme and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that skillful means, aspiration, strength, and primordial wisdom are differentiations of wisdom itself, so that all six perfections are brought to completion through the mind.

###### 4. Wisdom That Realizes Suchness Is Itself Accomplished by Guarding the Mind from Grasping at True Existence

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the wisdom that realizes the abiding nature is itself accomplished by guarding the mind from the deluded clinging that grasps phenomena as substantially real.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The perfection of wisdom, too, rests in the mind; not knowing the supreme sovereign of all phenomena — the secret of the mind — makes every effort at the practice of the six perfections come back to guarding and understanding one's own mind.

**Key points.**

- **What "the secret of the mind" means.** It is the mind's abiding nature — emptiness, selflessness, and luminosity — which lies beyond the domain of the childish and, since hearers and solitary realizers have not realized the selflessness of phenomena, remains secret even to them; conventionally the root of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa is the mind, and ultimately the mind's very nature is empty.
- **The cost of not realizing it.** Without realizing this secret, one wishes for happiness and to remove suffering, yet wanders pointlessly through saṃsāra, and neither liberation nor omniscience is attained however much austerity is undertaken.
- **All six perfections come down to the mind.** Skillful means, aspiration, strength, primordial wisdom, and the practice of all six perfections culminate in guarding and accomplishing one's own mind.
- **Scriptural support.** The great Mother sutra teaches, "the mind has no mind; the nature of mind is clear light"; the *Gaṇḍavyūha* teaches that all bodhisattva conduct depends on one's own mind; the *Prajñāpāramitā-saṃcayagāthā* and the *Kāśyapaparivarta* are likewise cited in support.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For this reason, he explains, since accomplishing great virtue depends on the mind, and since removing the various kinds of faults must likewise come through taming the mind, guarding the mind comes down to being the very root. Regarding "held well," there are two different ways of explaining it among the commentators: one holds that it means being well held by mindfulness and introspection, and, once held, continually and repeatedly examined so that it is not lost, and thereby well guarded in an unbroken stream; the other divides mindfulness and introspection, so that mindfulness first properly recognizes what is and is not to be engaged in, and having held this well, introspection then repeatedly examines whether one is or is not acting accordingly, and guards accordingly — there are these two ways of understanding it. Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind from what is unwholesome, other disciplines — bodily conduct and verbal speech — are, relative to this discipline of guarding the mind, merely ordinary; hence he says, what use are many disciplines, since they alone cannot accomplish anything of great importance.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-18]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Since all the fears of suffering in this life and the next, and all virtue as well, arise from the mind, therefore this mind of mine must be well held by mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded, and must be well guarded by introspection that repeatedly examines body and mind — for every instruction is contained within this. As for the means of guarding one's own mind, apart from the discipline that serves as the support for a sense of conscience and shame — namely wearing the white robe with the sacred thread as a lay vow-holder, or the saffron robes of full ordination — no other observance, such as wearing a white garment, binding up the hair on the crown of the head, or wearing saffron robes, becomes a support for guarding the mind through a sense of conscience and shame. So what use are the many different disciplines of this kind, which are merely exhausting and fruitless? There is no need for them at all.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-18]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Since abandoning faults and accomplishing good qualities depend on the mind, therefore I must hold this mind of mine well through mindfulness and introspection, and guard it well so that it does not decline. Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind, what use are the many other disciplines — recitation and the like — that do not guard the mind? There is no need for them.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Therefore I must hold this mind well through mindfulness, and guard it well through introspection. Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind, what use are many other disciplines? — meaning that they are pointless.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Since all phenomena depend on the mind, therefore this mind of mine must be well held through mindfulness and guarded well through introspection; for every instruction is contained within this. Guarding the mind is itself the supreme discipline; apart from it, what use are the many other disciplines that only bring exhaustion without result? There is no need for them; one should strive instead to tame the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-18]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

As explained just above, since the mind is the root of all virtue and non-virtue, and since there is benefit in guarding it and fault in not guarding it, therefore I must hold this mind well, relying on mindfulness, so that it stays firm, and must guard it well through introspection, relying on it, so that it does not decline — or, alternatively: having held this mind well through mindfulness and introspection, I must strive so that it does not decline under the influence of unfavorable conditions, and guard it well. Thus, since all the trainings for not allowing bodhicitta and the rest to decline are gathered within this discipline of guarding the mind from the afflictions, this discipline is the supreme and unsurpassed chief among all disciplines; apart from this alone, other physical and verbal disciplines cannot serve as a means of guarding the mind from unfavorable conditions, and so become fruitless, merely a cause of exhaustion — so what use are the many other such external disciplines of body and speech? They lack any real benefit or essential purpose.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-18]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Since all harm arises from the mind, and all virtue likewise arises from the mind, therefore this very mind of mine must be well held through mindfulness, and must be well guarded through introspection. As for the means of guarding the mind, apart from the two disciplines that serve as the support for a sense of conscience and shame — the white robe with the sacred thread, and the saffron robes of full ordination — matted hair and patchwork robes and the like serve no purpose; hence, what use are many other disciplines? There is no need for them.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC13_KTB -->
##### The Novice Monk Amoghadarshin and the God Indra's Test

In a side-note to Ngulchu Thogme's commentary on the phrase "apart from the discipline of guarding the mind," it is told how a vow is guarded by the powerful ally that is the mind. Long ago there lived a bodhisattva named Amoghadarshin, a novice monk of perfectly pure discipline. Wishing to rouse him from complacency, Indra, the lord of the gods, transformed himself into a beautiful young woman, lovely to behold and adorned with every kind of ornament, who laughed and showed affection toward the novice. The novice fled from place to place, but the girl pursued him, following close behind; and he said:

> I will renounce this through the mind —
> what use is there in fleeing with the body?
> A body that is firm, by fleeing alone,
> is unable to give up desire.

Having said this, she vanished, just as she had appeared.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Guarding the Mind Is the Chief of All Disciplines, Since Mind Is the Root of Both Fault and Virtue

All the commentaries agree that since abandoning faults and accomplishing good qualities — indeed all virtue and non-virtue — are rooted in the mind, the discipline of guarding the mind is itself the supreme discipline among all disciplines.

###### 2. "Held Well" Means Mindfulness; "Guarded Well" Means Introspection

Khenpo Zhenga's gloss, Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, and His Holiness the Dalai Lama, among others, explain that "held well" refers to being held by the mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded, while "guarded well" refers to being guarded by the introspection that repeatedly examines the state of body and mind — both mindfulness and introspection being indispensable.

###### 3. Other Outer Disciplines of Body and Speech Are Fruitless Exertion Unless the Mind Is Guarded

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, His Holiness the Dalai Lama, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain that outer disciplines of body and speech — such as the white robe and sacred thread, the saffron robes of ordination, or recitation — are, relative to the discipline of guarding the mind, merely ordinary, and become fruitless exertion if the mind itself is not guarded.

###### 4. The Story of the Novice Monk Shows Directly That Desire Must Be Given Up Through the Mind, Not by Fleeing with the Body

The story of the novice monk tested by Indra shows directly that desire cannot be given up by fleeing with the body alone — it must be given up through the mind.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Since fear and suffering, along with all virtue, arise from the mind, and since abandoning faults and accomplishing good qualities depend above all on the mind, one's own mind must be well held and well guarded; guarding the mind is the supreme discipline, without which many other disciplines serve little purpose.

**Key points.**

- **How to guard the mind.** The mind must be held by a mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded, and guarded by an introspection that repeatedly examines the state of body and mind; since every instruction is gathered within this single discipline, it is the chief and supreme of all disciplines.
- **Outer disciplines are fruitless exertion without it.** Without guarding the mind, wearing the white robe and sacred thread, or the saffron robes of ordination, or performing recitation and other accomplishments of outer body and speech, cannot by themselves achieve anything of great importance; apart from guarding the mind, the many other disciplines amount to fruitless exertion.
- **By way of example.** When the novice monk Amoghadarshin was roused from complacency by the god Indra appearing in a woman's form, he declared that desire cannot be given up by fleeing with the body — it must be given up through the mind — and fled accordingly.
- **Scriptural support.** The *Suhṛllekha* teaches that the mind is the root of the Dharma; the *Ratna-rāśi Sūtra* teaches that mind purifies the body, not the body the mind; and the *Gaṇḍavyūha* teaches that any undertaking that causes bodhicitta to decline is the work of Māra.
