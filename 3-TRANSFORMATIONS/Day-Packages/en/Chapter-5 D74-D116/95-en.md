## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the fifty-fifth and fifty-sixth verses of the fifth chapter. If even the Buddha could not fulfill everyone's wishes, what should we do when people's wishes do not agree?

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following story, which is easy to understand and encouraging. Even when we do good for others, some people become pleased and some do not. Even our teacher Shakyamuni could not please Sunakshatra, Devadatta, and others. So we ordinary people cannot please everyone. For that reason, we do not let our own mind lose heart. People's thoughts differ, and some have faith and some do not. All this comes from the afflictions, such as desire, anger, and jealousy. So we must take care of these people with love and compassion, as well as we can.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, even when other people's wishes do not agree, I will not get angry. I will practice developing a loving heart.

**Explanation:** *(Patience Practice)* People's wishes do not agree because of the afflictions. If we do not get angry about this, our own mind finds peace. If we meditate on love for others, people become harmonious with one another. This makes the good quality of patience grow in our mind.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

One makes oneself utterly certain, free of doubt, about what is to be adopted; one has deep faith, that is, the faith of confidence and the faith of aspiration, in that meaning; one is steady in thought, practice, and application; one is accompanied by respect and by the courtesy of bowing; one is accompanied by fear on account of conscience and a sense of propriety (ངོ་ཚ་ཁྲེལ་ཡོད) — that is, the fear regarding what is to be adopted (བླང་བྱའི་འཇིགས་པ), as the text has it; and one has the calm of restraining the senses, and must strive at such things as bringing joy to others by means of the four ways of gathering disciples (བསྡུ་བ་བཞི). And even though one has, from one's own side, the strength of mind that wishes to strive at such things, there is the danger of meeting bad friends. The sutra says: "A mad elephant may destroy the body, but it does not destroy the mind; an unvirtuous friend destroys the mind, so one must be careful."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-55]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whatever Dharma one practices with the three doors of body, speech, and mind — in retreat and the like — it will not do to practice it as a mere show for others; so one must practice it endowed with these nine factors for doing virtue. What are they? Whatever Dharma one practices, one is skilled in the meaning of its preparation, main part, and conclusion, and through having no doubt is utterly certain (firm conviction). One has confidence in its meaning, and so has deep faith. One keeps firm the pledge one has made to accomplish it, and does not give it up under the sway of conditions such as lack of food or illness (steadfastness). One is respectful of, and glad in, accomplishing it. One shows courtesy, with body and speech, to those worthy of homage such as one's preceptor, teacher, and lama. From one's own side one has conscience; from the side of others — lamas, buddhas, bodhisattvas, and so on — one is accompanied by dread, or fear. And one is calm: in general one must restrain the doors of the five senses, and in particular the sense doors of the three — eye, ear, and mind — for if they are not restrained, attachment, hatred, and the like will arise. Perhaps one thinks, "My attachment, hatred, and distraction are so strong that I cannot restrain the doors of the senses." But one must not belittle oneself and make oneself one's own ruin. It is not that those who restrained the doors of their senses and received the supreme prophecy were free of attachment and hatred from the very beginning. For example, there were Nanda (གཅུང་མཛེས་དགའ), who had strong attachment, Subhūti (རབ་འབྱོར), who had strong hatred, and Cūḍapanthaka (ལམ་ཆུང་པ), who had strong ignorance, and others like them; so strive at diligence. And such conduct of calm and taming must also be pursued solely for the sake of bringing joy to others: since bodhisattvas are a formidable object (ཡུལ་གཉན་པོ), one must guard against others' lack of faith.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-55]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

How should the mind be guarded? By abandoning doubt and wrong understanding about the things to be trained in, one is utterly certain. One has confidence in the Three Jewels and in the things to be trained in; through faith and aspiration one has deep faith (རབ་ཏུ་དང་བ). One is steadfast in intention and application. One is accompanied by respect and by courtesy, which includes bowing to everyone. Taking oneself as the reason, one shrinks from faults and so has conscience; reflecting on their drawbacks, one is accompanied by fear of the ripening of results. The senses are calm, and one strives at bringing joy to others as one's deliberate aim.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Firm conviction: constantly pledging oneself to virtue. Deep faith: being glad on seeing its good qualities. Steadfastness: not turning back even when it is hard. Respect: the mind bowing in reverence. Courtesy (ཞེ་ས): Vairocana (རྣམ་སྣང) explains that this is the body and so on bowing. Conscience: shrinking from misconduct. Fear: being accompanied by fear of faults. Calm: because the senses are restrained. And striving to bring joy to other beings; and —

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Firm conviction through having no doubt about what to adopt and what to abandon. Deep faith: entering into it with constant joy and delight. Steadfastness: not tiring of hardship. Respect toward those worthy of homage, and courtesy toward those worthy of reverence. Conscience: with oneself as the reference point, shrinking from misconduct. A sense of propriety (ཁྲེལ་ཡོད་པ): shrinking from misconduct out of fear of others' criticism. Calm and tamed, through restraining the doors of the senses. And one should strive to bring others joy and satisfaction through the four ways of gathering disciples (བསྡུ་བའི་དངོས་པོ་བཞི).

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One should endow oneself with the conditions conducive to accomplishing others' welfare. Firm conviction: abandoning doubt and wrong understanding about the trainings of engaging in and turning away from things, and finding certainty. Deep faith: through the faith of aspiration and confidence, seeing the qualities of those who have qualities, such as the Three Jewels; or else, entering with joy and delight into the things to be trained in, and so having deep faith, that is, being "clearly serene" (རབ་ཏུ་དང་བ). Steadfastness: being afraid of the difficult trainings and of working for others' welfare, yet not turning back from the start, and so having a firm intention and application. Respect: as explained before, respect from the heart for those with qualities and for the trainings. Courtesy: the body and mind bowing close to everyone, high and low; or else, as Vairocana (རྣམ་སྣང) says, the mind's bowing is respect and the body's bowing is courtesy. Conscience: taking as the reason that one holds the trainings and so on, one shrinks from misconduct. A sense of propriety (ཁྲེལ་ཡོད་པ): taking as the reason one's fear of the ripening of results should one enter misconduct, or one's fear of being made sport of before others, one shrinks from misconduct. Calm and tamed: even when conditions for restlessness and the like are near, restraining the senses from engaging with wrong objects. And striving to bring others joy and satisfaction by various means such as giving and kind words. Having endowed oneself with such qualities, one should engage in others' welfare and so on.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-55]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

There are nine factors that support accomplishing virtue. First, because one has no doubts such as "If I accomplish this virtue I wish to accomplish, will it help or not?", there is firm conviction. Second, through confidence in that virtue one wishes to accomplish, one has deep faith, that is, faith. Third, steadfastness: having pledged to accomplish that virtue properly, one does not show laziness or procrastination until it is completed. Fourth, in the same way, one's mind is utterly glad and respectful toward the virtue to be accomplished and toward the lamas and others who teach it. Fifth, one's mind is accompanied by courtesy toward those worthy of offerings or reverence — one's own lama, preceptor, and teacher, virtuous spiritual friends, and the Three Jewels. Sixth, conscience: because one is a Dharma practitioner, or because one holds the trainings in one's mind-stream, one does not engage in misconduct. Seventh, a sense of propriety, accompanied by fear: looking to the faces of others, such as other preceptors and teachers, or Dharma friends and patrons, one does not engage in misconduct oneself. Eighth, the senses are utterly calm and tamed in appearance. And ninth, "joy for others" means striving again and again to bring joy to the minds of those who are fit to be disciplined; that is, striving to satisfy the thoughts of those who are fit to be trained. Thus the meaning is that all nine of these factors must be complete for accomplishing virtue.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Nine Factors for Accomplishing Virtue Are Needed Together

This verse sets out that when one engages in the welfare of others, one must accomplish virtue with body, speech, and mind while endowed with nine supporting factors — firm conviction, deep faith, steadfastness, respect, courtesy, conscience, fear (a sense of propriety), calm, and bringing joy to others. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains them as a fixed number.

###### 2. Do Not Belittle Yourself: Even Nanda Tamed His Afflictions Through Diligence

In his instruction on restraining the doors of the senses, Khenpo Kunzang Pelden says that those who received the prophecy did not do so by being free of afflictions from the start. He illustrates this with the example of Nanda (གཅུང་མཛེས་དགའ) and others, and explains that one must tame the afflictions through diligence while still having them.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Gives Two Understandings of Each Factor

Minyak Kunzang Sonam divides the understanding of each of the nine factors into two (for example, the two understandings of firm conviction: finding certainty about what is to be trained in, or entering into the trainings with joy), setting down differing ways of explaining them by Vairocana (རྣམ་སྣང).

###### 4. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Warns Against Bad Friends While Striving

While one is striving at the nine factors, His Holiness separately sets down the advice to be careful of the danger of meeting bad friends.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows that for a bodhisattva to bring a virtuous deed to completion, one must be endowed with nine indispensable qualities: being certain about what to adopt and abandon, faith, steadfastness, respect and courtesy, and — through conscience and fear — calm senses, together with striving to bring joy to the minds of others.

**Key points.**

- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains the nine ways of accomplishing virtue in this verse, matching them clearly, one after another, to the verse's words.
- ⚑ There are differing ways of explaining the meaning of respect and courtesy. Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives two explanations by Vairocana (རྣམ་སྣང): respect for qualities and the trainings, with courtesy toward high and low; or else respect as the bowing of the mind and courtesy as the bowing of the body. Ngulchu Thogme likewise explains, by Vairocana, that respect is the mind bowing and courtesy the body and so on bowing.
- On bringing joy to others: Sabzang Mati Panchen explains that one strives to satisfy beings' minds through the four ways of gathering disciples, while Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains it as through various means such as giving and kind words.
- Khenpo Kunzang Pelden says that, like Nanda, Subhūti, and Cūḍapanthaka, one must not belittle oneself by saying "I cannot"; and, since bodhisattvas are a formidable object (ཡུལ་གཉན་པོ), one must strive at the conduct of calm in order to guard others' faith.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama, citing a sutra, says that while striving at virtue one must be careful, because an unvirtuous friend destroys the mind.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, because of those of small mind, the mutually conflicting condition of the childish, and the displeasing behavior that comes out of great desire — one's own mind is not discouraged, and one must be one who does not come under their power. And since these childish beings, under the power of the afflictions, have shown this behavior without self-control, one must not be angry or displeased with them, but must have especially great compassion; and —

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The desires of ordinary childish beings, who abide in mutual conflict — some pleased, some angry — are diverse. So when one practices bodhisattva conduct, some are glad and praise one and so on, while some are displeased and disparage one and so on; by these one should not be discouraged. For if one were discouraged, it would become an obstacle to bodhicitta, like the earlier story of Śāriputra and the pratyekabuddha. And not only should one not be discouraged: because their displeasure depends on afflictions such as attachment and hatred arising through beings' varied inclinations — or because these childish ones have come under the power of those afflictions — one thinks, "This kind of state of mind, displeased with bodhisattva conduct, with practicing Dharma, and so on, has arisen in them without self-control"; and bodhisattvas hold especially tender compassion for those unruly beings and become endowed with great compassion; and —

*(Editorial note: Khenpo Kunzang Pelden alludes briefly to a story involving Śāriputra and a pratyekabuddha as an example of this danger, without recounting it in full.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-56]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Through the conduct of the childish, in which, if one benefits one person, another becomes resentful — beings mutually in conflict — one is not discouraged by their contradictory wishes. And one is endowed with compassion, thinking, "Because afflictions have arisen in them, they are without self-control, and this mind of theirs has come about; may I free them from the afflictions." And one should not come under the power of the afflictions oneself.

> From the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*: "A wise person does not hold at fault a person whose mind is constantly made powerless by faults."

And so on; and as the *Catuḥśataka* says: "Just as a physician is not upset even by the anger of someone seized by a spirit, so the Sage sees the afflictions, and does not see the person as bound up with the afflictions."

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

One is not discouraged by the mutually conflicting desires of the childish; and even when they do one harm, one thinks, "Because afflictions have arisen in them, this harmful thought has come about in them without self-control," and so one should be endowed with especially great tender compassion for them, as the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* says:

> From the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*: "A wise person does not hold at fault a person whose mind is constantly made powerless by faults; [for such a person] comes to act wrongly even against his wishes — because compassion toward such a person increases all the more."

So it is said.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If one uses means to bring about the joy and satisfaction of one person, such as benefiting him, it can become, for the others, a cause of displeasure and anger and of piling up wrongdoing. Since they are childish beings whose thoughts conflict with one another and whose wishes are varied and manifold, one's mind is not discouraged by the very difficulty of accomplishing the means to please everyone. And those childish ones, having come under the power of the various arisen afflictions, are certain to have had these conflicting states of mind of theirs arise without self-control. Therefore, thinking, "May I free them from these faults of affliction that are the cause of their conflicting wishes," one should hold them, again and again, with especially great compassion, giving them advice and instruction; and one should also take the mind in hand, thinking, "May I too not come under the power of such afflictions."

> From the "Gang-bos zhus-pa" (source title as given): "If he sees beings' wrongdoing, he admonishes them in due time; he is always endowed with love and compassion, and is without resentment or anger."

So it is said; and from the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*:

> "A wise person does not hold at fault a person whose mind is constantly made powerless by faults; [for such a person] comes to act wrongly even against his wishes — because compassion toward such a person increases all the more."

So it is said; and also from the *Conduct Condensed into Instruction* (སྤྱོད་པ་མདོ་ཙམ་གདམས་ངག་ཏུ་བྱས་པ):

> "Because beings' intentions differ, examine and do what is of great purpose. Who could please everyone? Even so, being without regret, [one] does not cling to bliss, having [dealt with] the distinctions of suffering. Train your own mind and take hold of others' minds; when contempt, faults, and the like arise, train through the antidote, through action, and through falsehood."

*(Editorial note: two lines of this third quotation are obscure in the rail's Tibetan, namely སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཁྱད་བསད་བདེར་མ་ཆགས and གཉེན་པོ་ལས་དང་བརྫུན་པས་སྦྱངས; the English rendering of them is tentative.)*

So it is said.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Do Not Be Discouraged by Conflicting Wishes: See Them as Born of Afflictions and Respond With Compassion

This verse explains that even when beings' wishes conflict so that one cannot please everyone, one's mind is not discouraged; and, understanding that their conflicting behavior has come about without self-control through the power of the afflictions, one must develop especially great compassion.

###### 2. Even the Buddha Could Not Please Sunakṣatra and Devadatta

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk gives the example that even the Teacher Shākyamuni was unable to please some, such as Sunakṣatra and Devadatta, and advises that ordinary beings, who cannot please everyone, should not let their spirits fall.

###### 3. A Sūtrālaṃkāra Verse: The Wise Do Not Hold Faults Against Those Made Powerless by Them

A verse of the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* shows that a wise person does not hold at fault beings who have been made powerless by faults; Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Ngulchu Thogme, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam all specifically set down this passage.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Joins Three Passages on Love and Compassion Without Anger

Minyak Kunzang Sonam joins three scriptural passages — an unnamed sutra ("Gang-bos zhus-pa"), the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*, and *Conduct Condensed into Instruction* — and sets down at length the advice that one must generate love and compassion toward beings, without anger.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The wishes of ordinary childish beings conflict with one another, and they have fallen under the power of the afflictions; so one should not be discouraged, and, without coming under the sway of anger through others' conflicting behavior, one should generate especially great compassion for those beings.

**Key points.**

- Knowing that childish beings cannot all be pleased, because their intentions differ, one should not be discouraged.
- Even the Buddha, the Bhagavān, could not please Devadatta and Sunakṣatra; so even when conflict arises within one's retinue, one should not lose heart.
- Because beings act in conflict without self-control through the power of the afflictions, one should generate especially great compassion, as a physician is not angry at a patient seized by a spirit.
- One should not oneself fall under the power of the afflictions through others' childish behavior, so that no obstacle arises to bodhicitta.
