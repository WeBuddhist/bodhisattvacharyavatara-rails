## 8. Chapter Eight: Teaching Meditative Concentration ^8-0


Having built up joyful effort like this,
I should now settle my mind in meditation.
A person whose mind is scattered and distracted
stays caught between the sharp teeth of disturbing emotions. ^8-1


If my body and mind are kept in solitude,
no distraction can reach me.
So I should let go of worldly busyness
and set aside restless, wandering thoughts completely. ^8-2


People do not let go of worldly life
because of attachment and craving for things like wealth.
So, to truly let all of this go,
a wise person should reflect in this way: ^8-3


Understanding that insight, joined with calm steadiness of mind,
is what truly overcomes disturbing emotions,
I should first seek this calm steadiness,
which comes about through genuine joy, free from attachment to worldly things. ^8-4


Everything is impermanent — so who would ever
become truly, deeply attached to something impermanent?
By doing so, across thousands of lifetimes,
one may never see the person one loves again. ^8-5


Without seeing that person, there is no joy,
and the mind cannot settle in peace.
And even when I do see them, I am never satisfied,
so I am tormented by craving just as before. ^8-6


Becoming attached to other beings
blocks me from seeing the true nature of things clearly.
It ruins my healthy sense of wanting to be free of endless rebirth,
and in the end, it only brings grief and sorrow. ^8-7


By thinking only of that person,
this precious life slips away for nothing.
And because of unreliable friends, always changing,
even the eternal, true teachings can fade away too. ^8-8


Following the same habits as immature people
leads, without doubt, to a hard rebirth.
And being led toward things that are not truly good for me —
what is the use of associating with immature people at all? ^8-9


In one moment we may be friends,
and in the very next moment, enemies.
Since they become angry even at things that should bring joy,
it is very hard to please an ordinary, immature person. ^8-10


Even when spoken to kindly, for their own benefit,
they become angry, and turn away from that benefit themselves.
And if I do not listen to what they say,
they become angry, and this leads them toward a hard rebirth. ^8-11


They feel jealous of those above them, competitive with equals,
proud toward those below, and arrogant when praised.
They grow angry when criticized —
when would any real benefit ever come from an immature person? ^8-12


Spending time with immature people
inevitably leads to things like praising myself, criticizing others,
and talking about the pleasures of worldly life —
something unhelpful is bound to come of it. ^8-13


So relying on others in this way,
in the end, only brings harm to both sides.
They gain nothing real from me,
and I gain nothing real from them either. ^8-14


I should stay at a good distance from immature company.
But if I do meet them, I should be warm and pleasant,
without becoming too closely attached,
keeping things friendly, but simple and ordinary. ^8-15


Like a bee gathering only nectar from a flower,
I should take only what is truly useful from any encounter,
and then, as if I had never met that person before,
stay free from any deep attachment. ^8-16


If I think, "I have so much wealth and honor,
and so many people admire me,"
holding onto that kind of pride
will only bring fear after I die. ^8-17


So, whatever a confused mind
becomes attached to,
that very thing, multiplied a thousandfold,
turns into suffering and rises up against me. ^8-18


So a wise person should not become attached;
fear arises precisely from attachment.
Since all these things are, by their nature, bound to be lost anyway,
I should understand this deeply and stay firm. ^8-19


Even if I gain a great deal of wealth,
and even great fame and a good reputation,
none of that wealth or fame
can go with me to wherever I go next. ^8-20


If someone else criticizes me,
what real joy would praise bring me?
And if someone else praises me,
what real harm would criticism do me? ^8-21


Since beings have such varied wishes
that even the Buddhas cannot please everyone,
how could someone as ordinary as me even hope to?
So I should let go of worrying about what the world thinks of me. ^8-22


People criticize beings who have nothing,
and speak unkindly of those who have plenty.
Since it is so hard, by nature, to please people like this,
how could real joy ever come from trying? ^8-23


Because immature people
never find real joy without gaining something for themselves,
the Tathāgatas themselves have taught
that no immature person can truly be a reliable friend. ^8-24


The deer, birds, and trees living in the forest
never speak unkindly.
When will I finally get to live
together with such peaceful, easy companions? ^8-25


Living in a cave, an empty temple,
or beneath a tree,
when will I finally be able to move on
without ever looking back, free of any attachment? ^8-26


Living freely in wide open, natural places
that belong to no one,
acting with real freedom, without attachment —
when will I finally live this way? ^8-27


Carrying only a simple begging bowl and the like,
wearing clothes that no one else could want,
without even needing to hide or protect this body —
when will I finally live this way, without fear? ^8-28


Going to a charnel ground and seeing there
the bones of others alongside what my own body will one day become,
both equally subject to decay and impermanence —
when will I finally understand this clearly, treating them the same? ^8-29


Even this body of mine
will one day smell so unpleasant
that not even jackals will want
to come anywhere near it. ^8-30


Even this single body of mine,
though born as one whole thing,
will eventually break apart,
its flesh and bones scattering in every direction —
so what need is there to even mention other, external attachments? ^8-31


We are born alone,
and we die alone, all by ourselves.
Since no one else can share in our suffering for us,
what real use are attached friends and companions who only get in the way? ^8-32


Just as a traveler on a journey
takes shelter along the road,
in the same way, someone traveling the road of endless rebirth
takes shelter, for a time, in each new birth. ^8-33


Until four people carry my body away
while the whole world mourns around me,
until that moment comes,
I should go and live quietly in the forest. ^8-34


Without close friends and without enemies,
I should live alone in a peaceful, solitary place,
already thinking of myself as if already gone,
so that even at the moment of actual death, there is nothing left to grieve. ^8-35


With no one nearby
to cause grief or disturbance,
nothing will distract me
from remembering the Buddha and staying focused on the practice. ^8-36


So I should live alone,
in a joyful, easy, peaceful forest,
requiring very little,
where every kind of distraction is naturally calmed. ^8-37


Letting go of every other concern,
and focusing my mind on this one purpose alone,
I should work hard
to settle my mind and bring it under control. ^8-38


Ordinary desires cause real trouble,
both in this life and in future ones.
In this life, they lead to being killed, bound, or wounded;
in future lives, they lead to hell and other hard rebirths. ^8-39


For the sake of desire, people have gone through countless
go-betweens and messengers in the past,
sparing no effort for the sake of wrongdoing or scandal,
holding nothing back at all. ^8-40


For the sake of desire, people have thrown themselves into real danger,
have spent their entire wealth,
and yet the very things they cling to so tightly
are what bring them, in the end, only the greatest suffering. ^8-41


These bodies are, in truth, nothing more than skeletons,
not truly under anyone's own control, and not truly a "self" at all.
Craving and clinging to them so strongly,
why do people not simply seek real peace instead? ^8-42


Someone who at first works hard to lift their head high,
even when finally raised up, still looks down out of shyness.
Whether seen by others before or not,
they end up covering their face with cloth out of embarrassment. ^8-43


Your face, disturbed by emotion,
is exposed right now, just as clearly.
Seeing it uncovered, even by something as ordinary as a vulture,
why would you suddenly want to flee in fear? ^8-44


If someone protects even food that others simply glance at with their eyes
as if it were being eaten away by that gaze,
then you, so miserly and possessive,
why would you not protect your body in the same way? ^8-45


Seeing this pile of flesh
being eaten by vultures and other creatures,
you decorate someone else's meal
with garlands of flowers and sandalwood ornaments. ^8-46


If you are so frightened, even by the sight of
a lifeless body that cannot move at all,
why are you not equally afraid
when something moves it around, as if it were a zombie? ^8-47


From the very same food,
both waste and saliva are produced.
Yet you feel disgust at the waste,
so why do you feel no disgust at the saliva? ^8-48


Someone caught up in desire, confused about what is truly unclean,
takes no delight in a pillow
that feels soft to the touch, just because
it does not happen to give off a bad smell. ^8-49


If you are so attached to something
only when it is covered up, why would you not want it uncovered instead?
And if there is truly no real need for it at all,
why do you cling to it, covered or not? ^8-50


If you truly feel no attachment to something unclean,
then why do you embrace, in your very arms,
another person — a cage of bones tied together with sinew,
covered over with a layer of muddy flesh? ^8-51


You yourself are full of so many unclean things,
and you constantly make use of them yourself.
And yet, driven by craving, you also desire
other bags of unclean things, belonging to someone else. ^8-52


If you say, "I enjoy this flesh,"
and want to touch it and look at it,
then how can you possibly desire
flesh that has, by its very nature, no mind or feeling of its own? ^8-53


The mind that feels desire
cannot itself be touched or seen,
and whatever can be touched or seen
has no awareness of its own — so what real point is there in this pointless embracing? ^8-54


It is not so surprising that people fail to see
the unclean nature of someone else's body.
But it truly is surprising
that people fail to recognize this same unclean nature in their very own bodies. ^8-55


Why would a mind attached to unclean things
turn away from a fresh, young lotus flower,
opened wide by rays of sunlight on a cloudless day,
and instead delight in a mere bag of unclean things? ^8-56


If you do not even want to touch
ground that has become soiled and unclean,
then how can you possibly want to touch
a body that arose from that very same kind of source? ^8-57


If you truly feel no attachment to unclean things,
then why do you embrace, in your arms, another person,
who arose from an unclean field
and was grown from that very seed? ^8-58


You would not even want a small worm,
born from something unclean,
and yet you desire a whole body
made mostly of unclean things, and itself born from something unclean. ^8-59


Not only do you fail to feel disgust
at your own uncleanliness,
but, driven by craving,
you also desire other bags of unclean things belonging to others. ^8-60


Even pleasant things like camphor,
rice, or vegetables,
once put in the mouth and then spat back out,
make even the ground unclean and disgusting. ^8-61


If you still have doubts, even seeing
uncleanliness this clearly with your own eyes,
go and look at other unclean bodies
that have been left behind in a charnel ground. ^8-62


Knowing how frightening it is
when the skin is peeled away and what lies beneath is revealed,
how can you still, knowing all of this,
feel any renewed desire for it? ^8-63


Even the pleasant scent applied to a body
comes from sandalwood or another perfume — nothing else.
So why would you feel attracted to another person
because of a scent that actually comes from somewhere else entirely? ^8-64


If it is truly good not to feel desire for the body
because of its natural, underlying unpleasant smell,
then why do people in this world uselessly burn
sweet-smelling incense over it instead? ^8-65


And if that sweet-smelling sandalwood scent
actually belongs to the body, what is it doing there at all?
Why would you feel attracted to another person
because of a scent that actually comes from somewhere else entirely? ^8-66


If the body's true nature —
long hair, long nails, yellowed teeth, and other such unpleasant things,
along with all its various smells —
is, when left bare, actually something frightening, ^8-67


then why do you work so hard
to groom and decorate it, like polishing a weapon that could harm you?
Confused about your own body,
this whole world is thrown into such needless turmoil because of this kind of effort. ^8-68


If merely seeing a bare skeleton
in a charnel ground is enough to make someone feel disturbed and want to turn away,
then how could anyone delight
in a city full of moving skeletons, walking all around? ^8-69


Something so unclean as this
is never gained without a price.
Working so hard to obtain it
only leads to exhaustion, and even to suffering in hell. ^8-70


A child cannot yet build wealth,
so how happy it would be to pass straight from childhood to old age!
But if life is measured only by gathering wealth and possessions,
what use is desire, once old age finally arrives? ^8-71


Some poor souls caught up in desire
wear themselves out completely with a full day's work,
and come home at night so exhausted
that they collapse into sleep like a lifeless body. ^8-72


Some suffer the hardship of long journeys away from home,
enduring great difficulty and separation,
never getting to see their own wives and children,
even though that is exactly what they wanted and worked so hard for. ^8-73


Confused about what will actually benefit them,
some even sell themselves for the sake of wealth,
yet never actually gain what they were hoping for,
while being tossed about, uselessly, by the winds of others' demands. ^8-74


Some sell their own bodies,
losing their freedom, controlled entirely by someone else.
Even their wives, when it comes time to give birth,
are left to do so under a tree, in some desolate place. ^8-75


Fools, deceived by desire,
thinking, "I must make a living to survive,"
throw themselves into battle, risking their very lives,
or become servants to others just for the sake of profit. ^8-76


Some people caught up in desire even have their bodies cut apart;
some are impaled on sharpened stakes;
some are pierced through with spears;
and some are even burned alive. ^8-77


Understanding the endless troubles that come from gathering wealth,
guarding it, and then losing it,
I should understand that those distracted by attachment to wealth
never find any real chance to be free from the suffering of endless rebirth. ^8-78


For those caught up in desire, the drawbacks are many
and the rewards are small,
like a beast pulling a heavy cart
who gets only a mouthful of grass now and then as its reward. ^8-79


And even that small reward
is not hard to come by even through ordinary labor.
Yet driven by these desires, people destroy
this rare and precious opportunity of a full and free human life. ^8-80


Ordinary desires are certain to fall apart in the end,
and they lead people down into hell and other hard rebirths.
Since the reward is never truly great,
all the endless hardship and exhaustion involved is, in the end, wasted. ^8-81


But with only a fraction of that same effort,
a person could actually achieve full Buddhahood.
So compared to the path of the bodhisattva,
someone caught up in desire suffers far more, for far less true awakening. ^8-82


Thinking carefully about the sufferings of hell and other hard rebirths,
I realize that even weapons, poison, fire, deep chasms,
and dangerous enemies
are nothing compared to the true dangers of ordinary desires. ^8-83


So, feeling this healthy sense of weariness toward desire,
I should instead find real delight in solitude,
in peaceful forests
free from conflict and disturbing emotions. ^8-84


In a spacious, delightful stone dwelling,
cooled by moonlight and sandalwood,
in the quiet of a peaceful forest, swayed gently by a silent breeze,
a fortunate person walks about, thinking only of how to help others. ^8-85


Living for as long as I wish
in an empty house, beneath a tree, or in a cave,
I should let go of the burden of protecting and holding onto possessions,
and live freely, without worry or attachment to anyone. ^8-86


Living freely, without attachment,
connected to nothing in particular,
content and at ease —
even someone as powerful as a king would find this hard to achieve. ^8-87


Reflecting in these and other such ways
on the many benefits of solitude,
I should calm all restless, wandering thoughts,
and meditate on bodhicitta. ^8-88


First, I should work hard to meditate
on treating myself and others as equal.
Since happiness and suffering feel the same to everyone,
I should protect all beings, just as I would protect myself. ^8-89


Just as the hand and other body parts,
though many and different, are all treated as one single body to be cared for,
in the same way, all different beings, in their happiness and suffering,
share the very same wish to be happy — so all should be seen as one. ^8-90


Even though my own suffering
does not actually harm anyone else's body,
that suffering is still hard for me to bear,
simply because I cling so strongly to myself as "me." ^8-91


In the same way, even though another being's suffering
does not actually happen to me directly,
that same clinging to myself as "me"
makes it hard for me to feel and accept their suffering too. ^8-92


So I should work to clear away the suffering of others,
simply because it is suffering, just like my own.
And I should work to help other beings,
simply because they are living beings, just like my own body is part of me. ^8-93


Since both myself and others
equally want happiness,
what real difference is there between us,
that I should work so hard only for my own happiness? ^8-94


And since both myself and others
equally do not want suffering,
what real difference is there between us,
that I should protect only myself and not others too? ^8-95


Someone might say, "I do not need to protect others from suffering,
because their suffering does not actually harm me."
But then, suffering in the future does not harm me right now either —
so why do I protect myself from that future suffering? ^8-96


The thought, "I am the one who will experience that future suffering,"
is actually mistaken.
Because, in truth, the one who dies
is different from the one who is later born. ^8-97


If everyone should only look after
whatever suffering belongs to them,
then, since the foot's pain is not the hand's pain,
why would the hand ever help the foot at all? ^8-98


Someone might say, "Even if this reasoning isn't perfect,
it still makes sense because of clinging to a sense of self."
But then I should recognize that both self-clinging and other-clinging
are equally mistaken, and simply let go of both as much as I possibly can. ^8-99


What we call a "person" or a "group"
is, like a string of beads or an army, not truly a single real thing, but a label.
Since there is no single, real owner of suffering,
who exactly does that suffering actually belong to? ^8-100


Since suffering has no true single owner,
there is really no fundamental difference between anyone's suffering.
It should simply be cleared away, because it is suffering, plain and simple —
what use is there in debating over whose suffering it "really" is? ^8-101


So there is no need to argue about
whether the suffering of all beings should be cleared away.
If it should be cleared away at all, then all of it should be cleared away;
and if not, then that includes my own suffering too, since I am simply one being among others. ^8-102


Someone might ask, "Since compassion brings so much suffering with it,
why deliberately cultivate it at all?"
But when I think carefully about the vast suffering of all beings,
how could the modest suffering that comes with compassion possibly compare? ^8-103


If the suffering of compassion could actually end
the far greater suffering of countless beings,
then someone with real love and care
should welcome that suffering, both for themselves and for others. ^8-104


That is why the wise and beautiful Candrapradīpa,
even knowing that it would bring him harm from the king,
did not shy away from his own suffering,
because it would bring an end to the suffering of so many others. ^8-105


In this way, someone whose mind has become truly accustomed
to finding joy in calming the suffering of others,
will, like a swan diving into a lotus pond,
willingly enter even the deepest hell to help beings there. ^8-106


When beings are finally set free,
there is an ocean of joy in that alone.
Is that not already enough?
What more could someone seeking liberation possibly want? ^8-107


So even though I work for the benefit of others,
there is no need for pride or amazement in that.
Since I take joy solely in helping others,
I have no need to hope for any reward in return. ^8-108


So, just as I naturally protect myself
even from small, unkind words,
in that very same way, I should build within myself
a mind that protects others, and a heart full of compassion. ^8-109


Through habit, people come to think of
even a mere drop of another's blood and sperm
as "me" and "mine,"
even though there is truly no fixed self there at all. ^8-110


In that same way, why should I not
also come to think of another person's body as "myself"?
It should not, in truth, be any harder
to think of my own body as belonging to others. ^8-111


Understanding clearly that I myself am full of faults,
while others are like an ocean of good qualities,
I should meditate on letting go completely of clinging to myself,
and on taking up the care of others instead. ^8-112


Just as the hand and other such parts
are naturally considered limbs of the body,
why should living beings not, in the same way,
be considered like limbs of the whole world? ^8-113


Just as, through long habit,
a sense of "self" arose regarding this body, which has no true self at all,
why, through that same kind of habit,
should a sense of "self" not also arise regarding other beings? ^8-114


If I truly think this way,
even helping others brings no pride or amazement,
just as I do not expect thanks
simply for feeding myself. ^8-115


So, just as I naturally protect myself
even from small, unkind words,
in that same way, I should build the habit
of protecting others, and cultivate real compassion. ^8-116


This is why the protector Avalokiteśvara,
moved by great compassion,
even blessed his own name
in order to clear away the fears of all beings. ^8-117


I should never turn back from this difficult practice,
because through the sheer power of habit,
even things whose very name once caused fear
can come to feel unwelcome only in their absence. ^8-118


Whoever truly wishes to quickly protect
both themselves and others
should practice this sacred and profound method:
exchanging themselves for others. ^8-119


Because of clinging so strongly to this body of mine,
even a small danger fills me with great fear.
So who would not feel a kind of natural aversion,
as if toward an enemy, toward this very body that gives rise to so much fear? ^8-120


Wishing to cure their body of hunger, thirst, and other such ailments,
people kill birds, fish, and deer,
and lie in wait for them along their paths. ^8-121


Some people even kill their own parents
for the sake of profit or status,
or steal offerings meant for the Three Jewels —
and because of this, they end up burning in the deepest hell. ^8-122


So what wise person would ever
want to protect and cherish this body so much,
rather than looking at it
with the same suspicion one might show toward an enemy, or even scorn it? ^8-123


"If I give this away, what will I have left to use myself?"
Thinking this way, out of self-concern, is the way of a hungry ghost.
"If I use this myself, what will I have left to give?"
Thinking this way, caring for others, is the way of the gods. ^8-124


If I harm others for my own sake,
I will suffer in hell and other hard rebirths because of it.
But if I accept harm to myself for the sake of others,
I will gain every kind of true excellence instead. ^8-125


Wanting to raise myself above others
leads to a bad rebirth, foolishness, and a low position.
But turning that very same wish toward others instead
leads to a good rebirth and real respect. ^8-126


If I make others work for my own sake,
I will experience being made to serve others in turn.
But if I work hard for the sake of others,
I will experience being honored as a true leader instead. ^8-127


However much happiness there is in this world,
all of it comes from wanting others to be happy.
However much suffering there is in this world,
all of it comes from wanting only one's own happiness. ^8-128


What need is there to say more?
Just look at the real difference
between the immature person, who works only for their own benefit,
and the Buddha, who works for the benefit of others. ^8-129


If I do not truly and completely exchange
my own happiness for others' suffering,
I will never achieve Buddhahood,
and I will find no real happiness even within ordinary life. ^8-130


Setting aside future lives entirely,
even in this very life,
someone who fails to work for others, or fails to reward those who serve them well,
will not achieve their own worldly goals either. ^8-131


Giving up the true, complete happiness
that comes from working for both seen and unseen benefit,
and instead causing suffering to others,
a confused person takes on unbearable suffering for themselves instead. ^8-132


Whatever harm, fear, and suffering
exists anywhere in this world,
all of it comes from clinging to a sense of self.
So what real use is this great troublemaker to me at all? ^8-133


If I do not completely let go of this clinging to self,
I cannot possibly let go of suffering,
just as someone who does not let go of fire
cannot avoid being burned by it. ^8-134


So, in order to calm the harm done to myself
and to calm the suffering of others,
I should give myself over entirely to others,
and cherish others just as I would cherish myself. ^8-135


My mind, you must understand clearly:
"I now belong to others."
From now on, you should think of nothing else at all
except the benefit of every being. ^8-136


It would not make sense for my eyes and other senses,
which exist for the benefit of others,
to be used only to serve myself.
It would not be right to turn them against that very purpose. ^8-137


So, treating other beings as what matters most,
whatever I notice in this body of mine that could be useful,
I should take it, whatever it may be,
and use it entirely for the benefit of others. ^8-138


Placing myself in the position of someone lower,
and placing others in the position that used to be "myself,"
with a mind free of confused thinking,
I should meditate honestly on jealousy, competitiveness, and pride, as if from the other side. ^8-139


"This one is honored, and I am not.
I do not have wealth like this one has.
This one is praised, while I am criticized.
This one is happy, while I suffer." ^8-140


"I am the one who does all the hard work,
while this one simply enjoys comfort.
This one is considered great in the world,
while I am said to have no good qualities at all." ^8-141


But what real difference does having no good qualities make?
I truly do have good qualities of my own.
Compared to some, this one may seem lesser;
compared to others, I myself may seem greater. ^8-142


It is not truly within my control
whether my discipline or understanding declines, since disturbing emotions are so powerful.
I should do everything I can to heal myself of this,
and I should be willing to accept even harm done to me along the way. ^8-143


But if someone else refuses to help heal me in this way,
why would they look down on me for it?
And even if they have good qualities I lack,
what real use are those qualities to me, if not shared? ^8-144


Standing at the edge of a terrible, hard rebirth,
feeling no compassion for suffering beings,
yet still boasting outwardly about their own supposed good qualities,
such a person wants to rival even the truly wise. ^8-145


Comparing myself to others,
seeking only to raise myself above them,
I fight, argue, and struggle
to gain wealth and honor for myself alone. ^8-146


"How can I make my good qualities
known and admired by the whole world,
while making sure that no one
ever hears about anyone else's good qualities?" ^8-147


"I will hide my own faults completely,
and I alone will be honored, not this other person.
I will gain good things for myself,
and I alone will be respected, not this other person." ^8-148


"I will take real, lasting delight
in seeing something bad happen to this other person.
I will make them a laughingstock
in front of the whole world, mocked and shamed by everyone." ^8-149


"They say this person wants to compete
even with someone like me.
But do they really match me
in learning, wisdom, appearance, family, or wealth?" ^8-150


Hearing my own good qualities
praised and celebrated by everyone,
I hope to feel a thrill of real joy,
and to fully enjoy that kind of happiness. ^8-151


"Even if this person has some wealth,
if they end up working for me,
I will give them just enough to live on,
and take the rest for myself, by whatever means I can." ^8-152


"I will make sure this person loses their comfort,
and I will constantly arrange to cause them harm.
This is the very person who, in life after life,
across hundreds of lifetimes, has caused me harm." ^8-153


My mind, wanting only to serve your own interests,
even after countless ages have passed,
all that great effort of yours
has brought about nothing but suffering, again and again. ^8-154


So now, truly and completely,
devote yourself instead to the benefit of others.
The Buddha's words are never false —
you will come to see the real value of this for yourself later. ^8-155


If, from the very beginning,
you had put this same effort into helping others,
you certainly would not still find yourself
stuck in a state so far from the perfect happiness of a Buddha. ^8-156


So, just as you once came to think of
a mere drop of another's blood and sperm as "yourself,"
in that same way, you should practice
thinking of others as "yourself" too. ^8-157


Acting as a careful spy for others,
whatever you notice in this body of mine that could be helpful,
you should take it, whatever it is,
and use it entirely for the benefit of others. ^8-158


"I am happy while this other person is not.
I am important while this other person is lowly.
I am the one who helps, while this other person does nothing" —
why not feel a real, healthy sense of self-criticism about thinking this way? ^8-159


I have moved myself away from true happiness,
and instead taken on the suffering of others.
Whenever this happens,
I should examine it closely as a fault in myself. ^8-160


Even when someone else does something wrong,
I should turn that around and see it as my own fault.
And even a small wrong that I myself have done,
I should openly admit to many people. ^8-161


I should let others' good reputation
shine even brighter than my own.
I should treat myself like the lowest of servants,
ready and willing to work for the benefit of every being. ^8-162


This body is, by nature, full of ordinary faults,
so I should not praise it for chance good qualities it happens to show.
I should make sure that no one
ever even notices any good qualities in me at all. ^8-163


In short, whatever harm
I have caused others for my own sake in the past,
may all of that harm instead
fall upon myself, for the true benefit of all beings. ^8-164


I should not force this new way of thinking
suddenly, all at once, with harsh effort.
Instead, like a shy new bride,
I should hold it gently, with a healthy sense of caution and care. ^8-165


"You must do this. You must remain like this.
You must never do that.
This is how you must behave" —
if my mind ever steps outside these guidelines, I must correct it firmly. ^8-166


But even after being instructed this way,
if you, my mind, still refuse to follow,
then, since you are the source of every fault,
you yourself must be firmly corrected. ^8-167


"The time when you, my mind, used to harm me
is now over and gone.
I see clearly now where you are trying to lead me —
I will break down every bit of your pride." ^8-168


Let go, once and for all, of the thought
that you still have some private interest of your own to protect.
I have already given you away entirely to others,
so do not feel sad about it — offer yourself up freely, without complaint. ^8-169


If, through carelessness,
I fail to give you over fully to the service of beings,
you will surely end up given instead
to the guardians of hell. ^8-170


In just this way, you, my mind, have handed me over to suffering
many times before, for a very long time.
Remembering all of that,
I will now break down your habit of thinking only of yourself. ^8-171


So, if I truly want happiness for myself,
I should not seek it selfishly for myself alone.
And if I truly want to protect myself,
I should instead work always to protect others. ^8-172


The more I pamper and protect
this body of mine,
the more it falls, again and again,
into being overly sensitive and fragile. ^8-173


And even if this body falls into that state,
there is no way that all the wealth in this entire world
could ever truly satisfy its endless wants —
so who could possibly satisfy its every desire? ^8-174


Wanting things beyond what can actually be achieved
only leads to more disturbing emotions and a weakened resolve.
But someone who expects nothing from others at all
can never run out of true contentment and abundance. ^8-175


So I should not give the body's desires
any extra room to grow.
Whatever is truly not needed for real purpose
is best left unclaimed and untouched. ^8-176


In the end, this body will turn to ashes,
motionless, moved about only by others.
This unclean, unbearable form —
why would I cling to it as "myself" at all? ^8-177


Whether alive or already dead,
what real use is this mechanical body to me?
What real difference is there between it and a lump of earth or stone?
Oh, if only I could let go of my pride about it! ^8-178


By pampering and indulging this body,
I have gathered pointless suffering for myself,
along with attachment and anger toward it.
What real use is there in treating it like a piece of wood instead? ^8-179


Whether I care for it carefully
or it ends up eaten by vultures,
it feels neither attachment nor anger either way —
so why should I feel such strong attachment to it? ^8-180


If this body itself does not even know
what makes it angry when criticized,
or what makes it pleased when praised,
then for whose sake, really, have I worked so hard for it? ^8-181


Someone might say, "Whoever desires this body
should be considered my true friend."
But since everyone naturally desires their own body,
why should I not feel that same warmth toward everyone, for that very reason? ^8-182


So, without any attachment,
I should give this body over entirely for the benefit of all beings.
And even though it has many faults,
I should still use it as a tool, the way one uses any ordinary tool for work. ^8-183


So, enough of behaving like an immature person —
I should follow instead in the footsteps of the wise.
Remembering the teachings on being careful and mindful,
I should push away sleepiness and dullness of mind. ^8-184


So, in order to clear away every obstacle,
I should turn my mind back from wrong paths,
and always settle it evenly and steadily
on the correct object of meditation. ^8-185


This is the end of the eighth chapter, called "Teaching Meditative Concentration," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^8-a
