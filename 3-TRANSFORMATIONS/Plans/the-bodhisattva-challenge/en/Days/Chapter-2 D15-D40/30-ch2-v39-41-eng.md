## Today's Verse

> Not pausing even a day or night, This life’s continuously depleted, And there is no extending it. So why would one like me not die? ^2-39

> While I am lying in my bed Surrounded by all my relatives, I will experience alone The feeling of my life being cut. ^2-40

> When seized by Yama’s messengers, What good are friends? What good are kin? Merit alone will guard me then, But I, alas, haven’t practiced that. ^2-41

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 39 through 41 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. Our life is being spent without a pause, day and night, and nothing is being added back. When we finally lie on the deathbed, however many relatives and friends are in the room, the experience itself is ours alone to go through. At that point nothing protects us except the merit we have already gathered. These verses are a serious push not to spend our days on nothing.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

Kunzang Palden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both compare this life to a pond whose feeding spring has been cut. Water goes on draining at the same rate as before and nothing flows in, so the level only ever falls. Kunga Wangchuk adds two more that catch the speed of it: the sun already on its way down, and a river running into the ocean. The commentaries also make a small point that is easy to skip past. It is not only that life shortens; it is that there is no mechanism anywhere by which anything could be added back. That is the whole force of verse 39.

The commentaries also tell of a man who has gone over a cliff edge and caught hold of a clump of grass, while above him a white mouse and a black mouse take turns gnawing through the roots. The two mice are day and night. Nothing about the outcome is uncertain and none of the timing is his, and the fault the story is pointing at is that he does nothing with the time he has.

For verse 40 the Dalai Lama gives the physical detail. On the last bed, the person seized by the illness is like an old tree coming down: he tries to turn himself over and cannot. Sazang and Kunga Wangchuk describe the room as fully staffed — relatives, friends, doctors, attendants, guarding on every side — and say it makes no difference. The Dalai Lama adds that the medicine taken and the rituals performed do not help either. Khenpo Zhenga states the reason exactly: not the smallest fraction of that feeling can be passed to anyone else. Minyak Kunsö puts it as there being no method by which a relative or friend could take a share.

Verse 41 then names what does work, and it is more specific than "merit" in the abstract. Minyak Kunsö says what protects is holy Dharma alone, and gives two examples of it: having gone for refuge, and clean conduct. Kunga Wangchuk says that if there is merit genuinely purified, gathered by taking refuge in the Three Jewels and doing virtue, it actually protects at that moment. Gyaltsab reduces the whole verse to one line: the regret is for not having done the virtue.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today I will not let the time go to waste. I will help one person in need or one animal, and gather merit that way.

**Explanation:** _(Doing good)_ The reason we postpone virtue is that we assume there is plenty of time to get to it, and the reason we waste days is that no single wasted day feels expensive. These verses take away both assumptions: the time is draining without pause, and at the end only merit is any use to us. So today I will act on that instead of agreeing with it. I will look for one real chance to help, and it can be small. Carrying something for someone who is struggling, giving food to a street animal, sitting with a person who is unwell. Starting small is the point, because a habit of virtue that actually holds is built out of ordinary days.
