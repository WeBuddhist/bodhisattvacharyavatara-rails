### 1.8 Second has two parts: the correctness of obtaining benefits from the aspiring mind, and the correctness of obtaining them from the engaging mind.

163. First has four parts: the benefits being great due to the infinite activity of accomplishing virtue, there being no other beneficial attitude superior to it, such a beneficial attitude never having been dreamed of even for their own sake by those very sentient beings in the past, and concluding with praise. ^1-8-1

#### First: If one merely thinks of clearing just a headache of sentient beings, with a small focus and aspect, one still possesses a beneficial attitude toward others, and

165. is endowed with immeasurable merit. If so, what need is there to mention the infinite merit of wanting to dispel the boundless unhappiness of every single sentient being and wanting to establish every single sentient being in the boundless qualities of a Buddha? ^1-8-2

166. Second: Where in the world is there such a beneficial attitude? Even parents who wish to benefit their children, or any other person, do not have such a beneficial attitude that seeks to accomplish supreme bliss. Neither do gods, sages who speak truth, or even Brahma possess such a beneficial attitude. ^1-8-3

#### Third: If those very sentient beings have never even dreamed of such a beneficial attitude for their own sake in the past, how could it ever arise in reality for the sake of others? It is unprecedented. ^1-8-4

168. Fourth: This precious, exceptional mind of the Heroes of Enlightenment—the wish to attain the state of a Buddha to accomplish the happiness and eliminate the suffering of all sentient beings, which does not arise in other sentient beings even for a single instant for their own sake—is an unprecedented, marvelous, and wondrous jewel of the mind. Others should also hold this as wondrous and make efforts toward it. ^1-8-5

### 1.9 Second has three parts: the actual explanation, its correctness, and clearing doubts.

#### First: Since the benefits of the aspiring mind are extremely great, the engaging mind conjoined with the practice of conduct is even more exceptional, because:

171. the merit of this mind, which is like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel, the cause of joy that leads all wandering beings to attain the happiness of happy migrations and liberation, and the alchemical elixir that overcomes the disease of suffering of all sentient beings—how can its measure be grasped? For it is infinite. In the *Questions of Vīradatta*, it is said: ^1-9-1

172. "If the merit of the mind of enlightenment ^1-9-2

173. were to take physical form, it would fill the entire expanse of space and even exceed it." ^1-9-3

174. Thus it is taught. ^1-9-4

175. This should be understood in detail as explained in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* by citing the *Array of Stalks Sūtra*. ^1-9-5

176. Second: If even the mere beneficial thought of "May I become a Buddha to protect all wandering beings" is taught in the *King of Meditative Concentrations Sūtra* to have merit far exceeding that of making offerings to the Buddhas in as many billions of Buddha-fields as there are, what need is there to mention the infinite merit of making great efforts in practicing and accomplishing the supreme happiness of every single sentient being without exception? ^1-9-6

177. Third: If someone asks, "Since sentient beings themselves desire happiness and do not desire suffering, and by making efforts they are able to obtain happiness and avoid suffering, are they not themselves skilled in adopting happiness and discarding suffering? What need is there for others to strive for their sake?" ^1-9-7

178. It is definitely necessary to strive, because: ^1-9-8

179. although sentient beings have the wish to discard suffering, under the power of afflictions they accomplish only the causes of suffering, thereby running directly toward endless cycle of existence and the sufferings of bad migrations. Although they desire happiness, they are ignorant of its methods, and through their conjoining with delusion, they destroy their own happiness like an enemy, thus entering wrongly into the methods for happiness and suffering. For that reason, what other virtue is equal to this mind, whose activity satisfies those sentient beings who are ignorant of the methods of happiness and suffering, destitute of happiness, and endowed with abundant sufferings, with all the happiness of happy migrations and definitive goodness, cuts off all suffering, and dispels the ignorance of not knowing the causes of happiness and suffering through great wisdom. ^1-9-9

180. Where else is there such a helpful spiritual friend, who accomplishes all benefit and protects from all harm? ^1-9-10

181. Where else is there such merit, which unerringly teaches all points of adoption and abandonment? There is none, so one must make efforts to generate the mind of enlightenment. ^1-9-11

182. Fourth has three parts: being worthy of praise because of acting as a friend drawn by great compassion even without being asked, being even more worthy of praise for accomplishing all benefit and happiness if those who accomplish minor benefits are praised, and being worthy of taking refuge because of being a supreme field of merit. ^1-9-12

#### First: The Heroes of Enlightenment are indeed to be praised and lauded, because:

184. if someone in the world who repays a past favor is temporarily worthy of praise as a "grateful person," what need is there to mention how worthy of praise by gods and humans alike are the Heroes of Enlightenment, who, without being asked, are drawn by great compassion to perfectly accomplish all benefit and happiness? ^1-9-13

185. Second: In the world, if someone who gives a minor thing like ordinary food to a few wandering beings—such as a hundred—for just a brief moment, with a low practice conjoined with disrespect and contempt, and of minor benefit such as satisfying them for half a day, is still honored by people as a "great benefactor who performs virtue"; then what need is there to mention how worthy of honor is the Hero of Enlightenment, who, for a long duration throughout the cycle of existence, gives to a vast field of infinite sentient beings the exceptional object of giving—the supreme, stainless bliss of the Blissful Ones—and ^1-9-14

186. who constantly gives with a practice conjoined with respect, completely fulfilling all the thoughts of benefit? Therefore, an intelligent person should be single-mindedly devoted to the Heroes of Enlightenment. ^1-9-15

187. Third has three parts: the reasons why it is improper to disparage a Hero of Enlightenment even slightly, the reasons why it is proper to cultivate faith, and why it is therefore proper to bow down and take refuge. ^1-9-16

#### First: Towards such a supreme benefactor, a Son of the Victorious, who gives all benefit and happiness to sentient beings,

189. if one should generate a harmful mind that wishes to disparage and harm them, that is the ultimate of negativity, because: ^1-9-17

190. if one generates a harmful mind, in the *Sūtra of the Miracle of Complete Pacification*, it is said: "O Mañjuśrī, for as long as a Hero of Enlightenment generates a mind of anger and contempt toward another Hero of Enlightenment, for that many eons he must wear the armor of remaining in hell." ^1-9-18

191. Since it is taught by the Sage that one will remain in hell for as many eons as the number of moments of generating that harmful mind, one must be extremely careful regarding this point. ^1-9-19

192. In the *Sūtra of the Mudra of Generating the Power of Faith*, it is said that generating a harmful mind toward a Hero of Enlightenment who is inclined to the Great Vehicle produces far more countless negativity than destroying or burning as many stūpas as there are grains of sand in the River Ganges. The reason for this is explained to be that since Buddhas are born from Heroes of Enlightenment, harming the cause of Buddhas is the ultimate of negativity, as is taught in many scriptures and commentaries. ^1-9-20

#### Second: On the other hand, if a person generates clear faith and devotion toward a Hero of Enlightenment,

194. the fruit of that faith increases far more than the fruit of negativity explained before, ^1-9-21

195. and for far more countless eons than the moments of clear faith, one will experience exceptional fruits, ^1-9-22

196. as is taught in the *Sūtra of the Mudra of Entering the Definite and Indefinite*. Third: Even if harm and suffering are produced toward the great Sons of the Victorious, they do not generate a negative mind of anger even at the cost of their lives; rather, their virtue increases naturally. According to the commentary, it is explained that because anger and so forth have no purpose in abiding in them, they do not arise in the continuum of the Heroes of Enlightenment even under great pressure. ^1-9-23

197. The meaning is that even at the cost of their lives, the Heroes of Enlightenment do not commit negativity, and their virtue increases naturally. ^1-9-24

198. For this reason, one bows down with devotion through the three doors to the body of anyone in whom this precious, supreme mind has arisen. As will be explained in the Chapter on Patience, and as in the *Jātaka of King Maitrībala* where the five spirits drank the blood of his body yet were ultimately established in the noble state, even if one harms these Heroes of Enlightenment, they not only refrain from retaliating but produce only happiness. Thus, one takes refuge in this source of happiness. In brief, whether entering the path of Mantra or the Perfections, the very generation of the mind is the doorway for entering the Great Vehicle; therefore, one must make efforts to generate this mind through various methods. ^1-9-25

199. To generate it, as explained before, one must first meditate on the benefits of generating the mind so that one's joyful effort toward the benefits increases from the depths of one's heart, and one must practice the seven-limb worship along with taking refuge, as is taught in this supreme commentary showing the stages of the path of a Hero of Enlightenment and in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*. ^1-9-26

200. The benefits thus taught can also be condensed into two: temporary benefits and ultimate benefits. ^1-9-27

201. The former also has two: not falling into bad migrations and being born in happy migrations. ^1-9-28

202. If this generation of the mind is born, many previously accumulated causes of bad migrations are purified, the future accumulation of them is cut off, previously accumulated causes of happy migrations are grasped by it and increase extensively, and newly accumulated ones are motivated by it and are endless. ^1-9-29

203. Ultimately, liberation and omniscience are also easily accomplished by relying on this mind; before that, if one lacks an uncontrived aspiration that seeks the temporary and ultimate benefits, then saying "one must make efforts to generate the mind because those benefits arise from generating the mind" becomes mere words. If one examines one's own continuum, this is extremely clear. ^1-9-30

204. To generate the desire to obtain the two benefits of happy migrations and definitive goodness, one must train the mind of lesser and medium persons, and then enter the meditation on the mind of enlightenment which has love and compassion as its root. ^1-9-31

205. For those wise ones who have obtained a human body of leisure and opportunity, diligently meditating on the two minds of enlightenment is the supreme way of taking the essence of the support of leisure and opportunity; first, diligently meditate on the benefits of that mind! ^1-9-32

206. This is the summarizing verse. ^1-9-33

207. Second, the title of the chapter: From the *Commentary on Entering the Way of the Heroes of Enlightenment, entitled 'The Gateway for the Heirs of the Victorious'*, this is the commentary on Chapter One, entitled "Explaining the Benefits of the Mind of Enlightenment." ^1-9-34

# Chapter Two: Confession of Negativity — excerpt, verses 2.51–2.65 (ལེའུ་གཉིས་པ། སྡིག་པ་བཤགས་པའི་ལེའུ།)

*Note: this section translates only the closing portion of Chapter Two (verses 51–65), continuing the numbered commentary from the point corresponding to these verses.*

Likewise, I seek refuge in the Bodhisattvas Ākāśagarbha and Kṣitigarbha, as well as Maitreya, Sarvanivaraṇaviṣkambhin, and all the other great compassionate protectors, and crying out in anguish from the depths of my heart, I say: "Protect me!" ^2-51-1

I also go for refuge to Vajrapāṇi, the wielder of the vajra, the mere sight of whom causes the messengers of the Lord of Death, the guardians of hell, and other beings hostile to sentient beings, to be struck with terror and flee in all directions. ^2-52-1

In the past I transgressed your command and committed wrongs while failing to practice virtue. But now, seeing the great terror of endless saṃsāra and the lower realms, I go for refuge to you. I will act, regarding what is to be adopted and abandoned, exactly according to your command; therefore please swiftly dispel this fear. ^2-53-1

If, fearing an ordinary illness caused by the disturbance of wind, bile, and so forth, and worrying I might die, I must apply the remedy exactly as the physician instructs — then, since we are constantly afflicted by the great illness of the three poisons (desire and the others), which since beginningless time has been the source of hundreds and thousands of faults, what need is there even to mention that it is fitting to rely on the supreme physician's remedy, in accordance with the Victor's word? It is fitting to strive at the antidote to wrongdoing. ^2-54-1

Something like anger toward a Bodhisattva is so harmful that even a single instance of it can drag all people dwelling in this world down into hell. If, apart from the Teacher's instruction on the antidote for these afflictions — the way of meditating on the path and so forth — no other remedy to cure these illnesses can be found anywhere else, even in Brahmā's world or any other realm, then it is exceedingly rare indeed. ^2-55-1

For that afflictive illness, not to follow the instruction of the Omniscient Physician, who removes every pain of the afflictions, but instead to set one's mind on the causes of endless saṃsāra and the lower realms, is a state to be reproached as utter delusion. Therefore, one should go for refuge to the Teacher from the depths of one's heart, and put the Teacher's instruction into practice correctly. ^2-56-1

If, at a mere ordinary cliff or mountainside where a fall would injure only a limb, I must be watchful and careful for fear of falling, then what need is there to mention being watchful about falling into an abyss of thousands of leagues — such as thirty-two thousand leagues — where, having fallen, one must remain for a very long time? Therefore, one should strive at relying on the antidote to the afflictions. ^2-57-1

One might think, "Although I must strive at the antidote, I will strive next month or next year." But it is right to strive from this very day: it is not reasonable to remain in comfortable idleness, not striving at the antidote, thinking "I will surely not die today." Since there is no certainty that I will not die today, the time when I will cease to exist will most likely, without doubt, come tomorrow. Therefore, without laziness, I should strive from this very day onward — as it is said: "To say 'Today I shall do this and that' is not good conduct for a person; the day will surely come when you will cease to exist, and that tomorrow will inevitably arrive." ^2-58-1

One might object: "Since being afraid of death does not help, there is no need to fear it." But even though we see and hear that everyone else dies and fear the possibility of falling into the lower realms after death, what noble being has granted me freedom from fear of death and wrongdoing? No one at all has granted this. So if I do not strive at the antidote, how can I possibly be certain of being freed from wrongdoing and death — I cannot be freed, and it is certain that I will die and cease to exist. Given this, it is unreasonable to remain complacent under the power of laziness instead of striving at the antidote to wrongdoing; I should strive at meditating on the path. ^2-59-1

Of all the enjoyments I have already experienced in saṃsāra and which, being unreliable, have since perished, what excess still remains for me? Nothing at all remains. Yet I have fervently clung and become attached to that which has no essence, and thereby acted contrary to the guru's word and committed wrong — and for this I feel regret. ^2-60-1

My essenceless body, in this very life and thereafter, must be left behind, along with my relatives and friends; I alone must go, without control, to some uncertain destination. Given this, what use is there in all my attachment and aversion toward the loved and unloved? There is not the slightest benefit in it, so it is unreasonable to remain attached to them. ^2-61-1

Since suffering such as that of hell arises from non-virtue like killing, "How can I be certain of being freed from that suffering?" — I should reflect, day and night, continually, on this matter of the karmic law of cause and effect between virtue and non-virtue alone. If one has not gained deep, firm conviction in the workings of karma, one will not find certainty pleasing to the Victors in any teaching. Therefore all should strive at this. Some claim to have gained certainty in emptiness while disregarding the workings of karma — this clearly shows their understanding has gone astray, for they have failed to gain certainty that emptiness has the meaning of dependent origination. ^2-62-1

Being deluded and ignorant regarding karma, whatever wrong I have committed — whether it belongs to the class of naturally unwholesome acts, which remain wrongs regardless of who commits them, or whether it belongs to the class of conventionally prohibited acts, which are wrong only for someone bound by a precept — whatever bad deed I have done, of either kind: ^2-63-1

— all these I now confess, directly before the Protectors, the Buddhas together with their heirs, with my conduct of joining palms, and with a mind afraid of suffering, prostrating again and again. ^2-64-1

For this reason, I ask the Guides to accept as faults the wrongs I have committed. Since committing wrong is not good conduct, from now on, even at the cost of my life, I shall never do it again. This means that from today onward I shall cut off wrongdoing continuously and maintain restraint. Although the scriptures together with their commentaries teach many methods for purifying wrongdoing, the complete antidote is this confession endowed with all four powers; through it, even wrongs certain to be experienced as karmic result can be purified, as is taught in the *Blaze of Reasoning* and the *Great Commentary on the Eight Thousand [Verse Perfection of Wisdom]*. Since we are unskilled in discerning the workings of karma, and even when we know a little we fail to properly adopt and abandon accordingly, many doors to wrongdoing open for us each and every day. Therefore we should continually engage in the path of confession endowed with the four powers, and in particular should strive with the specific aim of purifying the obstacles to the arising of the mind of enlightenment. ^2-65-1
