# Day 32 — The cry that finds a listener

> **Notification**
> **Title:** Call out for help once today, plainly and out loud inside
> **Detail:** Today's verses cry "who can save me?" The chapter's quiet answer: there is someone who hears.

## Opening

Shantideva pushes the fear to its limit: seized, sick, and crying out, "Who can save me?" while looking everywhere and finding nothing. It reads like a closed door. But the cry itself is the opening. This is the lowest point of the chapter, and the turn that follows is to take refuge. The desperate question was never really without an answer; it just had not yet been pointed in the right direction.

## From the Tradition

Sazang Mati Panchen, a fourteenth-century teacher, reads the searching as a question that is waiting to be aimed. The dying person looks in all four directions and cries, who can save me from this. Sazang completes the cry that the verse leaves open. It becomes, he says, a loud call of suffering to Avalokiteshvara, the lord of compassion who looks after every being. So the search was not pointless after all. It was a refuge instinct that simply had nowhere to land, because it kept looking outward at the room instead of toward the ones who actually hear. The hard image is doing gentle work: it shows us that underneath our panic is a real call for help, and it tells us who that call is for. Naming who you are calling, while you are calm, is what lets the cry reach home when you are not.

## Today's Practice

Once today, when something sits heavy on you, try actually calling out for help inside, in plain words: to the Buddha, the dharma, and the sangha, or to Avalokiteshvara, the buddha of compassion. Not a polished prayer, just an honest "please help me with this." Letting yourself ask is itself the practice of refuge.
