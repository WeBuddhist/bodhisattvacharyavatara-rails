## Combined Commentary Summaries

The three classical Tibetan commentators—Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme—express absolute consensus in their interpretation of Verse 1-33. They analyze this verse as the second half of a powerful *a fortiori* comparison (commenced in Verse 1-32) that contrasts the extremely limited, flawed, and short-lived charity of worldly benefactors with the infinite, perfect, and ultimate generosity of a Bodhisattva (Hero of Enlightenment). By systematically demonstrating how the Bodhisattva's giving reverses every limitation of worldly charity, the commentators establish why the heirs of the Buddha are incomparably worthy of the highest praise, honor, and single-minded devotion.

### 1. The Vastness of the Recipients (Infinite Objects)
While worldly benefactors focus only on a highly restricted group of recipients (which Sazang Mati Panchen defines as "a few wandering beings of a specific area" and Gyaltsab Je quantifies as "such as a hundred" individuals), the commentators highlight that the Bodhisattva's field of generosity is boundless:
- **Gyaltsab Je** characterizes the recipients as a "vast field of infinite sentient beings."
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** explains that the Bodhisattva's focus is is a "vast object" encompassing the "infinite and countless realms of sentient beings."
- **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly defines the objects of focus as "infinite sentient beings."

### 2. The Supremacy of the Gift (Stainless Bliss)
In contrast to the worldly benefactor who offers mediocre, ordinary material things like plain food (which merely satisfies the recipients' physical hunger for "half a day"), the Bodhisattva bestows a gift of supreme spiritual value:
- **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** identify this supreme substance of giving as "the supreme, stainless bliss of the Blissful Ones (Sugatas)" (*bde gshegs kyi bde ba bla na med pa*).
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** defines it as "the supreme object of the unexcelled happiness of the Blissful Ones."

### 3. The Respectfulness of the Manner (Conjoined with Devotion)
Worldly charity is frequently accompanied by a disrespectful, condescending attitude (characterized by Gyaltsab Je as a "low practice conjoined with disrespect and contempt," and by Sazang Mati Panchen as giving "without making offerings, and with mediocre application, such as treating them with contempt, fighting, and beating them"). By contrast, the Bodhisattva's manner of giving is marked by profound care and veneration:
- **Gyaltsab Je** notes that they give "with a practice conjoined with respect, completely fulfilling all the thoughts of benefit."
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** describes this as a "peaceful, disciplined, and respectful application, in a manner that completely fulfills all the various desires in their minds."
- **Ngulchu Thokme** emphasizes that they give "with deep respect, completely fulfilling all their desires."

### 4. The Constancy and Eternity of the Duration
While the worldly benefactor's charity is fleeting and momentary, the Bodhisattva's giving is continuous and eternal:
- **Gyaltsab Je** notes that the Bodhisattva's giving lasts "for a long duration throughout the cycle of existence."
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** explains that they constantly give "for a vast duration as long as space remains and the cycle of existence is not emptied." Sazang also highlights a subtle textual variation, noting that the Sanskrit root text reads "as long as space and wandering beings are not completely exhausted" (rather than "the unexcelled happiness of the Blissful Ones"), which carries the exact same meaning.
- **Ngulchu Thokme** provides a valuable scholastic note, stating that the word "always" or "constantly" (*rtag tu*) in the root text is explained by classical commentators as meaning "never exhausted as long as space and wandering beings remain."

### 5. Conclusion: Worthiness of Veneration
Based on this stark contrast, all three commentators argue that if society deems it proper to praise and honor a worldly benefactor for a tiny, disrespectful, half-day gift of food, then the Bodhisattvas are infinitely more worthy of praise, honor, and devotion:
- **Gyaltsab Je** concludes that because of this supreme, limitless benefit, an intelligent person should be "single-mindedly devoted to the Heroes of Enlightenment."
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** asks rhetorically "what need is there to mention how worthy of praise and respect are those endowed with the mind of enlightenment."
- **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly emphasizes that they are the "supreme benefactor" and are incomparably worthy of honor and veneration.
