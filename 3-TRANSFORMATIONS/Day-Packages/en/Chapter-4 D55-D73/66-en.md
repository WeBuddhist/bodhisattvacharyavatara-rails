## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 30 and 31 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. No outer enemy, however strong, can throw us into the lowest hell. But the one inner enemy, the afflictions, can throw us there in a single instant.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a saying that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Mount Meru, the king of mountains, is eighty thousand leagues high and extremely solid. Yet this inner enemy, the afflictions, can burn even Mount Meru to ash without a trace in a single instant. In the same way, if even one moment of anger or ill will arises in a bodhisattva, it can throw them into the lowest hell.

However strong the outer enemies of this world may be, they cannot throw us into the lowest hell. But the afflictions in our own mind can throw us into a great hell in a single instant. So we must always watch for and guard against the afflictions in our own mind, even more than we watch for a fearsome outer enemy.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, the moment anger arises in me, I will recognize it and stop it from spilling into my speech or my actions.

**Explanation:** *(Giving up anger)* Anger is more fearsome than any outer enemy. A single moment of anger can burn away a great store of doing good and cause us to fall into the lower realms. So we must put out the flame of anger right away and guard our own mind.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Commentaries Agree: However Mighty, Worldly Enemies Cannot Lead One into the Fire of the Lowest Hell

Even the most powerful worldly enemies — gods, demigods, and their like — can at most harm this life's body, possessions, or life itself; none of them can cast one into the unrelenting hell.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Names the Gods and Demigods by Name to Fix the Scale of the Harm

Naming Indra and Brahma among the gods, and Virūpākṣa among the demigods, he shows that even the mightiest of them can only harm body, wealth, or life in this world — not cast one into the hell of unrelenting torment in a future birth.

###### 3. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Shows That Even All the Beings of the Three Realms Rising as One's Enemy Cannot Force One into Suffering

Basing this on the acceptance of karmic cause and effect, he teaches that though all sentient beings of the three realms might rise up as one's enemy, they cannot make oneself sink into suffering.

###### 4. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen Links the External Enemy Directly to the Enemy of One's Own Afflictions

Having shown that no worldly enemy, however united, can lead one into the fire of the lowest hell, he moves directly to the harm of the afflictions within one's own mindstream and counsels striving to destroy them.

###### 5. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Fixes the Limit of a Worldly Enemy's Power, by Contrast with the Inner Enemy

He shows that even all the gods and demigods joined together can do no more than rob one's wealth, strike the body, or take life — none of them can lead one into the fire of the lowest hell.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that even if every worldly enemy — every god and demigod — rose up against me at once, they could only harm this life's body, possessions, or life; none of them can lead me into the fire of the lowest hell. The point is to make one reflect instead on the far greater harm done by the powerful enemy within one's own mindstream, the afflictions, and to strive to destroy it.

**Key points.**

- Even if Indra and Brahma among the gods, and Virūpākṣa and the like among the demigods — all the mightiest beings renowned in the world — rose up against me, they could do no more than harm this life's body or possessions, or at worst take my life.
- These worldly enemies, however powerful, cannot lead and cast me into the fire of the unrelenting hell in a future birth.
- Unlike an outer enemy, the powerful enemy of one's own afflictions can, the instant it meets that fire, reduce even Mount Meru to ash without a trace and cast me into it — so it is this that must be abandoned.
- Building on the point that no united worldly enemy can lead one into the fire of the lowest hell, one is counseled to turn instead to the harm of the powerful enemy of one's own afflictions and strive to destroy it.
- Accepting that happiness and suffering are experienced as the results of karmic cause and effect, one is shown that even if all sentient beings of the three realms rose up as one's enemy, they could not force one to sink into suffering.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that the powerful enemy of one's own afflictions is the true enemy that yokes one to ruin: the moment it meets anything at all, even Mount Meru, it reduces it to ash without a trace, and it can cast oneself into the fire of the lowest hell in a single instant. Since it is more fearsome than any outer enemy, one is urged to rely on the antidote and strive to abandon and destroy it.

**Key points.**

- A single afflicted thought, arising for just an instant, can cast one into the pit of the fire of the burning, unrelenting hell that would reduce even Mount Meru to ash — so the afflictions themselves are the true enemy that yokes one to ruin.
- Unlike every outer enemy of this world, which cannot lead one into the fire of the lowest hell however powerful it is, the powerful enemy of one's own afflictions can cast one there in a single instant — so one must strive to destroy this enemy.
- Even a single evil thought such as ill will toward a bodhisattva, arising for a single instant, carries the extreme peril of casting oneself into the fire of the lowest hell that would reduce even Mount Meru to ash without a trace.
- This enemy also serves as the cause and condition that stirs up and stores the gravest wrongs, such as the acts of immediate retribution, and reduces even Mount Meru to ash while casting oneself into that same fire in an instant — so it is fitting to abandon the afflictions.
- One is urged to rely on the antidote and to strive, by every possible means, to abandon and destroy this powerful enemy of one's own afflictions.
