## Combined Commentary Summaries

The three classical commentators—Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme—express profound consensus in explaining the final verse of the first chapter (Verse 1-36). They organize their explanations around three cohesive themes: the supreme worthiness of the Bodhisattva as an object of homage, the resolution of the apparent paradox of how harming a Bodhisattva leads to happiness, and the absolute necessity of taking refuge in Bodhisattvas as the ultimate wellspring of joy.

### 1. Homage to the Bodhisattva (The Royal Prince Analogy)
All three commentators agree that because of the boundless qualities of the mind of enlightenment, anyone in whom this precious mind has arisen becomes a supreme object of homage:
- **Bowing Down through the Three Doors:** Gyaltsab Je and Sazang Mati Panchen both state that we must pay homage with deep devotion through the three doors of body, speech, and mind at all times to anyone in whom this precious, supreme mind has arisen. Sazang explains that this mind is like a wish-fulfilling jewel that clears all degenerations and grants all fortunes.
- **The Royal Prince Analogy:** Ngulchu Thokme highlights this worthiness by citing a beautiful analogy from the **Ratnakūṭa Sūtra** (*དཀོན་མཆོག་བརྩེགས་པའི་མདོ།*). The sūtra explains that when a great king has a son endowed with the marks of royalty, the citizens and minor kings gather to honor the young prince the moment he is born. Likewise, the moment the precious mind of perfect awakening is generated, that "prince of the Victorious" (the Bodhisattva) is immediately honored and cherished with devotion by the entire world, including the gods.

### 2. Resolving the Paradox: How Harming a Bodhisattva Leads to Happiness
The core philosophical paradox of Verse 1-36 is how "even an offense results in happiness" or how "even those who have harmed them find connection with bliss." This seems to contradict Verse 1-34, which states that generating anger toward a Bodhisattva leads to remaining in hell for eons. The commentators resolve this apparent contradiction with perfect harmony:
- **Ripening of Negative Karma vs. Bodhisattva's Compassionate Resolve:** Ngulchu Thokme and Sazang Mati Panchen explicitly address this doubt. They explain that there is no conflict. The statement that harming a Bodhisattva leads to hell (Verse 1-34) refers to the **natural ripening effect of the negative mind itself**—the severe negative karma generated by the harmer's own hostility. On the other hand, the statement that harming them results in happiness (Verse 1-36) refers to the **Bodhisattva's compassionate resolve** and the power of their aspirational prayers. 
- **Skillful Methods and Aspirational Prayers:** Sazang explains that even when others commit offenses, abuse, or violence against them, no negativity ever arises in the Bodhisattvas. Instead, through their great compassion, skillful methods, and the power of their aspirational prayers, they eventually connect their harmers with temporary and ultimate happiness, guiding them to liberation.
- **The Example of King Maitrībala:** To illustrate this, Gyaltsab Je and Ngulchu Thokme both cite the **Jātaka of King Maitrībala** (*རྒྱལ་པོ་བྱམས་པའི་སྟོབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱེས་རབས།*). In this account, five spirits (yakṣas) drank the blood of the king's body. Despite this fierce harm, King Maitrībala did not react with anger or retaliation; instead, through the unshakeable power of his love and patience, he established those very harmers in temporary and ultimate happiness, placing them on the path to liberation.

### 3. Taking Refuge in the Source of Happiness
Because Bodhisattvas respond to both benefit and harm with nothing but compassion and joy, they represent the ultimate refuge:
- **Trust and Refuge:** Sazang Mati Panchen and Ngulchu Thokme emphasize that because the Bodhisattvas are the sole source of all happiness (*བདེ་བའི་འབྱུང་གནས།*), we must place our trust and take refuge in them, promising to always rely on these noble beings.
- **The Doorway to the Great Vehicle:** Gyaltsab Je concludes by placing this entire chapter in context. He explains that whether one enters the path of Mantra (Vajrayāna) or the Perfections (Pāramitāyāna), the very generation of the mind of enlightenment is the sole doorway for entering the Great Vehicle. Therefore, trainees must make every effort to generate this mind through various methods, such as meditating on its benefits, practicing the seven-limb worship, and taking refuge.
