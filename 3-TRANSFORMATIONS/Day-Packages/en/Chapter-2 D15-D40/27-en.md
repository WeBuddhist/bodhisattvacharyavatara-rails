# Day 27 — Harm confessed — before death arrives

**Date:** Aug 1  
**Chapter:** 2  
**Verses covered:** 2-30 to 2-32

---

<!-- sec:challenge -->
## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 30 through 32 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Over a lifetime we do a great deal of harm, some of it deliberate and much of it careless, to the very people we owe the most: our parents, our teachers, and the Three Jewels. Today's verses set that beside a second fact, that this life is short and the hour of death is not fixed. Together they push us to stop postponing and to confess our mistakes now, with real regret.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These verses stop saying "I have done wrong" and start saying who it was done to. Driven by the afflictions, Shantideva harmed the Three Jewels, his own parents, and his teachers, with body, speech, and mind. He lays the worst of it before the guides. Then he says the thing that makes it urgent: he may die before he is cleansed, so he asks to be rescued quickly.

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains why quickly is the whole point. That we die is settled. When we die is not. A road accident ends a life in a moment, and no doctor anywhere will guarantee that a particular person is still alive tomorrow. So the stretch between now and death is not a period we can make plans inside.

The commentaries give an old image for what waiting costs us. Rust does not attack iron from outside. It rises out of the iron, and then it eats the iron. Our own misdeeds work like that. They sit in us and consume us, and if death arrives while they are still sitting there, there is nowhere to go but downward.

Which is why this is something to do today rather than something to schedule.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will not speak a harsh word to my parents or my teachers. I will speak to them with respect and warmth.

**Explanation:** *(Avoiding Wrongdoing)* The people who have been kindest to us are often the ones we are sharpest with. Anger comes out most easily at home, and we let ourselves say things to a parent or a teacher that we would never say to a stranger. Today's verses count this as a serious wrong, not a small domestic slip, because these are the people whose kindness we are standing on. So today I will keep watch on my tone with my parents and my teachers. Before I answer them, I will bring to mind one specific thing they have done for me. This clears something of what has already been done, and it stops the next round of it from starting.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

What came before was said in general terms; here the explanation proceeds object by object. Relying on the Three Jewels as the object; and further on parents and gurus; and on "others" — the field of those who suffer: beings who are sick, beings with no protector, beings tormented by hunger and thirst, beings in suffering who are especially objects of compassion, those in a lowly state. Relying on all of them, and under the power of the afflictions in one's own mind — the three of attachment, aversion and confusion — the way karma comes to expression is this: what is accumulated by way of the body, what is accumulated by way of speech, and what is accumulated by way of the mind. (Here he explains the meaning of verses 30 and 31 together.)

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Now, specifically, the gravest of faults. Whatever harm I have done — to the field endowed with good qualities, that is, the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha, the Three Jewels; and to the field of kindness shown to me, my father and mother; and to the field weighty with the burden of good qualities and worthy of honour, that is, other gurus such as one's abbot and preceptor — done from a motivation of the afflictions, the three of attachment, aversion and confusion and the rest, and carried out with the body by wrecking and destroying, beating and striking and so on, with the speech by slander and abusive words and so on, and with the mind by wrong views and ill will and so on. And graver still than that are actions that harm the Three Jewels, and stealing what belongs to them. All the requisites of the Buddha, of the Dharma and of the one who teaches the Dharma are the property of the Buddha and the Dharma, and to rob or steal them, or to take them by deceit without their being given, is explained to be an extremely grave fault. In short, whatever property belongs to the Sangha, great or small, if one robs or steals it, or takes it with a trader's cunning and deceit, the sutras say that one is certain to be born in the hells, with no method left by which to confess it away.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

The Three Jewels are the field of unsurpassed good qualities; parents are the field of kindness shown to one; the guru is the field of both good qualities and kindness shown. Distinguishing these three fields, the underlying gloss sets it out; and by "to other beings as well" it explains that beings in general are also gathered into the object named by "others."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

This is the third branch of the confession of wrongdoing ("third:"): here the confession made by distinguishing the objects begins. Whatever harm I, who am subject to delusion, have done — to the objects that are the Three Jewels, and to my parents, or to gurus and others who are fields of merit — arising from its cause, the afflictions, the three poisons, and through the doors from which wrongdoing arises, body, speech and mind: all of that I shall confess.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

I, the agent, have committed whatever fault or harm — to the objects: the Three Jewels; my parents; or gurus, that is, abbots and preceptors, or any others whatsoever — with an intention possessed by the afflictions, through body, speech and mind. In short, whatever exceedingly unbearable wrongdoing I have done, I who am a wrongdoer stained by the flaws of many faults such as attachment: all of that I confess to the guides. (Here he explains the meaning of verses 30 and 31 together.)

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whatever harmful action I have done, having in view the holy objects — the field of good qualities, the Three Precious Jewels in their three kinds; and the field of kindness shown to me, my father and mother; and the field worthy of honour, other gurus such as abbots and preceptors — with a motivation of the afflictions such as hatred, and carried out through body, speech and mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whatever harmful or faulty action I in my confusion have committed — having in view the objects: the special field of unsurpassed good qualities, the Three Jewels; the field of kindness shown to me, my father and mother; or the field of graciousness, the guru, that is, abbots and preceptors, or any others whatsoever — set in motion by its cause, the three poisons of the afflictions, and carried out through body, speech and mind, the bases from which wrongdoing arises: because such action has the function of producing a vast and unbearable ripening, it becomes the cause of the sufferings of samsara in general and of the lower realms in particular, and it obstructs the path to the higher realms and to liberation. In that respect it is called being "stained by the flaws of many faults." (Here he explains the meaning of verses 30 and 31 together.)

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

I in my confusion, towards the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha — the Three Jewels in their three kinds — and towards my kind father and mother, or gurus and spiritual friends, abbots, preceptors and others, through whichever of the afflictions applied: desire, anger, pride, envy and rivalry, ill will and the rest. And "through body, speech and mind" means this. By way of the body, for example: with regard to the Jewel of the Buddha among the Three Jewels, damaging its supports, the painted images and so on. By way of speech: mocking and reviling. By way of the mind: holding wrong views, or having no faith. And likewise: scattering all the kinds of scriptural volumes and letters that are the support of the Jewel of the sublime Dharma, or burning them in fire, or defiling them with filth; and beating and striking all the members of the Sangha, taking their lives, seizing their robes and religious garments, and so forth. In short, whatever harm has been done to the Three Jewels through body, speech and mind — and, in the same way, whatever harm has been done to one's own father and mother: beating them with the body, abusing them with speech, harming them with the mind through evil intent; and whatever such offences have been done to gurus, abbots, preceptors and spiritual friends — all of these are "many faults," that is, exceedingly numerous. (Here he explains the meaning of verses 30 and 31 together.)

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- div:divergences -->
##### ⚑ Divergences (where the commentaries differ)

- **kunpal / gyaltsab / ngulchu-thogmed / sabzang / minyak-kunzang-sonam / khenpo-kunga:** they take the word "other" in "other gurus" to point to some further guru — an abbot, a preceptor and the like — and so read the scope of the object narrowly.
- **khenpo-zhengah / tenzin-gyatso:** they read "others" as gathering in beings in general as well; and His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes it explicit that beings in a lowly state who suffer — the sick, those with no protector, those tormented by hunger and thirst — are especially objects of compassion.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — The Illness of the Adept Kyergangpa

As an illustration of the words "stealing what belongs to the Three Jewels is a grave fault": the body of the adept Kyergangpa was pierced again and again by a white syllable Aḥ, so that he fell gravely ill. This is said to have been the ripening of his having drawn to himself the offering-fee that had been given for recitations of the *Eight Thousand [Line Perfection of Wisdom]* on another's behalf.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — A Cluster of Hell Stories About Using the Sangha's Property

From the *Vinaya*: someone who fouled the walls and pillars of the Sangha's assembly hall with spittle and mucus was born in a hell that was itself like those walls and pillars; someone who used the Sangha's pestle and broom and the like for himself was born in a hell that was like that pestle and broom; and those who turned the Sangha's summer dues into winter dues and its winter dues into summer dues and so failed to distribute them became beings whose waists were severed, held together only by a sinew, who go about in extreme suffering. And from the *Sutra of the Wise and the Foolish*: a great tree so covered with all kinds of worms that not even a needle's tip of it was left uncovered, all of them eating it — this is explained to have been the ripening for a Sangha official named Lita, who used the Sangha's property for himself and gave its food and drink away to lay households.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — The Four-Legged Worm in the Cesspit

In a filthy cesspit in the city of Rājagṛha there lived a snake-like worm with four legs, which underwent measureless suffering for many years. This is explained as the ripening of the following. Long ago five hundred merchants offered jewels as goods for merit, and these were entrusted to the monk who was steward. When he was later asked whether they should be sold, he grew angry and, instead of distributing them to the whole Sangha, gave them to that one man alone. By that cause he fell into the hell of no reprieve, and then wallowed in filth for ninety-one aeons.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — The Story of Gyaljung, Abbot of Nālandā

In a cave to the north of Nālandā a boy was seen with tongues of flame flickering from his mouth, blazing, and crying out in Sanskrit. He had formerly been Gyaljung, the abbot of Nālandā. Because he had helped himself, as though it were his by right, to half a measure of the Sangha's broken rice, he had now been born as a hungry ghost with a burning belly, so that all his inner organs are constantly consumed by blazing fire and the flames flicker out from his mouth and nose.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — The Five Hundred Hungry Ghosts of the Cockerel Temple

The sutras tell of five hundred men who long ago muddied the Sangha's water at the Cockerel Temple, so that the water could no longer be used to cook rice and the Sangha's Dharma-feast for that day was spoiled. As the ripening of that, they were born as five hundred terrifying hungry ghosts and underwent great suffering.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — The Scrupulous Steward of Nālandā

When Lord Atisha was staying at Nālandā, there was a steward who acted in accord with the Dharma. One day, as he was serving drink to the Sangha, he saw that not even a drop was left in the water vessel; thinking "I must have made a mistake," he checked again and again, going over the count over and over. In the end, it is said, the fruit ripened as a lessening of his obscurations. Kunzang Pelden therefore advises that gurus, monastery treasurers and all who administer property must be careful and conscientious with the property of the Three Jewels in general, and with the property of the Sangha in particular.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-30]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of the Old Man "Rgan-skyes"

Told as an illustration of this verse's line "through my body, my speech… driven by destructive emotions." In Rājagṛha the wife of a wealthy merchant carried a son in her womb for a very long time; later ten more sons were born, but the first son never came out of the womb. At her death the woman said, "All that is gathered is exhausted in the end; all that is high is destroyed in the end," and so on, and died. The physician Jīvaka cut her open, and a boy came out alive — white-haired, thin, with the flesh of an old man — and so he became known as "Rgan-skyes," the one born old. He advised others never to speak harsh words to their parents or to their abbots and preceptors. When the Blessed One was asked the reason, he said that in a former life this man had spoken harshly to an abbot, saying, "May you not come out of the house for sixty years," and that this was the ripening of that action.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-30]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **"Rust arises from iron, and the rust that has arisen devours the iron itself"** (ལྕགས་ལས་གཡའ་ནི་ལངས་གྱུར་པ། ལངས་པ་དེས་ནི་དེ་ཉིད་ཟ།) → one goes to the lower realms through one's own unexamined actions. - **"Like poison, fire and a blade — such is the evil one has done oneself"** (དུག་དང་མེ་དང་མཚོན་ལྟ་བུ། རང་གི་བྱས་པ་ངན་པ་ཡིན།) → one's own wrongdoing is the first among all enemies. The *Sutra of the Close Placement of Mindfulness* says that the bad actions one has done oneself harm and destroy like poison, fire and a blade, and are the first enemy among all the enemies of this world and the next.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Turning Point From Confession in General to Confession Object by Object

Verses 27–29 confess wrongdoing in general terms; from this verse on, the confession proceeds by distinguishing each object. Gyaltsab therefore opens a new division here with "third:," and His Holiness the Dalai Lama likewise states explicitly, "here the explanation proceeds object by object."

###### 2. The Three Objects — the Three Jewels (the Field of Good Qualities), Parents (the Field of Kindness Shown), the Guru (the Field of Both Good Qualities and Kindness Shown)

Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary sets out the distinction between these three fields directly.

###### 3. The Three Doors of the Offence — Harm Done Through Body, Speech and Mind by Way of the Afflictions

All the commentaries agree that the harm was set in motion by one or another of the afflictions — attachment, aversion, confusion and the rest — and came about through the three doors of body, speech and mind.

###### 4. Among Harms Done to the Three Jewels, Using or Stealing What Belongs to the Sangha Is an Especially Grave Fault

Kunzang Pelden shows this with many accounts, together with the advice: "What belongs to the Sangha is like poison; but for poison there is an antidote, while for the Sangha's property there is none."

###### 5. Illustrations of the Offence — Harming Images and Scriptural Volumes; Beating Parents and Gurus and Speaking Harshly to Them

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk sets out detailed illustrations of harm through the three doors of body, speech and mind.

###### 6. ⚑ A Difference in How the Scope of "Others" Is Explained

Most gather "other gurus" into the class of gurus — abbots, preceptors and the like — while Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary and His Holiness the Dalai Lama read it as also including other beings in general: the sick, those with no protector and others who have become objects of compassion.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** From this verse on, the confession of wrongdoing shifts from the general to a confession made object by object; and it states the pledge to confess all the harm done, by way of the afflictions and through body, speech and mind, to the Three Jewels, to parents, to gurus and to others.

**Key points.**

- Both Gyaltsab and His Holiness the Dalai Lama make it explicit that this is the turning point where the confession of wrongdoing moves from the general mode to the mode of distinguishing each object.
- The three objects are the field of good qualities, the Three Jewels; the field of kindness shown to one, one's parents; and the field of both good qualities and kindness shown, the guru. Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary draws the distinction between these three fields.
- Kunzang Pelden makes it clear that using or stealing what belongs to the Sangha is an exceedingly grave fault, and conveys this through many accounts, such as the adept Kyergangpa and the worm in the cesspit.
- ⚑ There is a reading that restricts the object named by "others" to some further guru, such as an abbot or preceptor; Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary and His Holiness the Dalai Lama, however, take it to cover other beings in general, explaining that beings who suffer are objects of compassion in particular.
- To establish the gravity of harming the Three Jewels, Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites many passages, among them Maitreya's line "Whoever's mind is hostile to the Dharma — how could there be liberation for such a one?" and the confession verses of the *Golden Light Sūtra*.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Here the explanation proceeds object by object. Relying on the Three Jewels as the object; and further on parents and gurus; and on "others" — the field of those who suffer: beings who are sick, beings with no protector, beings tormented by hunger and thirst, beings in suffering who are especially objects of compassion, those in a lowly state. Relying on all of them, and under the power of the afflictions in one's own mind — the three of attachment, aversion and confusion — the way karma comes to expression is this: what is accumulated by way of the body, what is accumulated by way of speech, and what is accumulated by way of the mind. In essence: whatever act of harming others I have committed — I who from beginningless time have been steeped in many faults, a wrongdoer who falls into error even while knowing better, deeply confused, with an untamed mind, coarse and stiff and unyielding, a great and lowly sinner — such wrongdoing being not a matter of a mere few items but exceedingly inexhaustible; or, if it is applied to the ripening, wrongdoing that produces an exceedingly unbearable ripening; or, if it is counted by its kinds, wrongdoing without end. All the wrongful action I have thus accumulated, I confess from the very depths of my heart before the guides.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-31]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

So then: whatever wrongdoing I have committed — I, a wrongdoer, an old hand at wrongdoing, in whose mind the flaws of the many faults of the three doors have settled and remain like rust on iron — whatever gravely faulty and unbearable bad action that leads to birth in the hells and the like: all of that I acknowledge one by one and confess before the guides, the buddhas together with their heirs. And the reason it must be confessed quickly is that there is no telling when death will come and no certainty about its circumstances, so there is no certainty that one will not die this very day. And if one does die, the suffering of the severing of the vital points at the moment of death, the suffering of the intermediate state after death, and all the sufferings of the lower realms in the next life arise from their cause, wrongdoing and non-virtue. Therefore, he explains, one must generate regret for the wrongdoing one has already done.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whatever harmful or faulty action I in my confusion have committed — having in view the objects: the special field of unsurpassed good qualities, the Three Jewels; the field of kindness shown to me, my father and mother; or the field of graciousness, the guru, that is, abbots and preceptors, or any others whatsoever — set in motion by its cause, the three poisons of the afflictions, and carried out through body, speech and mind, the bases from which wrongdoing arises: because such action has the function of producing a vast and unbearable ripening, it becomes the cause of the sufferings of samsara in general and of the lower realms in particular, and it obstructs the path to the higher realms and to liberation. In that respect it is being "stained by the flaws of many faults." Or else, read this way: whatever non-virtuous act of wrongdoing I have committed — I, a wrongdoer stained by the flaws of the many faults of the afflictions, attachment and the rest, the causes that generate the sufferings of the hells and so on — wrongdoing exceedingly heavy and, because it generates fierce and long-lasting suffering such as that of the Hell of Unrelenting Torment, exceedingly unbearable: all of that I acknowledge one by one and confess in the presence of the objects of confession, the compassionate guides who lead beings out of the lower realms and out of samsara to the states of the higher realms and of liberation.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Whatever I as a person have done, from beginningless former lives up to this present life, in whatever way it happened — to the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha, the Three Jewels in their three kinds; and to my kind father and mother, or to gurus and spiritual friends, abbots, preceptors and others — through whichever of the afflictions applied: desire, anger, pride, envy and rivalry, ill will and the rest. And "through body, speech and mind" means this. By way of the body, for example: with regard to the Jewel of the Buddha among the Three Jewels, damaging its supports, the painted images and so on. Likewise, by way of speech, mocking and reviling; by way of the mind, holding wrong views or having no faith; and scattering all the kinds of scriptural volumes and letters that are the support of the Jewel of the sublime Dharma, or burning them in fire, or defiling them with filth; or tearing down and doing away with the practice centres, teaching colleges and ritual liturgies by which the sublime Dharma is accomplished; and beating and striking all the members of the Sangha, taking their lives, seizing their robes and religious garments, stealing or smashing the utensils gathered for the Sangha's meals, ruining them altogether, and so on. In short, whatever harm has been done to the Three Jewels through body, speech and mind — and, in the same way, to one's own father and mother: beating them with the body, abusing them with speech, harming them with the mind through evil intent; and to gurus, abbots, preceptors and spiritual friends too, beating them through the body, mocking and reviling them through speech, losing faith in them through the mind, and whatever such offences there have been — all of these are "many faults," that is, exceedingly numerous. Since a person who is a wrongdoer, stained by many flaws, may well carry a great many such wrongdoings in his own mind, therefore: whatever exceedingly unbearable and harmful wrongdoing of mine there is, all of it, with fierce remorse and regret, I confess in the presence of the perfect buddhas — those who lead all the beings of the three realms out of the abode of samsara to liberation — together with their heart-heirs, the bodhisattvas. So he explains the confession.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of the Rākṣasa Child

In the house of a certain brahmin there lived a creature not of human kind — utterly savage, of low birth, ugly of face, with the appearance of a crippled monkey. He had a very beautiful wife, and he beat and thrashed her constantly. One day, when she had not swept the house, he set about killing her. Terrified and trembling, she fled, but no one was able to protect her; then the brahmin, filled with great compassion, gave her refuge. When the rākṣasa child saw the brahmin's splendour he was struck with terror and begged, "Look on me with compassion; I come to you for refuge." The brahmin told him: "You obtained this wretched body because of bad actions you have done; but if fierce regret arises in you, that will be for your happiness." At this the rākṣasa child felt violent regret: he tore out his hair, wept tears that fell as blood, grieved that his bad actions had ripened upon him, and prayed that his former actions might ripen here and now. So he made his confession — and immediately his body became beautiful, his voice sweet and his form pleasing, and his former ugly appearance was gone. This is set out as an illustration for the line of this verse "I confess all of it now, before every buddha who has ever guided beings": if faults are confessed with fierce regret, they are swiftly purified.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. What Is to Be Confessed Is the Many Faults Accumulated Through the Three Doors

All the commentaries agree in the end that the wrongdoing to be confessed is the harm done, by way of the afflictions and through the three of body, speech and mind, to the Three Jewels and to one's parents or other teachers — wrongdoing stained by the flaws of many faults and exceedingly unbearable.

###### 2. Wrongdoing Settles in the Mind and Remains There Like Rust on Iron

Kunzang Pelden gives the illustration of the flaws of many faults settling in the mind and remaining like rust on iron, showing that the ripening of wrongdoing is not discarded anywhere along the way.

###### 3. "Exceedingly Unbearable" Means That It Delivers a Vast and Unbearable Ripening

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that such wrongdoing is exceedingly unbearable because it becomes the cause of the sufferings of samsara in general and of the lower realms in particular, and obstructs the path to the higher realms and to liberation; and he settles the point with scriptural passages from the *Golden Light Sūtra* and other sutras.

###### 4. The Object of Confession Is the Compassionate Guides

Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain "the guides," the object of confession, as the perfect buddhas who lead beings out of the lower realms and out of samsara to the states of the higher realms and of liberation, together with their heart-heirs, the bodhisattvas.

###### 5. A Difference Over Which Field the Object of Confession Is — ⚑

Ngulchu Thogme and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain the "others" of "my parents, or any other teacher" as further members of the class of gurus, abbots and preceptors and the like; His Holiness the Dalai Lama, however, also includes beings in a lowly state who suffer, the sick and those with no protector, who have become objects of compassion.

###### 6. The Reason Confession Must Be Made Quickly, With Fierce Regret, Is That Death Is Uncertain

Kunzang Pelden explains that, since there is no telling when death will come and no certainty about its circumstances, one must confess quickly, before the ripening of wrongdoing brings the sufferings of the lower realms.

###### 7. The Method of Confessing Wrongdoing Is Fierce Regret

The story of the rākṣasa child is given as an illustration: though he had obtained the face of a rākṣasa through bad actions, when he confessed with fierce regret his body immediately became beautiful and his voice sweet.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

If one asks what the reason is for treating wrongdoing as so serious and confessing it with fierce regret in this way: the fruit of wrongdoing is nothing but suffering, and since we do not want suffering, that is precisely why we must see wrongdoing as a danger. The flaw of wrongdoing is suffering; because we do not want suffering, we must refrain from wrongdoing; and for what has already been accumulated there is only one method — to make confession. And if one asks why confession must be made right now, without putting it off: "before I have cleared away this wrongdoing" — that is, before the wrongdoing has been exhausted and cleared away through confession and restraint — I may perish; I may die. What certainty is there that I will not die? If no other agent is involved, "perish" means dying a natural death; if there is one, it means being killed. Hence: "Please protect me quickly, so I can truly be freed from it in time." Since the time of death is uncertain, it is settled that whoever is born will die in the end; and when death will come is also uncertain. People die in car accidents, for instance: the circumstances of death are uncertain. And even where someone comes forward to underwrite a person's life and bodily safety by providing medical support and the like, if you searched the three worlds you would not find anyone able to give a hundred-per-cent guarantee against dying. So the time of death is uncertain. Since there is no telling when death will come, if some sudden circumstance of death arises and one has not managed to purify one's wrongdoing before it, then it is settled that one will go to the lower realms in the next life. On that ground I confess my wrongdoing without delay.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-32]]

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

If from now on I do not exert myself, with the intention to refrain, in the antidote that is virtuous action, then "before I have cleared away this wrongdoing" — before the bad actions I have done have been cleared away by confession — I may perish; that is, I will come to die. And if I die without having confessed my wrongdoing, I will undergo the sufferings of the lower realms and the rest. Therefore: by whatever means my own mind may certainly be freed from this wrongdoing, please protect me, swiftly and without delay.

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If the wrongdoing is not confessed at once, then before it has been cleared away — while the wrongdoing is still with me — the Lord of Death may make me perish, and I will be born in the lower realms. So, by whatever means may be found for me to be certainly freed from this wrongdoing, please protect me quickly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-32]]

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Why does one make the effort to confess? Because if I perish before the wrongdoing has been cleared away, then I would go to the lower realms; so, by whatever means may be found for me to be certainly freed from this wrongdoing, please protect me quickly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-32]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

If it is not confessed, then before the actions of wrongdoing have been cleared away and exhausted I will perish quickly, with no leisure left to stay — I will die. And if I die without the wrongdoing having been cleared away, I will undergo fierce suffering in the next life. Therefore, by whatever means may be found for me to be certainly freed from this wrongdoing, which binds me in samsara, please guard and protect me from this wrongdoing together with its fruit, swiftly and without delay.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-32]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

As for that: if the act of wrongdoing is not confessed quickly, as soon as it is done, then before it has been cleared away — before it has been exhausted by confession — I will perish quickly, that is, I will come to die, with my mind still stained by wrongdoing and downfalls and still bearing the fault. And if I die like that, before the wrongdoing has been confessed, I am certain to be born in the lower realms and to undergo fierce suffering. Therefore, by whatever means may be found for me to be certainly and inevitably freed from the cause, this act of wrongdoing, and from the fruit, the suffering of the lower realms — and since it is hard to be freed from them — in order to be free of them, please protect me from this non-virtuous action together with its fruit, in the swiftest possible way. So, in fear of wrongdoing and of suffering, he seeks refuge in the Three Jewels.

Some explain this verse as showing the reason why refuge must be sought, applying it to the power of the support, whereas most commentaries explain it as the power of remorse — generating regret out of fear of dying while still carrying one's wrongdoing. Yet the two do not conflict, since it is with regret that one seeks refuge.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-32]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Again, generating the wish to confess wrongdoing at once and to be parted from it: I myself, before the wrongdoing, the non-virtuous obscurations and the faults that I have accumulated up to now through body, speech and mind have been purified or cleared away — before that — may perish; that is, I may come to die. Saying this in respectful terms amounts to saying, "there is a danger that I will die." Since life is impermanent, there is the danger of dying before the wrongdoing has been purified, and so he says "I may perish." And then: in order that I may certainly be freed from all the wrongdoing I have previously accumulated, may you buddhas and bodhisattvas, my sublime places of refuge, purify all this wrongdoing of mine — the wrongdoing of a person marked by wrongdoing — swiftly, and protect me from it. Thus he prays, insistently.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-32]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **Dying in a car accident** (རླངས་འཁོར་བརྡབ་སྐྱོན་ཤོར་ནས་ཤི་བ།) → the fact that the time of death is uncertain. His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives the example of a sudden circumstance of death, such as dying in a car accident, to show that although it is certain that everyone born will die in the end, when death will come is not certain. He adds that even where medical support is available for a person's life and bodily safety, no one at all can give a hundred-per-cent guarantee against dying — and he applies this as the reason why wrongdoing must be confessed without delay: because if a sudden circumstance of death arises before one has managed to purify one's wrongdoing, it is settled that one will go to the lower realms in the next life.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Since One May Die Before the Wrongdoing Has Been Cleared Away, One Prays to Be Protected Quickly

The main point of this verse is that the Lord of Death may make one perish before the wrongdoing has been confessed and cleared away, and so one prays to be protected quickly, so as to be certainly freed from this wrongdoing.

###### 2. The Danger of Dying Without Having Purified One's Wrongdoing: Birth in the Lower Realms and Fierce Suffering

Many commentaries make it clear that the reason it must be confessed at once is precisely that, if one dies without confessing, one will undergo the sufferings of the lower realms and the rest.

###### 3. The Reason the Time for Purifying Wrongdoing Must Be Now: The Time of Death Is Uncertain

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that, since life is impermanent, there is the danger of dying before the wrongdoing has been purified; and His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains in detail, with the example of the car, why the time of death cannot be known.

###### 4. The Wrongdoing in Question Has Been Accumulated From Beginningless Time

In his annotation commentary Zhenphen Jampé Gocha makes it explicit that the acts of wrongdoing I have accumulated were accumulated from beginningless time.

###### 5. The Prayer Is Addressed to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas as Places of Refuge

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that one prays insistently to the buddhas and bodhisattvas, the sublime places of refuge, to be protected from wrongdoing swiftly.

###### 6. A Disagreement About Where This Verse Belongs in the Outline — the Power of the Support or the Power of Remorse

⚑ — Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that some commentators take it as the power of the support, showing the reason why refuge must be sought, while most commentaries take it as the power of remorse, generating regret out of fear of dying while still carrying one's wrongdoing; and he explains that the two do not conflict.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** All the commentaries explain this verse in the same way: since the Lord of Death may make one perish before the wrongdoing accumulated from beginningless time has been cleared away, and since dying like that brings the danger of being born in the lower realms and undergoing fierce suffering, one asks the buddhas and bodhisattvas to protect one quickly, as the means of being certainly freed from this wrongdoing.

**Key points.**

- Since one may die before the wrongdoing has been confessed and cleared away, one prays to be protected quickly so as to be certainly freed from it.
- If one dies without having purified one's wrongdoing, there is a great danger of being born in the lower realms and undergoing fierce suffering.
- The time of death is explained to be uncertain; His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives the example of the car and makes clear the reason why wrongdoing must be confessed without delay.
- The object of the prayer is the buddhas and bodhisattvas, the sublime places of refuge.
- There is disagreement about where this verse belongs in the outline. ⚑ Some explain it as the power of the support, most commentaries as the power of remorse; Minyak Kunzang Sonam reconciles the two as not conflicting.
