## Today's Verse

> All of the things that I have used Will become objects of memory, As if experienced in a dream. I’ll never see what’s past again. ^2-36

> Even during this life, many of those I like and dislike have passed away. And yet the terrible misdeeds I’ve done for their sake remain before me. ^2-37

> Because I have not recognized That I, as well, am ephemeral, I have committed many wrongs Out of delusion, greed, and hatred. ^2-38

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 36 through 38 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. We treat our friends, our rivals, and our possessions as if they were going to stay, and we pile up wrongdoing for their sake. In fact every pleasure and every difficulty of this life passes the way last night's dream passes and becomes nothing but a memory, and we ourselves are here only briefly, with no fixed date of leaving. These verses show us that plainly, and urge us to drop the misdeeds that rest on pointless attachment and dislike.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

All the commentaries read verse 36 the same way, and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk is the one who makes it ordinary. Take last night's dream, he says: you ate food in it, you wore clothes, you sat comfortably at home, you had a fight with someone. You wake up and there is nothing left but a faint sense that you dreamt something. Now think about the early part of this life — the relatives and neighbours you ate and travelled with, the arguments you had. Look for them now and you find exactly the same faint trace. That is what everything we have used comes to.

Verse 37 puts the sting in it. The people we did wrong for are already gone, many within this one life. The wrongdoing is not. Kunzang Palden quotes *Advice to a King*: wherever a person is born, their actions follow them like a shadow.

The commentaries then answer a question the verse raises but does not settle — in what sense does a past misdeed "remain before me"? Not lying about somewhere waiting. Ngulchu Thokme calls it a latent imprint. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk says it stands before one's own mind in the manner of an imprint, and the Dalai Lama puts it as a trace or potential that has already been placed on the consciousness. The act is finished and cannot be repeated; what it deposited is carried. That is why the arrangement is so badly one-sided. The reason for the misdeed dissolves quickly, and the deposit does not.

Kunzang Palden adds a picture for when the deposit matures. A great person arriving somewhere is met and escorted by an entourage sent ahead. In the same way, he says, those carrying misdeeds are met and led on by Yama's agents, and those carrying virtue are met and led by devas and teachers. Verse 38 names what makes all of this avoidable and unavoided. Kunzang Palden, Sazang, and Minyak Kunsö all read "I am ephemeral" through the same image: we are like a guest, arrived without knowing where from and leaving without knowing where to. Sazang sharpens it — we came from a previous life having left everything, and we go to the next having left everything.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, remembering that my possessions and the people I like and dislike are as passing as a dream, I will let go of attachment or dislike the moment I notice it rising.

**Explanation:** _(Taming the mind)_ Attachment and dislike feel reasonable while they last, because whatever they are aimed at seems solid and important at that moment. Today I will test that. When I catch myself pulled toward something I want or hardened against someone I resent, I will pause and remember that this will pass the way last night's dream passed, and end up as a memory and nothing more. Then I will let the pull go and carry on with what I was doing. Practising this a few times in a day loosens the grip these reactions have on us and leaves the mind noticeably quieter.
