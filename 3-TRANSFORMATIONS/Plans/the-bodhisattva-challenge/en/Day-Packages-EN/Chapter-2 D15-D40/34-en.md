<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Terrified by such fear as has just been described, I shall offer myself to the bodhisattva Samantabhadra as well. In the same way, to the noble Manjushri too I shall offer this body of mine. This is not like being made to hand over one's body against one's will at someone else's bidding: it is an offering taken up gladly, out of one's own wish.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-49]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Utterly terrified by the fear of the moment of death, by the fear of the intermediate state once one has died, and by the fear of samsara and the lower realms in the life to come, one must cry out — "Samantabhadra, supreme noble one, foremost among the heirs of all the victorious ones!" — and call to him: "To you I offer my own body and everything I have." Otherwise, if one cries out like this with no real fear or terror behind it, one is lying to the victorious ones and their heirs, and it would only embarrass them, he says. In the same way, to the protector Manjushri, the one father of all the victorious ones, I shall offer this body of mine — myself, unprompted by anyone else.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **Terrified by fear, one offers oneself to Samantabhadra and Manjushri.** — All the commentaries explain in agreement that, utterly terrified by the suffering of samsara and the lower realms (some add the fear of the moment of death and of the intermediate state), one offers one's own body — together with everything one has — to Samantabhadra, the buddhas' heir who has the power of aspiration-prayer, and to Manjushri.
2. **The offering to Manjushri is made of one's own wish, unprompted by anyone else.** — Kunzang Pelden, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and His Holiness the Dalai Lama all state plainly that this body is offered not at someone else's bidding, but out of one's own faith.
3. **The cry for help has to come from real fear.** — Kunzang Pelden explains that the cry with which one offers body and possessions to Samantabhadra must be made out of genuine terror; to cry out otherwise, with no real fear behind it, is to lie to the victorious ones and their heirs, and it would only embarrass them.
4. **What Samantabhadra and Manjushri are.** — Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk states plainly that Samantabhadra is the embodiment of the fulfilled aspiration-prayers of all the buddhas and that Manjushri is the wisdom body of all the buddhas; and he explains that body and mind are offered in a way that holds nothing back.
5. **This verse opens the section on going for refuge to the bodhisattvas who have the power of aspiration-prayer.** — Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that going for refuge to the bodhisattvas who have the power of aspiration-prayer means asking for refuge from the foremost among the bodhisattvas by offering them one's body. Ngulchu Thogme likewise marks off the passage from this verse through 2-52 as the section on going for refuge to the bodhisattvas who have the power of aspiration-prayer.
6. **Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains verses 2-49 and 2-50 as a pair.** — He joins them together: after offering one's body to Samantabhadra and Manjushri, one also cries out in a wretched voice for refuge to Avalokiteshvara, the protector whose compassionate conduct never goes astray.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse states the commitment made while utterly terrified by the suffering of samsara and the lower realms: to offer one's own body — together with everything one has — of one's own wish and unprompted by anyone else, to Samantabhadra, the buddhas' heir who has the power of aspiration-prayer, and to the venerable Manjushri. It opens the section on going for refuge to the bodhisattvas who have the power of aspiration-prayer.

**Key points.**

- The reason for the fear is the suffering of samsara and the lower realms; some commentators add the fear of the moment of death and of the intermediate state.
- Both offerings are described: body and possessions offered to Samantabhadra, and the body offered to Manjushri. Many commentaries state plainly that the offering to Manjushri is made by oneself, unprompted by anyone else.
- Kunzang Pelden explains that the cry for help must come out of genuine terror; to cry out otherwise is to lie to the victorious ones and their heirs.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk glosses Samantabhadra and Manjushri separately, and explains that the offering is made in a way that holds nothing back.
- Ngulchu Thogme and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both gather this verse through 2-52 into the section on going for refuge to the bodhisattvas who have the power of aspiration-prayer, while Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains 2-49 and 2-50 as a pair.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

And so, to Avalokiteshvara, the protector of beings, whose intention and conduct in working for others out of great compassion never go astray even in the slightest — to him too, by my lament, that is, by a wretched voice, or by crying out with all my strength, I pray: protect me, who have done great wrong.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The noble Great Compassionate One, from the time he first generated the awakening mind in the presence of the Buddha Ratnagarbha, has held great compassion for limitless beings. Since he possesses that great compassion, and since he acts for the welfare of others alone, without so much as a hair's tip of self-interested attachment, his inner quality of compassion and his outer conduct never go astray — that is, they never contradict one another. And because at all times and in every way he looks upon all beings without ever closing his eyes, his very name accords with the meaning: "He Whose Eyes Do Not Close," or Avalokiteshvara, the protector. To him too one calls out: with a trust that does not turn back, with longing and in utter wretchedness, not with deceit and not with the mouth only but from the marrow of one's bones; frightened by the fear of samsara and the lower realms in general, and, when the need presses close, by the fear of dying and of the intermediate state; worn down by suffering and utterly wretched, one raises the voice high, cries out with all one's strength, gives a great shout, and calls upon the noble Great Compassionate One. "I, who have done wrong" — that is, in body and speech and above all in mind, and more than that: as it is said, all bad actions come from conceptual thought; all conceptual thought comes from the grasping at self that clings to "I" and "mine," to self and other; and since grasping at self is ignorance, at all times and in every way — while teaching the Dharma or listening to it, while reciting, while meditating and so on — one has not a single instant of mind to spare, but follows after bad thoughts such as attachment and aversion, so that delusion spreads without limit, bad karma is accumulated, and one goes on endlessly through samsara into the three lower realms and the rest. Acknowledging that this is exactly what one is, one prays: "I, whose actions are bad, please protect me."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-50]]

To that protector — the very embodiment of great compassion, whose intention and conduct in working for others out of great compassion never go astray even in the slightest, and who with his loving eyes constantly watches over and sustains beings in their wretchedness — to him too, wretched and panic-stricken with the fear of suffering, one starts up, raises the voice high, calls him by name and cries out: "I, who have done great wrong, who am at the point of death and certain to undergo the fear of the lower realms — protect me." <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **The point of crying out to Avalokiteshvara for refuge.** — All the commentaries agree in the end that, frightened by the suffering of samsara and the lower realms, by the fear of death and by the fear of the intermediate state, one cries out in a wretched voice to Avalokiteshvara, the protector whose compassionate conduct never goes astray, praying: "Protect me, who have done wrong."
2. **What "compassionate conduct that never goes astray" means — acting for others with no thread of self-interest.** — It refers to one who, possessing great compassion, works for the welfare of others alone, without so much as a hair's tip of self-interested attachment, so that the inner quality of compassion and the outer conduct never contradict one another. It is also explained as compassionate conduct that works for the welfare of others both spontaneously and without interruption.
3. **Avalokiteshvara is the compassion of all the buddhas gathered into a single embodiment.** — Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that Avalokiteshvara the protector acts solely for the welfare of others with a great compassion that has no reference point, is free of the stain of the delusion that thinks of one's own benefit, and is the compassion of all the buddhas gathered into one embodiment.
4. **The cry is not put on: it comes from the marrow of one's bones.** — Kunzang Pelden explains clearly that this cry for refuge is not mere talk or pretence, but a cry that breaks out from the marrow of one's bones under the force of being worn down by suffering and utterly wretched.
5. **What "I, who have done wrong" means — a person who, driven by the ignorance of self-grasping, has accumulated bad karma and wanders in samsara.** — All bad actions come from conceptual thought, and all conceptual thought comes from the ignorance of self-grasping; so, following after the bad afflictions, one accumulates bad karma and goes to the lower realms and the rest. Acknowledging that this is what one is, one seeks refuge.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows the manner of crying out to the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara for refuge. Frightened by the suffering of samsara and the lower realms and by the fear of death and of the intermediate state, one cries out in a wretched voice to Avalokiteshvara, the protector whose compassionate conduct — untouched by any concern for himself — never goes astray, and prays: "Protect me, who have done wrong." All the commentaries explain this in the same way.

**Key points.**

- The reasons for crying out for refuge are three: the suffering of samsara and the lower realms, the fear of death, and the fear of the intermediate state.
- "Compassionate conduct that never goes astray" indicates that Avalokiteshvara's inner compassion and his outer working for others never contradict one another, and that this activity is spontaneous and uninterrupted.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that Avalokiteshvara the protector is the compassion of all the buddhas gathered into a single embodiment.
- The cry is not mere talk or pretence but a cry that breaks out from the marrow of the bones; and "I, who have done wrong" means a person who, driven by the ignorance of self-grasping, has accumulated bad karma.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam also cites another great commentary, the *Dar ṭīkā*, as his source for the phrase "whose compassionate conduct never goes astray."
