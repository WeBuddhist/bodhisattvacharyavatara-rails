## Today's Verse

> The Lord of Death, untrustworthy, Won’t tarry for what’s done or undone. So no one, whether or not they’re ill, Should place their trust in fleeting life. ^2-33

> I must leave all behind and go, But I have not yet recognized that. For the sake of those I like or dislike, I have done various misdeeds. ^2-34

> Those I don’t like will cease to be, And those I like will cease to be. I myself will cease to be, And everyone will cease to be. ^2-35

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 33 through 35 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. We live as though this life will hold, and we spend our days pushing away the people we count as enemies and holding tight to the ones we count as friends. But death does not wait for our work to be finished, and when it comes we leave friends, enemies, and everything we own behind and go on alone. These verses are a sharp reminder of that, and an encouragement to set down attachment and aversion that lead nowhere and take up something that is actually worth the day.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

The commentaries first take away the assumption that we will get some warning. Kunzang Palden compares this life to sunlight through a gap in the clouds, and to a lamp standing in the wind. He also gives a number that lands harder than any image: for every one person who dies after a long illness, a hundred people in good health die suddenly. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk adds the mayfly that lives only until evening and frost sitting on a stone, and Minyak Kunsö the hailstone that arrives on your head without being thought about. What they all say is the point of verse 33: the Lord of Death does not check whether your business is finished.

Then a story, from the commentary on renunciation. A householder named Gautama in central India had a young son, Netso. The boy watched people take a beehive. The bees that had been gathering the honey were killed and the rest fled, and the hive was simply gone. He said to himself that his father's household and all its wealth was no different from a broken hive, and something in him gave way. When his father spoke to him, the boy answered that the body is like dew, about to fall, and the breath is like mist. Father and son gave away everything they had and turned to practice.

Minyak Kunsö gives the image for the leaving itself. We go like a hair drawn out of butter: the hair comes out and nothing that surrounded it comes with it. Kunzang Palden spells out how far "leave all behind" reaches — land, region, relatives, friends, household, spouse, wealth, and finally your own food, clothes, and body.

Verse 35 then removes the last foothold. Kunzang Palden lists the positions people hold and how they end: high as the sky, mighty as lightning, rich as a naga, beautiful as a god, prosperous as a rainbow, and all of it gone, with the piled-up fortress and the gathered wealth and the parents and brothers and sisters. The Dalai Lama gives the mechanism plainly: relatives and companions are gathered right now only by their own temporary conditions, the way clouds gather suddenly, and when the conditions change they disperse. Gyaltsab and Sazang draw the conclusion Shantideva is confessing. Both the friend and the enemy end, and so do we, so the misdeeds we did for either were pointless from the start.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, when anger rises toward someone, I will remember that both he and I will die, and let the hatred go.

**Explanation:** _(Taming the mind)_ Because we quietly assume we will be here indefinitely, we treat our dislikes as permanent business and spend real energy on them, gathering harm along the way. Today I will use these verses as a check. When someone irritates me and I feel the reaction building, I will remember one plain fact: he will die and so will I, and this particular argument will not survive either of us. That thought is not meant to make the day feel grim. It shrinks the grievance to its real size, so it stops governing me and I can take charge of my own mind instead.
