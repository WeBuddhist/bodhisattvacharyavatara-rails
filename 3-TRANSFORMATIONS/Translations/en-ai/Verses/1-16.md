## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three commentators reach an absolute consensus on the meaning of Verse 1-16, which uses the classical analogy of a journey to illustrate the distinction between the aspiring mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-praṇidhi-citta*) and the engaging mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-prasthāna-citta*). They explain that while both minds share the same ultimate goal of seeking Buddhahood for the sake of all sentient beings, they are distinguished by whether or not they are conjoined with actual practice.

### 1. The Analogy of the Journey
The commentators map the two aspects of the mind of enlightenment to the stages of travel:
*   **The Aspiring Mind (Wishing to Travel):** 
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** describes this as the mind that wishes to go to a destination while still remaining in bed, without yet being engaged in the physical action of going.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** similarly defines it as the state of a person who merely desires and seeks to travel to a desired destination.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** explains it as the wishing attitude of wanting to go to a certain place.
*   **The Engaging Mind (Actually Traveling):**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** describes this as the mind of someone who is actually engaged in the action of going, such as actively lifting and placing down one's feet.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** defines it as the state of one who has entered the road and is actually traveling.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** defines it as actually traveling there, entering the path that leads to Buddhahood, and wishing to practice that path.

### 2. The Critical Distinction: Conjoining with Practice
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** clarifies that the aspiring mind represents the desire to travel to great awakening, whereas the engaging mind is characterized by entering the path of the six perfections (*pāramitās*) and actually traveling upon it.
*   **Gyaltsab Je** explains that although both minds are identical in their core thought—wanting to attain the state of a Buddha for the sake of others—they are distinguished by whether they belong to the type that does not depend on being conjoined with the practice of the perfections (such as generosity), or the type that does depend on it.

### 3. Scholastic Consensus and Lineage Authority
*   **Gyaltsab Je** emphasizes that this explanation of the two minds is not an isolated interpretation, noting that the assertions of other wise Indian masters, such as Master Kamalaśīla (in his *Bhāvanākrama*), are in perfect agreement and share this same understanding.
*   **Ngulchu Thokme** concludes that those wise ones who wish to accomplish the goals of themselves and others must understand this distinction respectively, just as they understand the difference between the two analogies.
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** similarly highlights that "the wise who unerringly realize the way things are" should recognize this distinction.
