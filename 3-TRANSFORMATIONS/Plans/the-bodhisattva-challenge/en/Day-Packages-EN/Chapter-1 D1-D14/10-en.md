# Day 10 — We want to be happy, and run the other way

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:notification -->
### Notification

**Title:** Name what you were really reaching for  
**Detail:** We chase happiness and, confused, run from it. Today, name what you actually wanted.

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's verses say something striking: just wishing to help others is worth more than the grandest gifts laid before the buddhas. Then they turn to something sad. People want to be happy, yet keep walking straight into what hurts them, not because they want pain, but because they are confused about what leads where.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These three verses show that the awakening mind is like a wish-fulfilling jewel, and like the finest medicine, the nectar that removes all the suffering of beings. If merely wishing to help beings brings more merit than making offerings to countless Buddhas, then think how much greater it is to actually work for the good of others.

Why do beings need such compassion and such an awakening mind? Because, even though they do not want suffering, they rush toward harmful actions like moths flying into a flame. And even though they want happiness, out of confusion and ignorance they destroy the very causes of their own happiness, as if those causes were enemies. For these beings — whose thoughts and actions are both so mixed-up — there is no refuge anywhere greater than compassion and the awakening mind.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice**: Today, I will stop doing one habit that I know causes me suffering.

**Explanation**: Today's verse explains that even though people want to escape suffering, they often run right toward it. Out of confusion, we accidentally destroy our own happiness as if it were an enemy. By choosing to stop one harmful habit today, you are letting go of this confusion and using wisdom to protect your own joy.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### kunpal — Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

That [mind] is the **cause** from which arises all the temporary and ultimate **joy and happiness** of the boundless host of **beings,** and it has become the great medicine, the healing **essence,** that removes without remainder the illness of the **suffering** of **all beings.** As for **whatever merit** or benefit there is of this **precious mind** of awakening — one cannot say "it is only this much"; **how could it be measured?** For the limits of space, the number of beings, the varieties of suffering, and the qualities of a buddha cannot be measured [either].

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### gyaltsab — Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If the benefit of the aspiring [mind] is exceedingly great, the [mind] seized by the deeds of practice is more excellent still: it brings all beings to attain the happiness of higher states and of liberation, and so it is the cause of joy; and it has become the medicine-essence that heals the illness of the suffering of absolutely all beings. Whatever merit there is of this mind, like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel — how could it be measured? Because it is boundless. As is taught extensively in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, where the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* is cited, this should be understood accordingly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### ngulchu-thogmed — Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-26 and 1-27 are explained together; since 1-26 is the first verse with complete wording, the full count-explanation is given here. See Verse 1-27 for the cross-reference.]*

Whatever merit there is of the precious mind that has become the cause of joy of all beings and the medicine-essence that removes the illness of beings' suffering — how could it be measured? As the *Sūtra Requested by Śrīdatta* ⚑ says: "Whatever the merit of bodhicitta — if it had form, it would fill the whole realm of space, and be greater even than that." And that merely wishing to benefit beings surpasses making offerings to the buddhas — the *King of Samādhi Sūtra* says: "Whatever the countless, measureless offerings there are, in fields filled with billions upon billions [of buddhas], offered daily and always to the supreme beings — they do not amount even to a fraction of a mind of love." If so, then what need is there to speak of one who strives to accomplish the happiness of absolutely all beings — how far more excellent that is!

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### sabzang — Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Making itself the cause from which arise, now and lastingly, the joy and happiness of absolutely all beings, and becoming the great medicine or healing essence that removes all the illness of beings' samsaric and lower-realm suffering — like a precious jewel that clears away all poverty — whatever merit there is of the mind of awakening, how could it be measured by signs, examples, numbers, and the like? Since it cannot, one attains merit as boundless as space.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### minyak-kunzang-sonam — Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Making itself the cause from which arise the joy of the higher states, at the level of every being without exception, and the ultimate bliss and mental happiness of supreme liberation; and becoming the great medicine-essence that removes without remainder the illness of the samsaric and lower-realm suffering and mental unease of absolutely all beings; and, like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel of great value that clears away all poverty and yields every need and wish — whatever merit there is of the mind of awakening, how could its number or measure be gauged? Since it cannot, one attains merit beyond number and measure. (The great commentary says that this scripture should be applied as showing the benefit of the engaging mind.) In any case, this shows bodhicitta itself to be the cause of both higher states and definite goodness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### khenpo-kunga — Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This generating of bodhicitta is the cause by which happiness — or joy — is attained in the body and mind of all beings, high, low, and middling. And for any being tormented by the sufferings of birth, aging, sickness, and death; afflicted by illnesses of wind, bile, phlegm, and so on; and beset by heat, cold, hunger, thirst, dull confusion, exploitation, and the like — [bodhicitta] has become, among all medicines, the natural essence, like a deathless-reviving nectar, that pacifies all the suffering in their body and mind. As for whatever merit there is of this generating of the precious mind of awakening, if one had to fix its measure — saying "it is just this much" — how could the intellect measure it? The meaning is that no measure can be set.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### tenzin-gyatso — Gyalwa Tenzin Gyatso (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus [bodhicitta] is the cause, or means, by which absolutely all beings attain the temporary goal, higher states, and the ultimate goal, the station of definite goodness; it has become like a nectar or medicine that pacifies the suffering of beings equal to space; and it is the mind that cherishes others more than the precious self. Whatever merit there is of that mind — immeasurable, distinguished in four ways — how, by any example or measure, could it be gauged? The meaning is that there is no way to take its measure.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-26]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The wish-fulfilling jewel** (ཡིད་བཞིན་གྱི་ནོར་བུ།) — All commentaries (KKP, KS, GDR, SMPLG, MKS, NKW) apply this image: just as a wish-fulfilling jewel is the most exalted of all precious things, granting every wish and clearing away every want, so bodhicitta is the most precious of all minds — the cause of joy for all beings and the medicine for all their suffering.

- **The medicine-essence / deathless-reviving nectar** (སྨན་གྱི་རྩི / བདུད་རྩི་འཆི་གསོས) — Khenpo Kunga develops the image especially: bodhicitta is not just a medicine but like a "deathless-reviving nectar," the natural essence among all medicines, because it pacifies every bodily and mental suffering — birth, aging, sickness, death; illnesses of wind, bile, and phlegm; heat, cold, hunger, thirst, dull confusion, exploitation, and the rest.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **Merit without limit.** Verse 1-26 is the first scriptural-and-reasoned proof establishing that bodhicitta's merit is boundless: because it is the cause of joy for all beings and the medicine (medicine-essence) for all their suffering, the merit of that jewel-like (wish-fulfilling-jewel) mind cannot be measured by any standard. The *Sūtra Requested by Śrīdatta* also proves by scripture that this merit, if it had form, would exceed the whole realm of space.

2. **The distinction of engaging over aspiring.** As Gyaltsab explains clearly: even though the benefit of the aspiring mind (aspiration) is very great, the engaging mind seized by the practice of the six perfections (engagement) is more excellent still. This progressive reasoning lays the ground for the argument that continues into verse 1-27.

3. **The great medicine-essence.** Khenpo Kunga explains at depth all the kinds of suffering that bodhicitta pacifies: birth, aging, sickness, and death; the three of wind, bile, and phlegm; heat, cold, hunger, thirst, dull confusion, exploitation, and the rest. This enumeration makes vivid the verse's point that bodhicitta is the medicine-essence that benefits every suffering without remainder.

4. **Citing scripture.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam notes that some commentaries hold the *Śrīdatta* citation should be applied as proving the benefit of the aspiring mind, and the great commentary applies it so; this shows how a single scriptural passage can be enlisted in several differing lines of exposition.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** As the first of a fivefold set of benefits, this verse establishes bodhicitta's merit as limitless: it sets out that [bodhicitta] is the cause of joy for all beings and the medicine-essence for their suffering, and interprets this by the scripture of the *Sūtra Requested by Śrīdatta*.

**Key points.**

- With the images of the wish-fulfilling jewel and the medicine-essence, bodhicitta is shown to be the very heart of concern for the happiness and suffering of all beings.
- That deathless-reviving-nectar-like bodhicitta can remove all suffering — birth, aging, sickness, death, and the rest — is shown.
- Scripture establishes that if that merit had form, it would fill the whole realm of space and be greater even than that.

<!-- sub:metaphors -->
#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The benevolent thought surpasses offering** (ཕན་པར་བསམ་པ་ལས་མཆོད་པ་ཁྱད་འཕགས།) — The central line of reasoning in this verse is comparative: if merely the thought of benefiting beings surpasses making offerings to countless buddhas, then actually striving to bring all beings to buddhahood is certainly, immeasurably, more excellent. This "what need is there to speak?" reasoning appears alike in all seven commentaries.

- **Fields as many as ten-million-billion** (བྱེ་བ་ཁྲག་ཁྲིག་གི་ཞིང་།) — The measure of the offering cited from the *King of Samādhi Sūtra* is not just "many offerings," but various offerings presented daily and unceasingly in buddha-fields as numberless as the sands of the Ganges. Yet even a boundless heap of offering like that is outshone by a single instant of the mind of love.

- **The distinction of application over aspiration** (སྨོན་པ་ལས་སྦྱོར་བའི་ཁྱད་པར།) — Minyak Kunzang Sonam draws out a further distinction: the previous verse showed the merit of the aspiring mind (aspiration); this verse rises higher, to the engaging mind (engagement) — actually striving, by way of the six perfections, to free all beings. The bodhisattva of this verse is not merely aspiring but has entered upon actual application.

#### Main Teaching Points

1. **A reply to a doubt.** Verse 1-27 shows the reason for the boundless merit stated in verse 1-26. Khenpo Zhenga (KS) sets it out clearly as an answer to the doubt "how is this reasonable?": that even merely the thought of benefiting beings surpasses boundless offerings is itself the proof. So this verse is not mere praise but a genuine establishing argument.

2. **The distinction of engaging over the mere thought of love.** Kunzang Pelden and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both make the progressive argument clear: verse 1-27 first shows that merely the mind of love (the mere thought of benefit) surpasses offering, and then rises ("what need is there to speak?") to actually striving, by way of the six perfections, to free all beings (striving for their happiness). The "what need is there to speak?" movement within the verse establishes far more than the single [prior] point.

3. **Established by two scriptures.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites a separate scripture for each point: for the first (that the mere mind of love surpasses offering), the *King of Samādhi Sūtra*; for the second (that generating bodhicitta surpasses filling Ganges-sand fields with jewels), the *Sūtra Requested by Vīradatta*. This twofold scriptural proof keeps the reasoning grounded.

4. **Counsel to fit intention and application.** Sabzang makes it clear that the merit spoken of here belongs to a bodhicitta seized by the intention to protect all beings equal to space — aiming at happiness both temporary and ultimate. Distinguishing the phase of aspiration from the phase of actual engagement, this commentary clarifies that the final object of the verse's "what need is there to speak?" is the bodhisattva whose practice is complete.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** As the second of the fivefold set of benefits, this verse shows how bodhicitta becomes supreme over all offering: if merely the thought of benefit surpasses even boundless, unequalled offering, then what need is there to speak of one who actually strives for the happiness of all beings? It is interpreted by two scriptures.

**Key points.**

- To the doubt "how is this reasonable?", it is shown by scripture that even merely a mind of love has boundless merit.
- That the engaging bodhicitta surpasses the aspiring is established by one line of the instruction's method.
- By citing both the *King of Samādhi Sūtra* and the *Sūtra Requested by Vīradatta*, the two concordant proofs are set out.

### Verse 1-28

> **Rail source:** `2-RAILS/Verses/1-28-summary.md` &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Rail status:** `draft`

<!-- sub:root-verse -->
#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^1-28`):*

> People want to escape suffering,
> yet they run straight toward it.
> They want happiness, yet, confused,
> they destroy their own happiness as if it were their worst enemy.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Are not beings themselves skilled in the means of accomplishing happiness and abandoning suffering — so what need have they of a helper? No, they are not. **Though they have a mind that wishes to cast off suffering,** they **run straight toward** the very causes of **suffering** — killing and the rest — and engage in them; and **though they wish for happiness,** through the power of **confusion** they **destroy, as if it were an enemy, [the causes of] their own happiness.**

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### kunpal — Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Though all beings **have a mind that wishes to cast off** the sufferings of this life and the next — short lives, much illness, no resources, and the rest, which they do not want — with wrong desires and wrong conduct they take up the ten non-virtues, killing, stealing, and the rest, and so **run straight toward suffering** itself — like a moth flying into a butter-lamp flame. And likewise, **though they wish for** the happiness of this life and the next — long life, health, and the rest — being confused and deluded about its means, [about] what to adopt and what to reject, they not only fail to practice the ten virtues but commit all sorts of non-virtue, and so **destroy their own** happiness, of this life and the next, **as if they were looking at an enemy.**

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### gyaltsab — Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-28, 1-29, and 1-30 are explained together; 1-28 is the first verse, so the full grouped prose (covering all three verses) is given here.]*

Beings themselves want happiness and do not want suffering; and since, by striving accordingly, they can gain happiness and abandon suffering, are they not themselves skilled in adopting happiness and rejecting suffering? What need is there for another to strive on their behalf? — [One must] certainly strive. For although beings have a mind that wishes to cast off suffering, through the power of the afflictions they accomplish only the causes of suffering, and so run straight toward the very suffering of endless samsara and the lower realms; and though they wish for happiness, being confused and deluded about its means, they by their conduct destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy — that is, they enter wrongly upon the means of happiness and suffering. For that reason: as for whatever mind satisfies with every happiness of higher states and definite goodness those beings who are deprived of happiness and burdened with much suffering, confused about the means of happiness and suffering; and cuts off the whole stream of their suffering; and also dispels the confusion that is delusion about what to adopt and reject — where is there any other virtue equal to that mind? Where is there any other friend like it, who accomplishes all benefit and protects from all harm? Where is there any other merit like it, which shows unerringly the whole domain of what to adopt and reject? Since there is none, one should strive to generate the mind set on awakening.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### ngulchu-thogmed — Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-28, 1-29, and 1-30 are explained together; 1-28 is the first verse, so the full grouped prose is given here.]*

Whatever [mind there is that], toward those who — though they have a mind wishing to cast off suffering, run straight toward non-virtue, the very cause of suffering; and though they wish for happiness, through the confusion of not knowing its means abandon virtue, and so destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy; those deprived of happiness and burdened with much suffering — with great love satisfies them with every happiness, and with great compassion cuts off all their suffering, and with great wisdom dispels even the confusion of not knowing the causes of happiness and suffering: where is there any virtue equal to that? And where is there any such benefactor-friend? And where is there any such merit as the merit it produces?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### sabzang — Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Foolish beings, though they have a mind wishing to cast off suffering, for the sake of quelling the suffering of hunger and the rest commit non-virtue, killing and so on, and so run straight toward the very suffering of the hells and the rest — like a moth flying into a butter-lamp flame. And likewise, though they have a mind wishing to obtain some special happiness, being confused and deluded about its means, they engage in all sorts of non-virtue, and so destroy their own happiness as if they were looking at an enemy.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### minyak-kunzang-sonam — Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-28, 1-29, and 1-30 are explained together; 1-28 is the first verse, so the full grouped prose is given here.]*

As a way of resolving a doubt about the earlier "striving for [beings'] happiness": are not beings each skilled at accomplishing benefit and happiness and removing harm and suffering — so what need is there for another to strive at it? There is need. In deluded beings, though by intention a mind arises that does not want suffering and wishes to cast it off, through the power of the afflictions of delusion about karma and its results, being unskilled in the means of what to adopt and reject, they enter wrongly by way of their conduct: for instance, to quell the suffering of hunger and the like, they kill beings; and wishing for wealth, they steal — thus running straight toward the very non-virtue that is the cause of the suffering of the hells, hungry ghosts, and the rest, taking exactly that into practice, like a moth rushing into a flame. Likewise, though by intention they wish to obtain the happiness of higher states and liberation, being confused and deluded about the means of accomplishing it, they by their conduct abandon virtue, the cause of happiness, and put into practice only non-virtue, the cause of suffering — and so destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy. This is entering wrongly upon what to adopt and reject regarding the causes of happiness and suffering: both intention and conduct have gone wrong, that is, both wrong desire and wrong practice. To those deprived of happiness, [bodhicitta] with great love satisfies body and mind with every happiness of higher states and liberation; to those burdened with much suffering, with great compassion it cuts off the whole aching pain of samsaric and lower-realm suffering; and to those confused about what to adopt and reject regarding the causes of happiness and suffering, with great wisdom it teaches, exactly and unerringly — "this is to be done, this is not to be done" — and so dispels even their confusion. Where is there any virtue equal, in benefit, to that active mind of awakening? Where is there any such friend or companion, who wards off harm and accomplishes benefit and happiness? Where is there any heap of merit-and-virtue like it, which gives rise to a distinguished, delightful joy and happiness, temporary and lasting?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### khenpo-kunga — Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Those beings [wandering] in samsara have gone wrong in both desire and conduct. By their own intention, however much suffering there is in their own body and mind, they have a mind that wishes to cast it all off; yet the causes that produce suffering are, from life to life: killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle talk, covetousness, ill will, and wrong view. Not knowing to abandon these, they take them into practice and cultivate them, and so run straight toward the very cause of suffering. And the cause or condition by which those beings would gain their own happiness is precisely the correct practice of the ten virtues — abandoning killing, abandoning stealing, abandoning sexual misconduct, and so on. Not knowing this, they beat back and cast away, as if it were an enemy, that very practice of the path of virtuous action which is the cause of attaining happiness. Therefore all beings, having gone wrong and mistaken in both their desire and their conduct, cannot accomplish their own happiness, and cannot dispel their own suffering.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### tenzin-gyatso — Gyalwa Tenzin Gyatso (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

In all beings there is a wish, "If only I could have happiness and be free of suffering." Yet though they do not want suffering, they do not recognize its causes; and even when they recognize them, through the force of habit they engage in them. And though they want happiness, they do not recognize its causes; and even when they do, there are cases where they lack the strength to rouse the courage and diligence to accomplish it. Thus, even wanting happiness, under the sway of confusion they destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy — no one is beyond this condition. Therefore one must certainly take up the burden of others' welfare and strive at it.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **Beings' confusion about the means of happiness and suffering.** Verse 1-28, building on the earlier stages (that bodhicitta is supreme over all virtue, 1-26/27), settles the reason why beings need a bodhisattva's help. Beings are not merely unfortunate; because both their desire (wish) and their conduct (deed) have gone wrong, they cannot accomplish their own good. This is not a matter of mere misdeeds but a condition rooted in confusion (delusion).

2. **The ten causes of wrongdoing.** Khenpo Kunga most fully sets out the complete ten non-virtues (killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle talk, covetousness, ill will, wrong view) as the very deeds beings take up while wishing to be rid of suffering. He explains clearly: to gain happiness one must practice the ten virtues, but beings, not knowing this, cast off those very causes as if they were enemies.

3. **The instruction (Gyaltsab).** Gyaltsab opens the grouped commentary on verses 1-28/29/30 with a sharp question — "Are not beings themselves skilled at abandoning suffering and gaining happiness? Why must another strive?" — and answers it: the mark that beings are not skilled is precisely that they run toward the cause of suffering and destroy the cause of happiness. He then turns to the necessity of the bodhisattva's diligence (one must certainly strive).

4. **Love, compassion, and wisdom.** Gyaltsab, Ngulchu Thogme, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam all, at the close of the grouped commentary on 1-28/29/30, explain how bodhicitta's three qualities meet beings' three troubles: great love satisfies those deprived of happiness; great compassion cuts off all suffering; great wisdom dispels the confusion that drives beings toward the causes of suffering. These three qualities are not set out idly, but answer exactly the condition described in verse 1-28.

5. **The lack of courage (Tenzin Gyatso).** Tenzin Gyatso adds a further analysis beyond mere confusion: even when beings recognize the cause of suffering, through habit they engage in it; and even when they recognize the cause of happiness, they lack the courage and diligence (courage and diligence) to accomplish it. Therefore, he concludes, one must certainly take up the burden of others' welfare (taking up the burden of others' good) and strive at it.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** As the second phase of the fivefold set of benefits — showing how bodhicitta is supreme over all virtue — this verse settles the condition in which beings, on their own, are unskilled in the means of happiness and suffering: both their desire and their conduct have gone wrong, and so they bring their own happiness to ruin.

**Key points.**

- In their desire, though wishing for happiness, beings come down to the means of running toward non-virtue, the cause of suffering.
- In their conduct, through the power of confusion, they destroy the causes of their own happiness as if they were enemies — made vivid by the image of a moth into a flame.
- Laid out against the ten causes of wrongdoing directly, [the verse] shows the fine and coarse workings of confusion.
- Gyaltsab, Ngulchu Thogme, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam close the grouped commentary on 1-28 to 1-30 by showing how the three — great love, compassion, and wisdom — accomplish benefit and remove harm for beings.
- Tenzin Gyatso adds to the means-confusion the further factor of lacking the courage and diligence [to act], and so concludes that one must certainly take up the burden of others' welfare.
