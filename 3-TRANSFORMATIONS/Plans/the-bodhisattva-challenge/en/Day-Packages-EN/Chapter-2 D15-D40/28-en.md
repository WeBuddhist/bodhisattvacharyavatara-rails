# Day 28 — Death does not wait, and everything disappears

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 33 through 35 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. We live as though this life will hold, and we spend our days pushing away the people we count as enemies and holding tight to the ones we count as friends. But death does not wait for our work to be finished, and when it comes we leave friends, enemies, and everything we own behind and go on alone. These verses are a sharp reminder of that, and an encouragement to set down attachment and aversion that lead nowhere and take up something that is actually worth the day.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries tell a story here. A householder named Gautama in central India had a young son, Netso. The boy watched some people take a beehive. The bees that had been gathering the honey were killed, the rest fled, and the hive was simply gone. He said to himself that his father's household and all its wealth was no different from a broken hive. When his father asked what was wrong, the boy answered that the body is like dew, about to fall, and the breath is like mist. The two of them gave away everything they had and turned to practice.

That story is today's three verses in one picture. Death does not check whether our business is finished. When it comes we leave like a hair drawn out of butter: the hair comes out clean and nothing that surrounded it comes with it. Land, house, savings, the people we love and the people we cannot stand, and finally our own body — all of it stays where it is.

And this is what Shantideva is confessing. The friend ends, the enemy ends, we end. Whatever we did wrong for either of them was pointless from the start.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when anger rises toward someone, I will remember that both he and I will die, and let the hatred go.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the Mind)* Because we quietly assume we will be here indefinitely, we treat our dislikes as permanent business and spend real energy on them, gathering harm along the way. Today I will use these verses as a check. When someone irritates me and I feel the reaction building, I will remember one plain fact: he will die and so will I, and this particular argument will not survive either of us. That thought is not meant to make the day feel grim. It shrinks the grievance to its real size, so it stops governing me and I can take charge of my own mind instead.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

This lord of death, who cannot be trusted, does not wait for whether some piece of work has been done or left undone. So all people alike, the sick and the well, cannot place their trust in this life, which may end at any moment; it is not fit to be relied upon. That is the meaning.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This lord of death — whose coming is not fixed and who cannot be relied on — when he takes a being's life, does not pause to examine whether that person's own affairs, or the affairs of others, have been finished or are still left undone. When death falls upon us, there is no place to say, "I am still young, I have not finished my studies, it is not yet my time to die," or, "I still have business to attend to," or, "I have not yet reached retirement, so it is not yet my time." Nor is there any place to say, "I am usually in good health, so it is not yet my time." We speak of tomorrow and tomorrow, laying plans and doing our accounts as though many years of life lay ahead; yet no one at all can guarantee that we will still be here when that planned-for future arrives. That is simply how things stand: there is no certainty whatever that death will not come suddenly. That death makes no distinction between what is done and what is left undone means this — sick and healthy alike must die suddenly, with no warning. So there is not the slightest thing in one's own life that can be trusted. And when this unpredictable death falls upon us, then however fine the things bound up with this life may be — an excellent body, excellent relatives, excellent friends, excellent attendants, excellent wealth and enjoyments, an excellent home — not one of them is of any help.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

One might think: "Even if I do die at some point, I have no time today to confess my wrongdoing, so surely I shall not die before then." But this demon, the lord of death, in whom no trust or confidence can be placed, does not wait until one has accomplished this or that — whether one has already begun the work of confessing one's wrongdoing, or has not begun it and only now intends to. As the saying goes, "for the life of one chronically ill person, a hundred who were never ill": anyone at all, whether sick or in good health and at ease, is like a patch of sunlight between the clouds or a butter-lamp in the wind — here now, and suddenly gone. Since there is no certainty of not dying suddenly, this life cannot be trusted. It is not fit to place in it even the confidence that thinks, "at least I shall not die today," because when one will die, where one will die, and by what circumstance one will die are all uncertain; and so there is no certainty of not dying this very day.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

Zhenga's annotation commentary has no separate expository commentary set alongside it, so the underlying gloss is given here from the annotation itself. The lord of death cannot be trusted: he does not wait for whether one's work has been done or left undone, and he comes suddenly upon all people alike, the sick and the well. Thus the underlying gloss sets out in brief how this life cannot be trusted.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One might ask: since I shall not die before my wrongdoing has been purified, what need is there to be protected quickly? Because this lord of death, who cannot be trusted for even a moment, does not wait for the time when the purifying of wrongdoing has or has not been done, and takes no account of whether a task is finished or unfinished. Any person at all, sick or well, may die suddenly even before the full measure of their life has run out. So one cannot trust even in not dying today; life is not fit to be relied upon. Therefore one should purify wrongdoing quickly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

[From within his treatment of this verse together with the following one (2-34).] What is the reason one needs protection quickly? Because there is no saying "he will not come for such-and-such a while": this lord of death, who cannot be trusted, does not wait for the time when the purifying of wrongdoing and other such work has or has not been done, and takes no account of whether a task is finished or unfinished. Since neither the sick nor the well can say, "I shall not die suddenly," life is not fit to be trusted.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

This lord of death, in whom no one can place firm confidence, does not pause to weigh whether my earlier tasks have been completed and my later ones are still undone and unfinished. So it holds for anyone at all — for those afflicted by illness and for those who are free of illness and dwelling at ease alike.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

No one can place firm confidence in this lord of death, saying, "he will not come for such-and-such a while." He does not wait — he does not linger — for the time of my purifying of wrongdoing or my other affairs of this life, the earlier ones already done and the later ones still to be done and not yet done; he takes not the slightest account of whether a task is finished or unfinished. So there is no certainty that one will not die quickly, before there is any leisure to confess one's wrongdoing. And if one thinks, "How could I die, when no violent cause of death such as illness has seized me?" — it holds for anyone at all, both those seized by illness through a disturbance of the elements and those who are free of illness, strong, young and dwelling at ease. Because the causes of death are many and the causes of staying alive are few, one cannot trust even so much as "I shall not die today" in this life that may end at any moment; it is not fit to be firmly relied upon. In the Indian manuscript and in the commentary too, where the text has "seeking suddenness" there appears "a sudden thunderbolt": it is like a great bolt of lightning falling on one's head when one is not thinking of it, and like a bubble on water struck by the wind. Therefore one should exert oneself quickly in virtuous actions such as purifying wrongdoing. This, like the earlier passages, is an exhortation to one whose stream of being is full of wrongdoing to purify that wrongdoing quickly, out of fear of death.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-33]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Here he shows why wrongdoing must be confessed quickly: if one does not confess it quickly, there is the danger of dying very soon. "Cannot be trusted": because the lord of death, Yama, kills beings, he is called the lord of death, Yama, the Dharma-king — but whatever name one gives him, there is no trusting, that is, no certainty, about when he will arrive. Whether he arrives today, or tomorrow, or in some five years, or ten, or at the far end of fifteen or twenty — there is simply no ground on which to say with confidence, "he will come at such a time and kill me." So this untrustworthy lord of death, Yama, does not wait upon worldly affairs — subduing enemies, caring for relatives, trade, farming and the like — whether some of them have been done or not. Nor does he in any way wait upon the Dharma: upon the sublime Dharma being heard, thought over and meditated on, whether some of that has been done or not. In this world, if a person is ill with disorders of wind, bile, phlegm and the rest, there is no certainty; and even one who is not ill, whose body is strong right now, has no certainty of not dying soon. For everyone alike, this life may end at any moment, and so this life is by its very nature untrustworthy in every respect: it has become like a fly at dusk, like frost on a stone. Hence there is no telling when death will come. If one is ill, there is no certainty of dying soon; if one is not ill, there is no certainty of staying a long while. For the lifetime of one sick person, many who are not sick die; and for the lifetime of one who is not sick, many sick people die — and so on. Therefore, since there is no certainty when I too shall die, I must confess my wrongdoing quickly. And so one prays: "Buddhas and bodhisattvas, bless me — grant me the means by which my wrongdoing may be purified."

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Lord of Death Comes Suddenly, With No Regard for What Is Done or Left Undone

All the commentaries agree in the end that this lord of death takes no account of whether my work of purifying wrongdoing and the rest has been completed or not, nor of whether a task is finished or unfinished; he simply arrives suddenly.

###### 2. Sick or Healthy, No One Can Trust in Life

Kunzang Pelden and Sabzang Mati Panchen state clearly that both those gripped by illness and those who are strong, young and dwelling at ease may die suddenly, and so this life cannot be trusted in any respect.

###### 3. Because Life May End at Any Moment, Wrongdoing Must Be Confessed Quickly

Since there is no certainty about the lord of death, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both urge that one exert oneself quickly in virtuous actions such as purifying wrongdoing.

###### 4. Death Takes Our Life Without Regard for Any of the Affairs of This Life

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both explain that death falls upon us without any examination of whether our own affairs and those of others have been finished or are still left over.

###### 5. Images for the Fleeting Nature of Life

The uncertainty of this life is conveyed by images: a patch of sunlight between the clouds and a butter-lamp in the wind; a great thunderbolt falling on one's head and a bubble on water struck by the wind; a fly at dusk and frost on a stone.

###### 6. This Verse Is the Definite First Item in the Extended Treatment of the Great Fear of Death, After the Mind of Going for Refuge Has Been Generated

Ngulchu Thogme sets it out above as the part that shows the reason one needs protection quickly, namely that the lord of death cannot be trusted.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows that the lord of death takes no account of whether my work of purifying wrongdoing and the rest is finished or unfinished, and that he comes suddenly upon sick and healthy alike, so that this life cannot be trusted; and all the commentaries explain it chiefly as the reason one must go for refuge quickly and confess one's wrongdoing.

**Key points.**

- The lord of death does not wait for whether a task has been done or left undone, and takes our life suddenly, with no regard for whether it is finished.
- Whoever it may be — the sick, or the healthy in the strength of youth — there is not the slightest thing in this life that can be trusted.
- The fleeting nature of life is conveyed by images: a patch of sunlight between the clouds, a butter-lamp in the wind, a bubble on water, a fly at dusk.
- Because there is no certainty about the lord of death, it is right to purify wrongdoing quickly — set out clearly with quotations from Ludwangpo, the *Sutra of the Auspicious Night*, the *Precious Garland* and others.
- The story of Netso is given as an example of someone who, seeing that life is impermanent, gave away all his wealth and went forth into the homeless life.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

When death falls upon us we must leave behind the body, our enjoyments, our relatives and kin — everything — and go on alone. That is how things are; but I did not understand it. For the sake of objects that will not accompany me beyond this life and are of no help at all, I made a division in terms of "the ones I love" and "the ones I dislike"; I marked out sides and factions, and where I said "this one is dear to me" attachment and clinging arose, and for their sake I piled up a great deal of wrongdoing. Where I said "this one I dislike" I looked on them as an enemy, and even where there was nothing to be gained beyond a single harsh word, I spoke out of resentment and the wish to harm. Where in truth there was no benefit at all, I clung by the force of my own way of taking things: attached to some, hostile to others. To spend a human life pointlessly in such churning surf of attachment and aversion is the price of not keeping death in mind. Not knowing how death really is, I did all kinds of wrong for the sake of the many I loved and disliked; and yet even those loved and disliked ones — for whose sake I took on hardship and heaped up all kinds of wrongdoing — have in them nothing permanent, nothing stable.

<!-- story:BCAC13_KTB -->
##### BCAC13_KTB — The Householder Gautama and His Son Netso (Renunciation of the World)

This story is told as an application of the words of this verse, "that I would have to leave everything behind." In central India there was a householder called Gautama who had a young son named Netso. When many people were taking a beehive, the boy saw that the bees gathering the honey were killed and the rest fled, and he thought, "The householder's people and wealth and all the rest are just like a wrecked beehive"; and sadness arose in him. When his father spoke to him, the son answered: "The body is like dew, on the point of spilling away. The breath is like mist, on the point of vanishing. The mind is like lightning, gone in an instant. Enjoyments are like an illusion — they appear yet are not there. In short, everything is like a wrecked beehive." At last he put the matter before the Buddha, who said: "Samsara is impermanent, like a wrecked beehive; everything held to be permanent will come to ruin." Relying on that, both father and son gave away all their goods and wealth in generosity and took up the practice of Dharma.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Heart of the Verse Is Regret: Not Knowing That Everything Must Be Left Behind at Death, One Heaped Up Wrongdoing for the Sake of Friends and Enemies

Many commentaries explain in agreement that, under the power of not knowing that everything here must be left behind and that one goes on alone, one did all kinds of wrong for the sake of helping those one loved and harming those one disliked.

###### 2. The Scope of "Everything" — From One's Land and Region Down to One's Own Body, Food and Clothing

Kunzang Pelden explains that one must leave behind land and region, relatives and companions, position in the world, enemies and kin, household and family, property and enjoyments, and even one's own food, clothing and body; and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that after death some go up, some down, some to the god realms, some to the hells, some to the human world, each taking rebirth and being scattered, and each having to go.

###### 3. The Motivation Behind the Wrongdoing — Driven by Attachment and Aversion, One Kills, Steals and the Rest

It is explained that, for the sake of helping and caring for the side one loves and of harming and defeating the side one dislikes, one is driven by desire, hatred, jealousy, rivalry and the rest, and does much wrong: killing, taking what is not given, and so on.

###### 4. The Labels "Friend" and "Enemy" Are Merely Imposed by One's Own Way of Taking Things, and Are of No Benefit at All

His Holiness the Dalai Lama says that marking out sides and factions and dividing people into those one loves and those one dislikes is merely imposed by one's own way of taking things and is of no benefit whatever; and he explains that, through failing to keep death in mind, a human life is spent pointlessly in the churning surf of attachment and aversion.

###### 5. The Place of This Verse in the Outline — the First of the Three Ways of Regret

According to Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, this verse and the next (2-35) are the occasion of the first of the three ways of regret in the framework: regret for the wrongdoing done for the sake of relatives, body, enjoyments and the rest, because one did not know that these cannot be trusted.

###### 6. The Example Given by the Story — the Tale of the Householder Gautama's Son

With the image of a wrecked beehive it is shown that loved ones, relatives and possessions are all impermanent; and it is said that both father and son then gave away all their goods and wealth and took up the practice of Dharma.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For whose sake did we accumulate so much karma through attachment, aversion and confusion? Saying "this one is my enemy," we watched for an opening — whether we found one or not — and out of great anger and ill will we schemed against them. Saying "this one is my relative," we pushed their cause forward; and whether or not we could actually take care of them, we thought "perhaps a lie would help," told people falsehoods, and even swore oaths for their sake. But those people, for whom we had to do all these things, will not remain. Those we love will certainly come to an end; those we dislike will come to an end too. And in the same way, the very "I" who counts them as loved and disliked, saying "my friend" and "my enemy," will not remain but come to an end. It is not only a matter of our own relatives and kin: whoever it may be among the beings of the three realms, there is not one who stays permanent and stable — all pass through birth and death, come to an end and change. Those who serve as the ground and occasion for accumulating karma through attachment and aversion, whom we call enemy and relative, are like clouds suddenly gathered: as soon as clouds have gathered, they can only part again when they meet some condition — as Gungthangtsang said. In the same way, we too are gathered here today by the conditions of our own individual karma and merit; and those very conditions, meeting other conditions, will change. So all of it becomes an object of memory and falls apart.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Enemies, Relatives and Oneself — All Three Alike Will Die and Be Gone

All the commentaries explain, in final agreement, that the main point of this verse is that the enemies one disliked and the relatives and friends one loved will all die and be gone, whether one subdued them or cared for them, and that oneself will likewise be gone.

###### 2. Therefore Doing Wrong for the Sake of Enemies and Relatives Is Pointless and Mistaken

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Sabzang Mati Panchen state clearly that, since both those one loves and those one dislikes, and oneself as well, will be gone, one must regret the wrongdoing heaped up for their sake out of attachment and aversion, and that it was a mistake.

###### 3. That Everything Born Ends in Death Is a Law That Admits No Exception

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that, since whatever being is born in the realms of the world dies in the end, it is a general law that everything born will one day be simply gone.

###### 4. The Outer World That Contains Us, and Every Temporary State of the Beings Within It, Is Impermanent Too

Kunzang Pelden shows that every temporary state — being high, being powerful, being rich, being beautiful, being splendid — is like the sky, a thunderbolt, a nāga, a god, a rainbow, and that the castle built up, the wealth gathered and all the rest will be gone; and so he extends the meaning of the verse to the whole outer world.

###### 5. Every Gathering Is as Impermanent as Clouds Suddenly Gathered

His Holiness the Dalai Lama cites Gungthangtsang to explain that the gathering of relatives, kin, servants and attendants is like clouds suddenly gathered, or like leaves stirred up by a great wind: when other conditions are met they are scattered, never to come together again.

###### 6. The General Sense, Applied to Beings and Enjoyments at Large

Ngulchu Thogme renders the meaning of the verse in general terms: all beings and all enjoyments alike will be gone.

- Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Sabzang Mati Panchen state clearly that piling up wrongdoing for the sake of enemies and relatives out of attachment and aversion is therefore pointless and mistaken.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that everything born dies in the end: this is a law of the world that admits no exception.
- Kunzang Pelden shows that every temporary state — being high, being powerful, being rich and so on — is impermanent, like the sky, a thunderbolt, a nāga, a god, a rainbow; and so he extends the meaning to the whole outer world that contains us.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama cites Gungthangtsang to explain that every gathering is like clouds suddenly gathered: when other conditions are met it is scattered, never to come together again.
- Ngulchu Thogme renders the meaning in general terms: all beings and all enjoyments alike will be gone.
