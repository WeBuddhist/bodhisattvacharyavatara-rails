# Chapter 1 — Commentaries (English)

*Natural English translation of the ༤། འགྲེལ་བཤད། (Commentary) section from each day of the Dalai Lama plan, Days 1–14, Chapter 1 of the* Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra.

---

## Day 1 — Verses 1–3

In the first three verses, Śāntideva bows with respect to the Buddhas, the bodhisattvas, and everyone else who deserves respect, and he makes his promise to write this text. Why should we bow to everyone worthy of respect? There is an old story that answers this. Long ago, the daughter of King Ajātaśatru was in truth an emanation of a bodhisattva. The great arhats were proud, and they would not bow to her — they treated her as no more important than a young fox. But in the end she overcame their pride and led them to the Great Vehicle, and they bowed to her. This story teaches us clearly that we should show respect to everyone, everywhere.

Śāntideva was not proud about how skilled he was with words and meaning. He said he wrote this text not so much to help others, but "to train his own mind" — to make his own heart familiar with the awakening mind (bodhicitta). This is like the saying, "the water of good qualities cannot stay on the ball of pride." It shows how important humility is, even for someone who is very learned. When we calm our own mind in this way and let our faith grow, little by little we also start to have a good effect on others like us, and something great is achieved. This is the deep meaning he is pointing to.

---

## Day 2 — Verses 4–5

These two verses use a beautiful example to show how hard it is to find both supports: a good body and a good mind.

**Verse 4 (how hard it is to find a human body).** This human body, complete with the eight freedoms and ten riches, is very hard to find. The commentary gives an example: imagine a blind turtle at the bottom of the sea who comes up to the surface only once every hundred years. On the surface, the wind pushes around a single piece of wood with a hole in it. It is easier for that turtle to put its neck through the hole than it is to find a human body. With this human body we can reach every goal, both for now and for the long run. So if we do not practice virtue now, we may never find such a chance again. This is a gentle push to make good use of it.

**Verse 5 (how hard it is to find a good mind).** On a dark night thick with black clouds, a flash of lightning lets you see everything clearly for just a moment. In the same way, in the darkness of samsara, and through the blessing of the Buddhas, a good "wish to do good" arises in us only now and then, for just a moment, and very rarely. So when that good thought does appear, we should not waste it — we should use that moment and put our energy into doing good.

---

## Day 3 — Verses 6–8

These three verses show three wonderful benefits of the awakening mind.

**It overpowers wrongdoing.** Our good deeds are weak, and our wrongdoing is very strong. But as the sutra says, "just as mice are overpowered when a cat looks at them," the moment perfect bodhicitta arises, it overpowers all of that strong wrongdoing.

**It brings happiness quickly.** The Buddhas thought deeply and carefully for countless ages. In the end they saw that the one and only path that easily brings help and happiness to countless beings is this awakening mind.

**It fulfils every wish.** So whether a humble person wants to end their own suffering, or a great person wants to end the suffering of others, or someone simply wants to enjoy much happiness — this good mind is the best way to make all those wishes come true. The advice is this: never let this good mind slip away from your heart.

---

## Day 4 — Verses 9–11

Today's three verses show a wonderful thing: the very moment the awakening mind is born in us, our name and our standing change completely.

**Verse 9 shows how our name changes in an instant.** Someone may have been a poor, suffering being, locked in the prison of samsara. Yet the moment bodhicitta arises in them, they receive the name "child of the Buddhas" and become worthy of the respect of gods and humans. There is a story about this. A merchant named Palbar had his eye gouged out by a robber, yet he felt not even a hair's worth of anger. Instead, he gave rise to a pure awakening mind. The robber was so amazed that he gave back everything he had stolen, bowed to the merchant, and became his follower.

**Verse 10 shows the example of the elixir that turns metal to gold.** Just as one measure of this special elixir can turn a thousand measures of iron into gold, the awakening mind turns this impure body of ours — a body tied down by our karma and afflictions — into the priceless body of a Buddha. So we are told to hold this mind firmly and never let it waver.

**Verse 11 shows the example of the wish-fulfilling jewel.** The perfect Buddha is the only guide who can lead beings across the ocean of samsara. When he looked with his boundless mind, he saw that the most precious and best way to end the misery of beings is this awakening mind alone. So anyone who wants to be free from the suffering of samsara is urged to hold this mind well, with mindfulness, so that it never fades.

---

## Day 5 — Verses 12–14

**Verse 12 (the tree).** Ordinary good deeds done only for oneself are like a banana tree: it bears fruit once, then dries up from the root and is gone. But a good deed held by the awakening mind is like a wish-fulfilling tree: it bears fruit again and again, never runs out, and keeps growing more and more.

**Verse 13 (the brave hero).** Even if someone has done very serious wrongs, such as the five worst acts, they can still be saved. Just as staying close to a strong hero keeps you safe from the fear of enemies and thieves, relying on the awakening mind can free you from the fear of wrongdoing in a single moment. So wise people are told to rely on it, whatever else they do.

**Verse 14 (the fire at the end of time).** At the end of an age, a great mass of fire burns up all the worlds in an instant. In the same way, this good mind burns away all the wrongdoing in us, right down to the root. The countless benefits of this mind are explained at length by the noble Maitreya in the Gaṇḍavyūha Sutra and other texts.

---

## Day 6 — Verses 15–16

**Verse 15 (the two kinds of awakening mind).** In short, there are just two kinds of awakening mind. The first is "aspiring bodhicitta," which focuses on the goal — the wish, "may I reach buddhahood for the sake of all beings." The second is "engaging bodhicitta," which actually sets out on the path of good actions, such as generosity, by taking the vow in order to reach that goal.

**Verse 16 (wishing to go and actually going).** The difference between the two is like the difference between wanting to travel somewhere and actually setting out on the road, step by step. In the same way, wise people should understand the difference between simply wishing for the goal and actually entering the path and doing the practice.

---

## Day 7 — Verses 17–19

Today's three verses show very clearly the difference in benefit between "aspiring bodhicitta" and "engaging bodhicitta."

**Verse 17 shows the benefit of the aspiring mind.** As the sutra says, even a cracked piece of precious diamond still outshines every golden ornament. In the same way, simply giving rise to the aspiring mind — even without actually doing the practice — outshines the qualities of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas and brings the great result of a good rebirth as a god or human. Even so, this alone does not create a non-stop stream of merit the way the engaging mind does.

**Verses 18 and 19 show the wonderful power of the engaging mind.** From the moment you truly take on, and keep unbroken, the firm vow to actually practise in order to free all beings from suffering, something amazing happens. From then on — even while you are asleep or not paying attention — your merit keeps growing more and more, without stopping, as vast as the sky. This shows how valuable it is to put our good intentions into real practice and firm commitment.

---

## Day 8 — Verses 20–22

These three verses use clear reasoning to establish the boundless merit of the awakening mind that the Buddha himself spoke of. There is a story that shows this. Once, a being was suffering in hell, with a burning iron wheel spinning on his head. In that moment he gave rise to a single kind thought: "May all the head-pain and suffering of other beings ripen upon me instead." Just from that one thought, he was reborn in the realm of the gods.

If there is boundless merit even in wishing to remove the mere headache of a few beings, then think how much greater the merit must be in the awakening mind — the mind that wishes to remove every last bit of suffering from each and every being, and to set every one of them in the countless good qualities of buddhahood. Reasoning makes this completely clear.

---

## Day 9 — Verses 23–25

These three verses clearly show that the awakening mind is a wonderful thing, with nothing else like it in the whole world. In this world, not even parents who love their child, not even gods and sages with special powers, not even Brahma who rests in the four boundless states, have this wish to lead all beings to buddhahood. Why not? Because they have never even dreamed of reaching buddhahood for their own sake — so how could such a wish arise for the sake of others?

This mind, which thinks only of the good of others — a mind that has never even arisen for one's own sake — is like a heavenly wish-granting tree growing out of a heap of filth. When it appears in our own mind, caught as we are in the trap of the afflictions, it is a wonderful, precious jewel of a mind, never seen before, like a wish-fulfilling gem.

---

## Day 10 — Verses 26–28

These three verses show that the awakening mind is like a wish-fulfilling jewel, and like the finest medicine, the nectar that removes all the suffering of beings. If merely wishing to help beings brings more merit than making offerings to countless Buddhas, then think how much greater it is to actually work for the good of others.

Why do beings need such compassion and such an awakening mind? Because, even though they do not want suffering, they rush toward harmful actions like moths flying into a flame. And even though they want happiness, out of confusion and ignorance they destroy the very causes of their own happiness, as if those causes were enemies. For these beings — whose thoughts and actions are both so mixed-up — there is no refuge anywhere greater than compassion and the awakening mind.

---

## Day 11 — Verses 29–30

These two verses show, in a way that is easy for ordinary people to understand, three wonderful qualities of the bodhisattva who removes the troubles of beings.

Beings want happiness but do not have it; with great love, the bodhisattva fills them with every kind of happiness, both for now and for the long run. Beings do not want suffering but have so much of it; with great compassion, the bodhisattva cuts away all the suffering of birth, ageing, sickness, and death, right at the root. And for people who do not know what to take up and what to give up when it comes to the causes of happiness and suffering, the bodhisattva, with great wisdom, clears away the darkness of their confusion.

What other good deed could equal the awakening mind, which helps beings through these three: love, compassion, and wisdom? There is no better friend, and no greater merit, anywhere.

---

## Day 12 — Verses 31–32

These two verses show, in a way that is easy for ordinary people to understand, the wonderful way the bodhisattva works for others.

**First.** In this world, people praise someone even for simply repaying a kindness to a person who helped them first. So how much more should we praise the bodhisattva, who — without ever having been helped, and without anyone asking — is moved by natural, great compassion to bring good and happiness to all beings.

**Second.** Suppose someone gives just a little poor food to a few beings, over a short time and even with a scornful attitude, filling their stomachs for half a day. People will still honour them and say, "They have done a great good deed." So how much more should we honour the bodhisattva, who always gives the highest happiness to countless beings.

---

## Day 13 — Verses 33–34

These two verses show the bodhisattva's magnificent generosity, and also how serious it is to feel ill will toward them.

If everyone honours even an ordinary worldly giver who offers just a little food to a few beings, then the bodhisattva is truly the holy one worthy of our homage and honour — for the bodhisattva always gives the highest happiness of the Buddhas to countless beings, until samsara itself is empty.

But if someone feels anger and ill will toward such a great giver, the Buddha said they will suffer in hell for as many ages as there were moments of that ill will. There is a story that makes this clear: long ago a monk, out of jealousy, spoke badly of a bodhisattva. Because of this he was reborn in hell for many ages and went through endless suffering. This shows plainly how serious it is to hold ill will toward others.

---

## Day 14 — Verses 35–36

These two verses easily show the bodhisattva's wonderful qualities, and why we should take refuge in them.

**First.** Anyone who feels faith and devotion toward a bodhisattva gains boundless merit. And whatever harm or trouble comes to these children of the Buddhas, they turn it into part of the path — just as wind only helps a forest fire grow. So their goodness naturally increases, and a harmful thought never arises in them.

**Second.** Whoever they are, high or low, once the awakening mind has arisen in their heart, they become a holy object of homage and refuge. They give the highest happiness even to those who harm them. For example, long ago our own Teacher was a sage known as "the one who speaks of patience." A king cut off his limbs, yet he did not give rise to a single harmful thought; instead, he made a heartfelt prayer for that king. Later, when the sage became a Buddha, that very king became one of his first good followers and was among the first to be led onto the path of happiness. This story shows it well. A good connection can lead to buddhahood in a single life, while a bad connection can keep samsara going without end. So the bodhisattva is truly the real source of all happiness.
