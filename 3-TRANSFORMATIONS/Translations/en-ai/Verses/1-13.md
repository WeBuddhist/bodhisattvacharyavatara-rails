## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three commentators agree that Verse 1-13 illustrates the protective power of the mind of enlightenment (bodhicitta) through the analogy of relying on a brave companion or hero escort to escape great danger. This analogy serves to inspire both conscientious and fearful practitioners to diligently rely on bodhicitta as the ultimate refuge from negative karma.

### 1. The Core Analogy: Relying on a Hero in Great Danger
*   **Consensus on the Metaphor:** The commentators explain the analogy systematically: just as a traveler who must traverse a highly dangerous road infested with thieves, enemies, or terrifying obstacles relies on a powerful, brave companion (*śūra* / *dpa' bo*) to escape harm and find safety, a practitioner who has committed extremely severe misdeeds can rely on the mind of enlightenment to be swiftly and instantly liberated from the terrifying fears of bad migrations (the lower realms and hells).
*   **The Nature of "Great Negativity":** The commentators clarify that the "dreadful wickedness" or "horrendous wrongs" mentioned in the verse refer to the most severe forms of negative karma. Gyaltsab Je specifically identifies them as "extremely fierce negativity such as harming the Three Jewels or the five heinous crimes," while Sazang Mati Panchen and Ngulchu Thokme define them as "actions of immediate retribution" (*ānantarya-karma*), which ordinarily lead directly to the unendurable sufferings of hell.
*   **Swiftness of Liberation:** All three commentators emphasize the immediate efficacy of this protection, noting that relying on the mind of enlightenment liberates the practitioner from these fears "in an instant" (*क्षणेन* / *yud kyis*).

### 2. Textual Variations: "Careful" vs. "Ignorant" Beings
The commentators address a subtle difference in the rhetorical question that concludes the verse, which stems from a divergence between the Tibetan translation and the Sanskrit root text:
*   **The Tibetan Reading ("Careful" / "Conscientious"):** The Tibetan translation of the root text (followed by Loden Sherab, Gyaltsab Je, and Ngulchu Thokme) asks: "Why then do careful (conscientious) people (*bag can*) not rely on it?" Gyaltsab Je and Ngulchu Thokme explain that conscientious practitioners who are dedicated to practicing adoption (of virtue) and abandonment (of non-virtue) should naturally hasten to rely on this meditation.
*   **The Sanskrit Reading ("Ignorant"):** The Sanskrit root text asks: "Why is it not relied upon by ignorant beings (*ajñasattvaiḥ*)?" 
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen's Scholarly Reconciliation:** Sazang Mati Panchen explicitly highlights this textual variation, noting that the Sanskrit text and Prajñākaramati's *Great Commentary* (*Bodhicaryāvatārapañjikā*) read: "Why do ignorant sentient beings who are devoid of wisdom not rely on it?" Sazang reconciles the two perspectives by stating that because the mind of enlightenment is such an exceptionally powerful remedy, it is proper for *everyone* to rely on it—both the wise/conscientious who understand its value and the ignorant who are terrified of suffering but do not know how to escape it.

### 3. Canonical Scriptural Citations
To establish scriptural authority for this analogy, both Sazang Mati Panchen and Ngulchu Thokme cite canonical Mahayana sūtras:
*   **Ngulchu Thokme's Citation:** Ngulchu Thokme explicitly cites the *Sūtra Taught by Akṣayamati* (*Akṣayamatiparipṛcchā Sūtra*):
    > "O son of noble family, for example, relying on a hero means one has no fear of any enemies. Likewise, a Bodhisattva who firmly relies on the hero of the mind of perfect enlightenment has no fear of any enemies of negative actions."
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen's Citation:** Sazang Mati Panchen cites a nearly identical passage (attributing it generally to "the Sūtras"):
    > "Noble son, it is like this: for example, one who correctly relies on a brave man does not fear any enemies. In the same way, an heir of the Buddha who correctly relies on the brave man of generating the mind of enlightenment does not fear any enemies of non-virtuous conduct."

### 4. Doctrinal Distinction: Overcoming vs. Destroying Negativity
*   **Ngulchu Thokme's Progressive Analysis:** Ngulchu Thokme provides a key doctrinal clarification to prevent confusion between this fourth analogy (the brave companion) and the fifth analogy (the aeon-ending fire in Verse 1-14). He explains that the brave companion analogy represents "overcoming" or "protecting one from the ripening effects of negativity," whereas the aeon-ending fire represents "destroying negativity from its very root." 
*   **No Conflict:** Thokme explains that there is no conflict between these two benefits because they are not contradictory; rather, they arise progressively as the practitioner attains higher and higher levels of generating the mind of enlightenment (such as the attainment of great patience on the path).
