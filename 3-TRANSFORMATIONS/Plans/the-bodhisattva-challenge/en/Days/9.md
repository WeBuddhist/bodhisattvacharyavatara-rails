# Day 9 — Even Brahma's boundless goodwill falls short of this

## Opening

Chapter 1 has been building the case for why bodhicitta matters. Today's four verses bring that argument to its close. Verses 1.23–1.25 scan every category of being — parent, god, sage, even Brahma — and conclude that this mind has never arisen anywhere in samsara. Verse 1.26 follows: a mind this rare is also immeasurable in what it does.

## Renewing the Bodhisattva Vow

*MIND TRAINING (recite 3 times)*

> May all beings enjoy happiness and the causes of happiness.
> May all beings be free from suffering and the causes of suffering.
> May all beings never be separated from supreme happiness, free from suffering.
> May all beings rest in immeasurable equanimity, free from attachment and aversion.

*REFUGE (recite 3 times)*

> Until I reach the heart of awakening, I take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha.

*TAKING THE BODHISATTVA VOW (recite 3 times)*

> Just as the buddhas of the past gave rise to bodhicitta
> And trained step by step in the bodhisattva conduct,
> So I too, for the benefit of all beings, will give rise to bodhicitta
> And train step by step in that same conduct.

## From the Tradition

Sazang Mati Panchen describes Brahma as one who has "trained in the four immeasurables and wishes to be the grandfather of all." The four immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity — are the most expansive states of goodwill that samsara contains. Brahma holds them universally, directed toward every being. Yet the commentators are firm: this is still not bodhicitta. The distinction they draw is one of aim. Brahma wishes beings happiness and peace — ease, relief, good rebirths. The bodhisattva's wish points at something categorically different: complete Buddhahood for every being without exception. Even meditative goodwill practiced on a cosmic scale still operates within the logic of samsara. Bodhicitta steps outside that logic entirely — which is why verses 1.23–1.25 can sweep through every category of samsaric being, including Brahma himself, and find this mind nowhere.

## Aspiration

> May the precious and supreme bodhicitta —
> Where it has not yet arisen, may it arise;
> Where it has arisen, may it not decline,
> But grow ever further.
>
> By the merit of this practice,
> May all wandering beings, without exception,
> Enter the path of the bodhisattva.

## Today's Practice Challenge

When you notice goodwill arising toward someone today — a colleague who's overwhelmed, a friend dealing with something hard — feel for where that wish naturally stops. It probably ends somewhere around "I hope things get better for them soon." Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen distinguishes the bodhisattva's wish from that: not just better, but completely free from suffering at its root. Try catching one such moment today and extending it past its usual endpoint, all the way to: may this person be entirely free.
