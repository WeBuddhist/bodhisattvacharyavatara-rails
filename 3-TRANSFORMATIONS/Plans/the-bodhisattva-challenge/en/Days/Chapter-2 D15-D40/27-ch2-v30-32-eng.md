## Today's Verse

> I have, because of the afflictions, Caused harm with body, speech, and mind To the three jewels and to my parents And to the gurus, among others. ^2-30

> I, who am wicked and am stained By numerous failings, have committed Misdeeds that are most terrible. Before the guides, I confess them all. ^2-31

> I am going to perish quickly, Before I’m cleansed of my misdeeds. How can I be rescued from them? I beg you, please deliver me. ^2-32

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 30 through 32 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. Over a lifetime we do a great deal of harm, some of it deliberate and much of it careless, to the very people we owe the most: our parents, our teachers, and the Three Jewels. Today's verses set that beside a second fact, that this life is short and the hour of death is not fixed. Together they push us to stop postponing and to confess our mistakes now, with real regret.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

Something changes at verse 30, and the commentaries mark it. Gyaltsab opens a new section here, and the Dalai Lama says the same thing directly: verses 27 to 29 confessed misdeeds in general, and from here Shantideva confesses them object by object. He stops saying "I have done wrong" and starts saying who he did it to.

Khenpo Zhenga sorts the three objects by what we owe them. The Three Jewels are the field of qualities: we owe them for what they are. Our parents are the field of benefit: we owe them for what they have done for us. Our gurus are both at once. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk gives the concrete harms, and they are recognizable: damaging statues and scriptures on the one hand, striking parents and teachers or speaking harshly to them on the other. Kunzang Palden singles out one as worst — misusing what belongs to the Sangha — and quotes the warning that what belongs to the Sangha is like poison, except that poison has an antidote and this does not.

Verse 31 explains why these stay with us. Kunzang Palden uses an image the scriptures already carry: rust arises from iron, and once it has arisen it eats the iron itself. Many faults settle on the mind-stream the same way, from the inside, and the ripening is not left behind anywhere. Minyak Kunsö explains what "most terrible" means here — not that it feels bad, but that it causes the suffering of the lower realms and blocks the road to a better rebirth and to liberation.

Then verse 32, and this is where His Holiness the Dalai Lama's point belongs. That we die is settled; when, is not. A road accident ends a life in a moment, and no doctor will guarantee that any particular person is still alive tomorrow. So the space between now and death is not a period we can make plans inside. That is why Shantideva asks to be rescued **quickly** rather than eventually, and why Kunga Wangchuk describes him as pressing the request insistently.

One place the commentaries part company, worth keeping: most read "the gurus, among others" as other teachers and abbots. Khenpo Zhenga and the Dalai Lama widen it to beings in general, including the sick and those with no one to look after them.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today I will not speak a harsh word to my parents or my teachers. I will speak to them with respect and warmth.

**Explanation:** _(Avoiding wrongdoing)_ The people who have been kindest to us are often the ones we are sharpest with. Anger comes out most easily at home, and we let ourselves say things to a parent or a teacher that we would never say to a stranger. Today's verses count this as a serious wrong, not a small domestic slip, because these are the people whose kindness we are standing on. So today I will keep watch on my tone with my parents and my teachers. Before I answer them, I will bring to mind one specific thing they have done for me. This clears something of what has already been done, and it stops the next round of it from starting.
