# Day 36 — The one doctor who can heal every affliction

**Chapter:** 2  
**Verses covered:** 2-54 to 2-56

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 54 through 56 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. A new section opens here. Day 32 carried the first of the four powers of confession, regret, and day 33 the second, reliance. These verses begin the third, the power of applying the antidotes. The image running through all three is medical: an ordinary illness makes us do as we are told, so what should this illness make us do? Today moves from feeling sorry and taking refuge to actually treating something.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The comparison is agreed by all the commentaries and it is worth taking slowly. When we have an ordinary fever we do what the doctor says, including the parts we would rather skip. The afflictions are also an illness. This one has been in us without a break for as long as we have existed, and there are hundreds of them. One of them alone, the verse says, could destroy everyone alive. And there is no second medicine for it anywhere.

Khenpo Kunzang Palden adds the part that is easy to miss. Doing good in general is not the same as applying an antidote. He uses a farming image. Sow a single pea in among a great deal of barley: the barley comes up thick all around, and the pea comes up too, entirely unharmed. Merit is the barley. The particular misdeed is the pea. You can accumulate a great deal of the first, he says, and it will not defeat the second unless you deliberately aim it there, as the remedy for that.

Which is why the instruction is not simply to be good today. It is to treat one specific thing as an illness and take the medicine for it.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, the first time anger or wanting rises in me strongly, I will name it as the illness it is and apply one remedy to that, rather than trying to be generally better.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* The move here is narrow and deliberate. When it comes up, and it will, I will stop long enough to say what it is: this is anger, this is wanting. Then I will do one thing aimed at it. For anger, look at the situation from the other person's side until it moves at all. For wanting, look at what the thing will actually be worth to me in a week. It does not have to work. It has to be pointed. Vague good intentions spread over the day are the barley in Kunzang Palden's field, growing thickly while the pea sits there untouched. One affliction, named, with one remedy aimed at it, is worth more today than a general resolution to improve.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Even when one is afraid of an ordinary illness — a disorder of wind, or of bile, or the like — one must still bring one's food and one's whole way of going about things into line with what is proper, following the word of a skilled doctor. #### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Even someone struck by an ordinary illness has to follow the doctor's word in what he takes up and what he gives up. So for us, who are struck at all times by the sickness of hundreds of faults — craving and the rest, an enormous number of faults — what need is there even to say that we must act according to your word, O Tathagata? We must certainly listen to the Teacher's word and train in the trainings just as he sets them out.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-54]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Third, the power of applying the antidote. Just as, in that other context, heedfulness, alertness and forbearance are what give rise to each of the principal factors, so here too this power is what gives rise, in one's own mindstream, to the antidote that purifies wrongdoing. By meditating again and again on cause and effect in action and on the impermanence of life, those who have no intention of producing in themselves the antidote that cleanses wrongdoing come to form that intention; and those who have a little of it but, out of laziness and distraction, do not apply themselves to it, come to apply themselves quickly. Then, once that intention has arisen and one is applying oneself: in the preparation, whatever virtue one practises is undertaken with the thought that it is for the sake of confessing wrongdoing; in the main part, there is the mindful wish to confess wrongdoing; and afterwards, there is the dedication for the sake of cleansing wrongdoing. When all three — preparation, main part and conclusion — are complete, then the whole of it is a confession of wrongdoing, and so it is the antidote. Moreover, even if one spends a whole life in solitude practising virtue, if some small fault has occurred and one does not confess it with the antidote, one will still have to undergo its ripening. For example, if a single pea lies among a great quantity of barley and it is all sown in the ground, the barley comes up in quantity, and yet that does not prevent the pea from coming up as well: it sprouts alongside. In just the same way, he says, even if one does a great deal of virtue, if it is not directed as an antidote to wrongdoing it will not overcome that wrongdoing. Even a virtuous person has no certainty of not being born in the lower realms in the next life, since the karma for being born there may still be present in his mindstream; and in particular, since everything depends on how one life links to the next at the moment of death, and one does not know what one's mind will link to, striving at the antidote that confesses wrongdoing is of great importance. So, thinking about the afflictions, which are the cause, [Shantideva] shows by the example of illness the reason why one must confess by striving at the antidote: if one is afraid of being struck by an ordinary illness — a disorder of wind, bile or phlegm, or all of them combined — one still has to take medicine according to the doctor's word and to put up with bloodletting, cauterization, lancing and the rest. What need, then, is there even to speak of this: that we, who from beginningless time have been struck at all times by the sickness of many hundreds of faults — craving, aversion, confusion, pride and the rest — must take up and give up exactly as the supreme doctor, the victorious one, instructs?

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Even when one is afraid of an ordinary illness — a disturbance of wind or bile and the rest — one fears dying of it and has to follow the doctor's word as the way of treating it. What need, then, is there even to speak of this: that we, who from beginningless time have been struck at all times by the great chronic disease of the three poisons, craving and the rest — the sickness that is the source of many hundreds and thousands of faults — should rely on the antidote according to the word of the supreme doctor, the victorious one, who dispels that disease? It is only right to strive at the antidote to wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-54]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Even when one is afraid of an ordinary illness such as a disorder of wind or bile, one must rely on its antidote according to the doctor's word. What need, then, is there even to speak of striving at the antidote according to the word of the victorious one, when one is struck at all times by the sickness of the hundred faults, craving and the rest? For that sickness has graver consequences, and what removes it is harder to find. *(Part of a passage in which verses 54 to 56 are gathered together and explained as one.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-54]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Ordinary illness is counted as four hundred and four: wind, bile, phlegm and their combination make four, and for each of those four there are a hundred that bring untimely death and one that brings death at its proper time. Even when one is afraid of being struck by any one of those four hundred and four, one must still take medicine according to the doctor's word. What need, then, is there even to speak of relying on the antidote according to the Buddha's word, when one is struck at all times by the workings of the four faults one has been familiar with from beginningless time — craving, aversion, confusion and pride — which likewise come to four hundred and four, since for each of the four there are a hundred for which there is no antidote and one for which there is, and whose workings are like a violent illness?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-54]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One might think: now that one has gone for refuge, surely that is enough — why is it also necessary to receive his instructions? Even when one is merely struck by an ordinary illness — the elements thrown out of balance, wind and bile and the rest out of proportion, an illness that does not last long — one is afraid and frightened, and as the way of treating it one relies properly on the antidote of medicine, diet and daily regimen just as the doctor has directed, and one does not go beyond what he says. What need, then, is there even to speak of what is only right: that we, whose mindstream is struck at all times by the great chronic disease of the afflictions — craving and the rest — a violent sickness that from beginningless time has been the source of many hundreds of faults and that no one can easily cure, should rely properly on the antidote and not go beyond the word of the supreme doctor, the Buddha, who dispels that sickness? For this sickness of the afflictions runs on for a long time, is hard to cure, and gives rise as well to the violent stabbing pain of suffering; so its consequences are graver than those of the earlier illness, and the antidote that removes it is also harder to find.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-54]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Moreover, even when one is tormented by the ordinary illnesses of the world — wind, phlegm and bile — and is in a state where one's very life is in danger, one has to put into practice, properly, what a skilled doctor says is to be taken up and what is to be given up. If that is so, what need is there even to speak of this: that we, who are struck at all times by those sicknesses of the afflictions — craving and aversion, jealousy and rivalry, pride and the rest, in short many hundreds and thousands of faults — and who are worn down and tormented by them, must take up and give up according to the word of the doctor, the perfect Buddha?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-54]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Advice to Strive Quickly at the Power of Applying the Antidote

With this verse the third of the four powers of confession, the power of applying the antidote, is taken up: by meditating on cause and effect in action and on the impermanence of life, one produces the antidote to wrongdoing, and one must then strive quickly at a confession of wrongdoing that is complete in all three parts — preparation, main part and conclusion.

###### 2. The Pairing of Ordinary Illness With the Sickness of the Afflictions

If, when one is afraid of an ordinary illness of wind, bile or phlegm, one has to act according to the word of a skilled doctor, then what need is there to say that we, who from beginningless time have been struck at all times by the sickness of the many hundreds of faults such as craving, must take up and give up according to the word of the supreme doctor, the victorious one? All the commentaries explain this in one voice.

###### 3. The Sickness of the Afflictions Is a Long-Standing Illness That Has Struck the Mindstream From Beginningless Time

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain that the afflictions — craving, aversion, pride, jealousy and the rest — are a great sickness that has struck the mindstream at all times from beginningless time.

###### 4. The Definite Count of Four Hundred and Four Diseases Is Used to Show That Even Ordinary Illnesses Are Many

Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam each cite lines from "the Indian text and the great commentary" that fix the number of ordinary illnesses at four hundred and four, and use them to show that the sicknesses of the afflictions are more numerous still.

###### 5. Even Virtue That Is Done Will Not Overcome Wrongdoing If It Is Not Directed as Its Antidote

Kunzang Pelden illustrates this with a single pea among a great quantity of barley, and advises that one must strive quickly at a confession and restraint that is complete in all three parts — preparation, main part and conclusion — with the intention that the virtue one practises be applied as the antidote to wrongdoing.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Set aside for the moment all the faults of that sickness of the afflictions — the many hundreds of faults such as craving that strike one at all times. Even a single fault has the power to destroy every person in this world of Jambudvipa. Among the many that there are — anger, pride, doubt and so on — a fault like anger, or like having no sense of when enough is enough, has the power on its own to throw the whole world into turmoil: the powerful lording it over the powerless, no one willing to be beneath anyone else, great craving and clinging for wealth and enjoyments, and in the end the making of war and the stirring up of conflict. Out of the many hundreds of afflictions, a single strand of one has the power to throw the world into turmoil; and for a sickness like that no other healing medicine can be found in any direction. In this world of ours there have been the First and Second World Wars; there is the talk of Hitler killing Jews in their thousands and tens of thousands, of many millions of people being killed in Russia in Stalin's time, and of more than eighty million killed by what Mao Zedong did in China. Explained from within the Buddhist teaching, it is perfectly clear that all of these were the work of the afflictions.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-55]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Among those sicknesses of the afflictions — craving and the rest — even a single one, or else even a single person in whose mindstream those afflictions have arisen, brings ruin through suffering upon all the people and other beings who live in this world of Jambudvipa, casting them into the lower realms and the rest. And as for the medicine that heals those sicknesses of the afflictions: anything other than the sublime Dharma cannot be found by anyone, though it be searched for in every direction.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-55]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Anger directed at a bodhisattva, for instance: even a single one of these is so harmful that it destroys all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa, casting them into hell. And as for the medicine that heals those sicknesses — anything other than the words of the Teacher, which set out the antidote, the way of meditating on the path and so on, cannot be found in any other quarter, not even in the world of Brahma; and because it cannot be found, it is extremely rare.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Even a single one of those sicknesses — craving and the rest — destroys all the many people, as illustrated by those who live in this world of Jambudvipa. And as for the medicine that heals them, anything other than practising according to the word of the victorious one cannot be found in any direction. *(Part of a passage in which verses 54 to 56 are gathered together and explained as one.)*

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

That is what is said, and the meaning is just as has already been set out. Among those afflictions, being tainted by even one of them singly brings ruin through suffering upon all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-55]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Among those sicknesses of the afflictions — craving and the rest — even a single one, that is, any one of them taken by itself, flings all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa into hell and the rest, and so ruins them with suffering that is violent and long-lasting. This is how the gravity of that sickness and the rarity of the medicine that removes it are shown: if, for example, anger directed at a bodhisattva arises in the mindstreams of a hundred people, all hundred are born in hell; and in the same way, if it arises in all the people of the world, all of them go to hell. That is the point being conveyed. And as for the medicine, the antidote that heals those afflictions — how one is to rely on the uncontaminated path and so forth — anything other than practising according to the word of the victorious one can never be found by anyone, though one search through all the world-systems of the ten directions; it is so rare that finding it is impossible.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-55]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Among those sicknesses of the afflictions — the eighty-four thousand of them and more — even a single one, such as craving or aversion, has the power to ruin all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa, or to fling them into the states of the lower realms. If that is so, then the medicine that heals those bad sicknesses of the afflictions is the sublime Dharma, properly put into practice as the accomplishing of virtue and the giving up of wrongdoing. Nothing else — no herbal medicine, no medicine from trees, no mineral medicine — is of any help; and so, though one goes searching eagerly in every direction, it cannot be found.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-55]]

<!-- div:divergences -->
##### ⚑ Divergences (where the commentaries differ)

The commentaries differ in the way they illustrate which affliction is meant by the verse's phrase "even one of these."

- **kunpal, khenpo-zhengah:** they render it as "even one such as craving," illustrating it by the afflictions in general — craving and the rest. ⚑
- **khenpo-kunga:** he renders it as "even one such as craving or aversion," bringing out both attachment and aversion in general terms. ⚑
- **gyaltsab, minyak-kunzang-sonam:** they illustrate it specifically as "anger directed at a bodhisattva," singling out aversion that takes a bodhisattva as its object. ⚑
- **tenzin-gyatso:** he renders it as "a fault like anger, or like having no sense of when enough is enough," bringing out both aversion and pride in general terms. ⚑

<sup>Note: all the commentaries agree in the end on the main point of the verse — that even a single affliction has the power to ruin all the people of the world, and that its antidote is rare. The difference lies only in which affliction is chosen as the illustration, so it is not a definite contradiction.</sup>

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Great Danger That Even a Single Affliction Has the Power to Ruin Every Person in the World

All the commentaries agree in the end that, among those sicknesses of the afflictions, even a single one has the power to bring ruin through suffering upon all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa, casting them into the lower realms and the rest.

###### 2. The Medicine That Removes the Sickness of the Afflictions Is Nowhere to Be Found Other Than in the Sublime Dharma, Though One Search All the World-Systems of the Ten Directions

Ordinary medicine — herbal, tree-derived or mineral — cannot help; and it is explained that there is no way of healing other than the proper practice of the sublime Dharma alone.

###### 3. The Commentaries Use Different Illustrations for Which Affliction Is Meant

Some illustrate it with "craving," some with "anger," some with "pride" and so on, each showing by its own example that any one of the afflictions carries such danger. ⚑

###### 4. The Story of King Padma of Kashi, Who Took Birth as a Rohita Fish, Illustrates How Even a Single Great Ordinary Illness Can Harm All the People of the World

Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes it from the great commentary as an example of how even an ordinary illness can harm all the people of the world.

###### 5. Modern Examples Are Used to Show How Great the Danger of the Afflictions Is

His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives the two World Wars, and the histories of the killings under Hitler, Stalin and Mao Zedong, as examples that make plain how a single affliction can harm a great many people.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows how great the danger of the sickness of the afflictions is and how rare its antidote. Even a single one of the afflictions has the power to bring ruin through suffering upon all the people who live in this world of Jambudvipa; and anything other than the medicine that heals them — the proper practice of the sublime Dharma — cannot be found, though one search all the world-systems of the ten directions. All the commentaries agree in the end in explaining it this way.

**Key points.**

- Any one of the afflictions, whichever it may be, is explained as having the power to harm every person in the world.
- Its antidote is nowhere to be found other than in the proper practice of the sublime Dharma, and ordinary medicine — herbal, tree-derived or mineral — cannot help.
- The commentaries differ in which affliction they use as the illustration: some take craving, some take anger directed at a bodhisattva. ⚑
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam uses the story of King Padma of Kashi, who took birth as a rohita fish, to show that even an ordinary illness can harm all the people of the world.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama uses the histories of modern wars and killings to show how a single affliction can harm a great many people.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

The Teacher, the omniscient one, is the one who gives the supreme medicine that heals such fierce sicknesses of the afflictions; he is the one who shows the single method by which every stabbing pain is drawn out. Not to practise according to his word, but to set one's mind elsewhere, is truly the lowest of the low. #### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC19_KKP -->
##### BCAC19_KKP — Humbling the Pride of Jivaka the Youth, King of Physicians

Here the story is told of how kings had three times consecrated Jivaka the Youth as king of physicians, and of how he himself grew proud, saying: "In this world the doctor who heals sicknesses of the mind is the Buddha, the Blessed One, and the doctor who heals sicknesses of the body is I myself." Seeing that if this pride were removed he would see the truth when the Dharma was taught, the Tathagata led him by miraculous power to the Snowy Mountain and had him gather medicines; but he failed to recognize most of them. The Tathagata knew them exactly as they were and pointed them out, and so broke his pride. Since a doctor knows only a little of the sciences and that is not enough, it was taught that the only omniscient doctor is the Buddha, and that there is no medicine other than the sublime Dharma.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-56]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of Craving

Nanda had grown so caught up in his craving for a beautiful woman that he lost heart, clung to that object and became unhappy in mind. To train him, the Teacher led him away and showed him a place where, in every mansion, a god and a goddess dwelt together as a couple — all but one, where a beautiful goddess sat alone with no consort, threading a garland of flowers. He was shown that this was the garland for the birth Nanda himself would take there once he had gone forth. And further, the Teacher led him to the hell realms and showed him a boiling cauldron, saying, "When the fruit of keeping the vows you took out of good aspiration is exhausted, this is where you will be born." Terrified, Nanda kept the ethical discipline of renunciation, meditated, and attained the state of an arhat. In this way the Teacher drew out the stabbing pain of craving.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-14]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — How the Stabbing Pain of Aversion Is Drawn Out

Angulimala, the minister's son, was a man of enormous strength. An evil teacher, moved by jealousy, deceived him blindly, telling him that if he cut off the heads of a thousand men and made a garland of their fingers he would be born in the world of Brahma. He killed nine hundred and ninety-nine, and at the very moment when he was about to kill his own mother as well, the Buddha, knowing that the time to train him had come, went before him and taught the Dharma. Regret arose in Angulimala; he went forth, meditated, and attained the state of an arhat. The relatives of those he had killed were still angry and had no faith, and so the Buddha showed them that, because Angulimala had kept ethical discipline, he would remain always among gods and humans and would not go to the lower realms on account of his former actions.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-15]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Stabbing Pain of Confusion

A monk named Panthaka the Lesser could not hold on to so much as a single word in a whole year; he grew despondent and wanted to turn back. The Buddha, knowing that the time to train him had come, taught him the Dharma, and he attained the state of an arhat. Later, when he was appointed to teach the Dharma to the nuns, a nun who knew the three baskets belittled him, saying that he was the very limit of stupidity; but he sat upon a great teaching throne and, by miraculous power, taught the Dharma extensively, so that a great many people came to have faith.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-16]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Stabbing Pain of Pride

King Prasenajit, lord of thirty-six thousand vassal kings, was full of pride, and because King Kapina of the south had not presented him with homage and offerings he set the law against him. The Buddha emanated himself as a still greater king and showed himself to Prasenajit, and Prasenajit, terrified, had his pride defeated. Seeing the power of that emanated king — he drew a bow no one else could draw, and the sound of it shook the three thousandfold world; he loosed a single arrow which became five, and out of their light there appeared universal monarchs one by one — the vassal kings too were delighted. Then the emanated king withdrew his miraculous display and revealed himself in the body of the Buddha, whereupon King Kapina went forth and attained the state of an arhat.

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Stabbing Pain of Jealousy

When the Teacher was slightly unwell in body, Jivaka prescribed a compound of medicines, of which he took thirty-two measures a day. Jealousy arose in Devadatta, and he demanded that the same be given to him. Jivaka instructed him to take no more than four measures a day, but he did not listen and ate more, so that it worked inward and his life was in danger. Ananda, out of compassion, made a request to the Teacher; and the Teacher, resting evenly in a loving mind that was equal towards both the devoted Rahula and the hostile Devadatta, said, "May Devadatta's sickness be healed this very moment" — and it was healed at once. Even so, Devadatta could not give up his jealousy, not even of Jivaka's skill.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Teacher, the Buddha, Is the One Omniscient Doctor Who Draws Out Every Stabbing Pain of Both Body and Mind

All the commentaries agree in the end that the supreme doctor who heals those sicknesses is the omniscient one, the Buddha, the Blessed One, who pulls out from the very root every stabbing pain of suffering, of body and of mind alike.

###### 2. Not to Act According to the Word of the Supreme Doctor, but to Set One's Mind on Some Other Method, Is the Mark of Great Delusion

It is explained as a state that the victorious ones and their heirs reproach, and that even the gods who guard and protect turn away from.

###### 3. The Story of Humbling the Pride of Jivaka the Youth, King of Physicians, Shows That the Buddha Alone Is the Omniscient Doctor

The Teacher led him to the Snowy Mountain and had him gather medicines, showing thereby that a doctor knows only a little of the sciences and that this is not enough.

###### 4. The Medicine That Draws Out Every Stabbing Pain Is His Word Itself — the Eighty-Four Thousand Collections of Dharma, So to Speak

One must act just as the ways of taking up and giving up are set out within it; and it is explained that to set one's mind instead on some other method is utter delusion, a thing the wise condemn.

###### 5. It Is Not the Sicknesses of the Body (Phlegm and the Rest) That Are the Real Faults, but the Afflictions of the Mind Alone That Show One the Lower Realms; So a Doctor of the Mind Is Greater Than a Doctor of the Body

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains this, illustrating it with the passage quoted from the *Saddharmasmṛtyupasthāna Sūtra*.

###### 6. To Remain in Laziness Rather Than Striving for the Nectar That Dispels Birth, Ageing and Death Is the Fault of Being Corrupted by a Dense Confusion

This is illustrated by the passage quoted from the *Śiṣyalekha*.

###### 7. The Story Section Gives Five Stories in Which the Buddha Healed Each of the Five Stabbing Pains — Craving, Aversion, Confusion, Pride and Jealousy

Nanda's craving, Angulimala's aversion, Panthaka the Lesser's confusion, King Prasenajit's pride and Devadatta's jealousy each illustrate, in its own way, the Teacher's activity of drawing out every stabbing pain.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse answers the question left by the two preceding ones: who is the supreme doctor for that great sickness? The supreme doctor who heals those sicknesses is the omniscient Buddha and no one else; and not to act according to the word of the one who draws out every stabbing pain of both body and mind, but to set one's mind on some other method instead, is utter delusion — a thing the wise condemn. All the commentaries explain it in one voice.

**Key points.**

- The Teacher, the Buddha, alone is explained to be the omniscient doctor who draws out every stabbing pain of both body and mind.
- Not to act according to his word, but to set one's mind on some other method, is a state of great delusion that the victorious ones and their heirs reproach and that even the gods turn away from.
- The story of Jivaka the Youth shows that the only omniscient doctor is the Buddha, and that there is no medicine other than the sublime Dharma.
- The medicine that draws out the stabbing pains is his word itself — the eighty-four thousand collections of Dharma, so to speak; and since it is the afflictions of the mind, not the sicknesses of the body, that are the cause of the lower realms, a doctor of the mind is explained to be the greater.
- The five stories in the story section, in which the Buddha healed each of the five stabbing pains — craving, aversion, confusion, pride and jealousy — illustrate the sense of the verse's phrase "who draws out every stabbing pain."
