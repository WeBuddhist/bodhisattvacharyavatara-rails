## Today's Verse

> If one must act according to a doctor's words
> even when afraid of an ordinary illness,
> what need is there to speak of one
> constantly afflicted by the illness of hundreds of faults, such as attachment? ^2-54

> That single fault can destroy
> all people dwelling on earth.
> If no other medicine to cure them
> can be found anywhere, ^2-55

> then to disregard the words of the all-knowing doctor,
> who extracts all piercing pains,
> is an act of extreme delusion and a cause for scorn. ^2-56

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 54 through 56 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. A new section opens here. Day 32 carried the first of the four powers of confession, regret, and day 33 the second, reliance. These verses begin the third, the power of applying the antidotes. The image running through all three is medical: an ordinary illness makes us do as we are told, so what should this illness make us do? Today moves from feeling sorry and taking refuge to actually treating something.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

The comparison is agreed by all the commentaries and it is worth taking slowly. When we have an ordinary fever we do what the doctor says, including the parts we would rather skip. The afflictions are also an illness. This one has been in us without a break for as long as we have existed, and there are hundreds of them. One of them alone, the verse says, could destroy everyone alive. And there is no second medicine for it anywhere.

Khenpo Kunzang Palden adds the part that is easy to miss. Doing good in general is not the same as applying an antidote. He uses a farming image. Sow a single pea in among a great deal of barley: the barley comes up thick all around, and the pea comes up too, entirely unharmed. Merit is the barley. The particular misdeed is the pea. You can accumulate a great deal of the first, he says, and it will not defeat the second unless you deliberately aim it there, as the remedy for that.

Which is why the instruction is not simply to be good today. It is to treat one specific thing as an illness and take the medicine for it.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, the first time anger or wanting rises in me strongly, I will name it as the illness it is and apply one remedy to that, rather than trying to be generally better.

**Explanation:** _(Taming the mind)_ The move here is narrow and deliberate. When it comes up, and it will, I will stop long enough to say what it is: this is anger, this is wanting. Then I will do one thing aimed at it. For anger, look at the situation from the other person's side until it moves at all. For wanting, look at what the thing will actually be worth to me in a week. It does not have to work. It has to be pointed. Vague good intentions spread over the day are the barley in Kunzang Palden's field, growing thickly while the pea sits there untouched. One affliction, named, with one remedy aimed at it, is worth more today than a general resolution to improve.
