## Combined Commentary Summaries

### 1. Structural Categorization and Purpose of the Analogy
All three commentators agree that Verse 1-10 uses the analogy of a gold-making alchemical elixir (Skt. *rasāyana*; Tib. *gser 'gyur rtsi*) to illustrate how generating the mind of enlightenment (bodhicitta) transforms what is fundamentally impure and base into a supreme, pure state. This represents the very first of a series of analogies praising the benefits of bodhicitta.

*   **Structural Divisions:**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** places this under the section "praising the benefits of generating the mind by way of analogies," identifying it as the first of six analogies: "the analogy of transforming the low into the supreme."
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** categorizes it under "praise through five analogies," with this being the first: "praise through the analogy of the gold-making elixir."
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** structures this under "praising benefits through analogies has six parts," identifying it as the first analogy that "shows that what is base is transformed into what is supreme."
*   **The Core Transformation:**
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** explains that by way of "cause and nature," bodhicitta transforms our impure physical body into the priceless, precious body of a Buddha (the Victor).
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** elaborates that this body, composed of "many impure substances," is transformed into the body of a perfect Buddha, which is endowed with the qualities of a priceless wish-fulfilling jewel and protects from all degradations.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly explains that the elixir takes this body, which is "impure by nature," and transforms it into the priceless, precious body of a Buddha.

### 2. Scriptural Source and the Quicksilver Analogy
All three commentators trace the scriptural source of this gold-making elixir analogy back to the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* (the *Array of Stalks Sūtra*).

*   **The Quicksilver Details:**
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** provides the most detailed alchemical explanation. He defines the gold-making elixir as a refined type of "quicksilver" (Tib. *dngul chu*) called "that which appears as gold" (*gser du snang ba*). He explains that a single ounce of this quicksilver can transform a thousand ounces of iron into gold, and that the iron cannot exhaust or turn the quicksilver back into iron.
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** synthesizes the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* passage, stating that a single ounce of the alchemical elixir called "Gold-Appearing" can transform a thousand ounces of iron into gold.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** also cites the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* with the same ratio (one ounce to a thousand ounces of iron), describing the elixir as the "supreme gold-making elixir."

### 3. Doctrinal Application of the Analogy
The commentators map the elements of the alchemy metaphor directly onto Buddhist doctrine:

*   **The "Iron" (Base Metal):** Represents our impure, ordinary physical body, as well as the accumulated "iron of karma and mental afflictions" (*las dang nyon mongs*).
*   **The "Gold" (Noble Metal):** Represents the priceless, precious body of a Buddha and the "golden color of omniscience" (*thams cad mkhyen pa*).
*   **The "Elixir" (Agent of Transformation):** Represents the mind of perfect enlightenment (bodhicitta), which must be "held by the wisdom that dedicates all virtue" (*dge rtsa bsngo ba's shes rab*).
*   **Inexhaustibility and Corruption-Resistance:**
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Ngulchu Thokme** both emphasize a crucial doctrinal point from the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra*: just as the single ounce of quicksilver cannot be corrupted or turned back into iron by the thousand ounces of iron, the single elixir of generating the mind of omniscience/perfect enlightenment can never be corrupted, afflicted, or exhausted by any amount of karma and mental afflictions.
    *   Rather, when conjoined with the wisdom of dedication, it completely consumes the iron of karma and afflictions and turns all phenomena into the golden color of omniscience.
