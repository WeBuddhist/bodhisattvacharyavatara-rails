### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable point, which is easy to understand and encouraging. The *Avatamsaka Sutra* and the *Jewel Cloud Sutra* teach the trainings of bodhisattvas. Those six volumes of the Avatamsaka are very important, so we must study them. In particular, the *Akashagarbha Sutra* teaches the root downfalls. These include the five downfalls taught to a king, one for ministers, and the eight root downfalls of a beginner bodhisattva. Therefore, a bodhisattva who has taken the engaging vow must first look at that sutra.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Because the sutras make appear all the trainings in what bodhisattvas are to adopt and abandon, therefore the sutras should be read with effort. #### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus — for example, sutras such as the *Avataṃsaka* (མདོ་སྡེ་ཕལ་པོ་ཆེ) and the *Jewel Cloud* teach those matters of the bodhisattva's training. Those six volumes of the great Avataṃsaka (ཕལ་ཆེན་པོ་ཏི་དྲུག) are extremely important, so they must be studied. In particular, a bodhisattva who has taken the engaging vow (འཇུག་སྡོམ) and holds the vows must definitely look first at the *Akashagarbha Sutra*, because it teaches the groups of root downfalls (རྩ་ལྟུང) — the five downfalls taught to a king, one for ministers, the eight root downfalls of a beginner bodhisattva, and so on.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-104]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Moreover, by meeting and looking at the sutras, the bodhisattva's trainings become visible and known. For that reason one should look at and read the sutras; and in particular, the sutra of the bodhisattva Akashagarbha (the *Akashagarbha Sutra*) clearly teaches the classification of the eighteen root downfalls of a bodhisattva, together with the means of repair should a root downfall occur. Therefore it must be looked at first — that is, right at the very beginning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-104]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

What is taught here, and the other trainings spoken by the Buddha, should also be known by reading the sutras, because in the sutras the trainings appear — they arise clearly. Even among the sutras, the *Akashagarbha Sutra* should be looked at first, because it teaches the downfalls and the means of repairing them, and these need to be known first.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-178]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

This life story of Palbyung (དཔལ་འབྱུང་བ), or this perfectly pure textual tradition, and the other things to be trained in that the Buddha, the Blessed One, taught should also be known by reading the various sutras, because the endless ways of adopting and abandoning that bodhisattvas are to train in appear in the sutras. Therefore one should read the vast sutras gradually; and in particular, one should look first of all at the sutra requested by the noble Akashagarbha, because it clearly teaches the bodhisattva's root downfalls together with the means of repairing them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-104]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

"The life story of Palbyung just explained, and" — so Master Gelha (སློབ་དཔོན་དགེ་ལྷ) explains; and "this text, the *Engaging in the Bodhisattva's Conduct* (སྤྱོད་འཇུག), which shows well how to train in conduct elsewhere, and" — so he says. As for the other trainings spoken by the Buddha that are not mentioned here, they must be known by reading the various sutras of the Great Vehicle. Why? Because in sutras such as the *Jewel Cloud* the bodhisattvas' places of training in how to adopt and abandon appear extensively and clearly. Therefore the profound and vast sutras that teach what is to be trained in should be read and known gradually, and one should also train just as they present it. In particular, the noble *Akashagarbha Sutra* must be looked at first, right after taking the engaging vow (འཇུག་སྡོམ). Why? Because the identification of the root downfalls that ruin the bodhisattva vow, together with the means of repairing those downfalls, is taught clearly in that very sutra, and it is extremely important for beginners to know these first.

As an illustration (རྒྱུ་དཔེར): in the *Akashagarbha Sutra* itself it also says, "The root downfalls are to be examined by this," it is said. The meaning of that sutra, as gathered in the *Compendium of Trainings* (Śikṣāsamuccaya), is:

> "Stealing the property of the Three Jewels / is held to be a downfall of defeat," and so on, up to: "These are the root downfalls, / the cause of the great hells for beings. / In a dream, sitting before noble Akashagarbha, / one should confess them."

As for the means of repairing those root downfalls, from that sutra:

> A bodhisattva calls on the name of Akashagarbha, prostrates, and makes the request; Akashagarbha shows himself in his own form, or in the form of a brahmin and the like, and has the downfalls confessed, and so on; and, with these words: "Dawn (སྐྱ་རེང), O Dawn, great compassionate one of great fortune, as soon as you appear in Jambudvīpa, please cover me with compassion," and so on — having spoken these words and gone to sleep in bed, as soon as the dawn breaks, in that dream the form of that bodhisattva sits before one and has one confess the root downfalls, and so on, is taught.

The ritual for making the request in the mantra tradition is also taught in that sutra.

Moreover, the root downfalls taught in the *Skill in Means Sutra* (ཐབས་ལ་མཁས་པའི་མདོ) and others — from "giving up bodhicitta entirely" and so on, up to "asserting a counterfeit as if it were Dharma" (ཆོས་ལྟར་བཅོས་མ་རྗོད་པ), as gathered in the *Compendium of Trainings* — are also shown here in the great commentaries and elsewhere; the differences in how these are divided and gathered, and how they do not contradict the root downfalls taught in the *Akashagarbha Sutra*, and so on, are explained in the *Thub-gongs* (ཐུབ་དགོངས) and others. These are, from the *Compendium of Trainings*:

> "The bodhisattva's vows / come extensively from the Great Vehicle," and: "By always looking at the sutras"

— it is just as that says. And moreover, from the same text:

> The foundations of the bodhisattva's training appear for the most part in the sutras; from this and that sutra the bodhisattva's all-round conduct and the foundations of the bodhisattva's training are laid out — because it is said so. Therefore, if one does not look at them, one will not even know when a downfall occurs and so will not give it up; so always show reverence by looking at the sutras.

Thus it is said that if one does not look at the sutras one does not know downfalls, non-downfalls, and so on; so, in order to know these and then adopt and abandon, one must look at the sutras.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-104]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Thus it is said. Moreover, in the sutras the bodhisattva's trainings — obtaining the bodhisattva's vow of generating the mind when it has not yet been obtained, guarding it from degenerating once obtained, the means of repair if it degenerates, and so on — appear just as they are. For that reason the sutras should be read; and among those sutras, the sutra of the bodhisattva Akashagarbha in particular must be looked at first, because in that very sutra the classification of the eighteen root downfalls that contradict the bodhisattva's vow of generating the mind, and the means of repairing a root downfall should one occur, and so on, are clearly shown.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-104]]

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Since the bodhisattva's trainings appear clearly in the sutras, the sutras should be met and read. In particular, a bodhisattva who has taken the engaging vow should look at the *Akashagarbha Sutra* first.

**Key points.**

- The trainings of bodhisattvas are taught in sutras such as the *Avataṃsaka* and the *Jewel Cloud*.
- The sutras teach how to guard the bodhisattva vow, once obtained, from degenerating, and the means of repair if it does degenerate; if one does not look at the sutras, one will not know downfalls or their repair.
- Because the *Akashagarbha Sutra* teaches the root downfalls and the means of repairing them, it should be looked at first.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Alternatively, for the time being, one may examine / the concise compendium of all sutras" — so it says. Just as was explained a moment ago, concerning how to practice giving up, guarding, purifying, and increasing, the *Compendium of Trainings* composed by Shantideva himself — a text that settles these matters through good explanation, drawing on many sutra sources (མདཱུའི་ཁུངས) — must be looked at again and again. That *Compendium of Trainings* is not an especially large work (ཕོན་ཆེན་པོ). It is easy to understand, but if it is too expansive and one cannot gather the meaning, then "alternatively" — Shantideva said that for the time being one should also look at the *Compendium of Sutras*, which is taught in a concise manner. Although this has been translated into Tibetan, it is not included in the Tengyur (བསྟན་འགྱུར).

In the lines "composed by the noble Nāgārjuna, / both also should be examined with effort," Gyaltsab Thamchad Khyenpa (རྒྱལ་ཚབ་ཐམས་ཅད་མཁྱེན་པ) said "the second" (གཉིས་པ): since the noble Nāgārjuna composed a *Compendium of Sutras* and it has been translated into Tibetan, one should look at the second, the *Compendium of Sutras*. Some, going by the presence of na-ro (ན་རོ), say "both" (གཉིས་པོ), since there is also a *Compendium of Trainings* composed by the noble Nāgārjuna. "Composed by the noble Nāgārjuna, / the second also should be examined with effort," or else "composed by the noble Nāgārjuna, / both also should be examined with effort" — there are these two explanations that differ according to whether na-ro (ན་རོ) is present or not; please investigate.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-105]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Next, the master's own *Compendium of Trainings* must be looked at. Why? Because what bodhisattvas are always to study and practice is shown extensively and in detail in that very text, so the one known as the *Compendium of All Trainings* must definitely, without fail, be looked at. And as for this, since looking once or twice does not enable one to fix the points to be trained in properly in the mind, one must look again and again, and make what is expressed familiar to and trained in the mind.

If one asks, "What places of training does it teach?": First, reflecting that the leisures and endowments are hard to find and of great meaning, one generates the wish to take the essence in this life. Then, generating firm faith — faith in general and, in particular, the faith of contemplating the qualities of the Great Vehicle — one generates aspiring bodhicitta (སྨོན་སེམས). Then, having taken the engaging vow (འཇུག་སྡོམ), when it explains what is to be trained in, there are three: showing the general outline of what is to be trained in, generating the wish to guard, and the actual way of guarding. The last has two: training in what is general and in what is specific. In the context of training in what is general, one must learn from what is taught extensively by citing the source of the *Jewel Cloud*, and:

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Alternatively, for the time being, one may examine / the concise compendium of all sutras" — when [someone] asked, "Where are those two treatises that say to refer to the *Compendium of All Trainings* and the *Compendium of All Sutras*?", [the answer was]: "They are the treatise whose nature is ten chapters. I wrote the *Compendium of All Sutras* and the *Compendium of All Trainings*, those two, in the pandit's script (པནྜི་ཏའི་ཡི་གེ); they are in the upper space (ཐོག་གི་གསེང) of where he lives. If you look there, it will be fine," he said, and so he also gave the complete pith instructions of both the *Way of Life* and the *Compendium of Trainings* — such is the history.

From then on, many Indian scholars have given teachings and listened to teachings based on this. For example, Master Sherjung Lodrö (ཤེར་འབྱུང་བློ་གྲོས) was the gatekeeper of the western gate of Nālandā in India. When many who wanted to debate came to those great Buddhist monastic seats of learning in India, there were gatekeepers; and these were not gatekeepers armed with swords, guns, and arrows. The great paṇḍita Naropa also has a history of having served as the northern gatekeeper of Nālandā. Gradually, as the teachings flourished in Tibet and many sutras and treatises were translated, among them the great commentary composed by Western-Gate Sherjung Lodrö (ནུབ་སྒོ་བ་ཤེར་འབྱུང་བློ་གྲོས) is now in the Tengyur. The *Compendium of Trainings* is also in the Tengyur. As for the *Compendium of All Sutras* composed by Master Shantideva, although it has been translated into Tibetan, it is not in the Tengyur and so was not entered into the printing, said Khunu Lama Rinpoche (ཁུ་ནུ་བླ་མ་རིན་པོ་ཆེ).

After this text appeared in the land of Tibet, many Tibetan scholars, relying on it, generated bodhicitta not yet generated; and many holy beings arose who composed many pith instructions on practice (ཉམས་བཞེས) for increasing it higher and higher once generated. If we count the commentaries, it was said that there are probably around a hundred Tibetan commentaries. It is probably the one treatise that has the largest number of Tibetan commentaries. From what I have seen, for example: the commentary by Buton, the Omniscient (བུ་སྟོན་ཐམས་ཅད་མཁྱེན་པ); the commentary by the holy lama Sönam Gyaltsen (བླ་མ་དམ་པ་བསོད་ནམས་རྒྱལ་མཚན); and many commentaries by later scholars.

Although Je Rinpoche (Tsongkhapa) did not write a commentary on the *Way of Life*, he taught the difficult points of the *Way of Life* in the *Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path* (Lamrim Chenmo): in teaching the six perfections, in the sections on the practice of generosity and on the perfection of diligence, he took the *Way of Life* as his basis. Furthermore, in the calm-abiding section he cited, in a scattered way, within the Lamrim Chenmo the *Way of Life*'s methods of guarding mindfulness and introspection. He taught clearly, as if opening one's eyes, those difficult points that other commentaries had not brought out. In particular, he drew the difficult points of the wisdom chapter at the beginning of that *Illuminating the Intent* of the Middle Way (དབུ་མ་དགོངས་པ་རབ་གསལ), and:

"If the root of the teachings is being a monk, / being a monk itself stands as difficult."

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

"This is a condensation of the bodhisattva's vows," it is said; and [the text] shows clearly, through twelve means — four each for the three of body, resources, and roots of virtue, namely giving to others, guarding, purifying, and increasing.

If one cannot look at and grow familiar with such an extensive text, then "alternatively, for the time being" — first look at the master's own *Compendium of All Sutras*, which concisely gathers the trainings spoken in the various sutras, and afterwards look also at others such as the *Compendium of Trainings*. Likewise, the two, the *Compendium of All Trainings* and the *Compendium of All Sutras* of the glorious protector, noble Nāgārjuna, should also be looked at with effort, because they clearly teach the many trainings not explained in this treatise.

Moreover, for reasons such as that Nāgārjuna's *Compendium of Trainings* is not seen in today's Tengyur, some explain "the second, the *Compendium of All Sutras* composed by the noble Nāgārjuna, should also be looked at with effort." Nāgārjuna's *Compendium of Sutras* teaches in many ways how the leisures and faith in the teaching are hard to find; that, further, generating bodhicitta is hard to find; likewise that great compassion for beings is hard to find; and that, beyond these, abandoning the karmic obscurations that wound the bodhisattva and the states of mind connected with them, the deeds of Māra, abandoning the Dharma, and so on, is extremely hard to find. And further, the stages of the path that are hard to realize are shown clearly and extensively by the Victors' heir Shantideva in the *Compendium of Trainings*, it is said.

Having looked, one must train in accord with them, which is: *(the block ends here, mid-sentence, as in the rail)*.
