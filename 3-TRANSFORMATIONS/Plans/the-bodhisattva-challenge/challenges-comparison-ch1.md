# Daily Challenges — Chapter 1 (Days 1–14)

English plan vs. Tibetan plan, side by side. The English plan carries one challenge per day; the Tibetan plan's challenge section (`ཉམས་སུ་ལེན་ཚུལ།`) also carries one named challenge per day in Chapter 1. The Tibetan column below gives the English translation of that challenge. The **Aim** column states, in English, what each day's challenge is training.

| Day (verses) | English challenge | Tibetan challenge (translated) | Aim of the challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** (1.1–3) | Sazang says the first kind of faith that this practice builds is simply trusting that what you do has consequences. Most of us believe this in theory. The next time you are about to snap at someone you live with, or let a small lie slide because it is easier, ask yourself whether you actually believe it in that moment. That question is the practice. | **Taming Pride and Arrogance:** In daily life, while working or studying, if pride arising from a desire for fame and praise arises in me, I will remember the humble conduct of the great Bodhisattva Shantideva and correct my motivation. | Catch pride and the craving for praise as it arises, and correct the underlying motivation. |
| **Day 2** (1.4–5) | Ngulchu Thokme quotes the sutras directly: do not let this opportunity go to waste. Think of one thing you keep meaning to do that actually matters — not a task on a list, but something to do with how you want to live. Something you believe in but keep deferring. Ask yourself honestly what you are waiting for. | **Seizing the Moment When a Virtuous Mind Arises:** In daily life, when a momentary thought of wishing to benefit sentient beings or of doing virtue arises, recognizing it to be as exceedingly rare as a flash of lightning in the dark of night, I will immediately put it into practice. | Act on a virtuous impulse the instant it arises — rare as lightning in the dark — instead of deferring. |
| **Day 3** (1.6–8) | Verse 1.8 is addressed to anyone who wants to end suffering and find happiness — which is everyone. Sazang grounds this in something concrete: birth, aging, sickness, and death. The next time you feel something difficult in your own body or life — tiredness, a moment of real pain, the weight of a bad day — pause long enough to notice that every other person is experiencing some version of this too. Let that recognition be the beginning of a wish. | **Cultivating Bodhicitta in Any Situation:** Bodhicitta (the Mind of Awakening) is the most beneficial antidote, no matter what situation one encounters. Today, if anger or attachment arises in me, I will immediately remember Bodhicitta. If I encounter difficulties, I will endure them through the generation of this awakening mind. If I see others suffering, I will rely on Bodhicitta through compassion. In this way, in whatever situation I find myself, I will rely solely on Bodhicitta as the antidote. | Use bodhicitta as the single antidote in every circumstance — anger, attachment, hardship, or seeing others suffer. |
| **Day 4** (1.9–11) | Sazang describes the person in verse 1.9 as "bound by the fetters of karma and afflictions" — and in the very same instant, elevated above gods and humans. The transformation hinges entirely on the generation of the wish. Today, when you feel stuck or caught in the same pattern again, try actually forming it — not as a recitation but as a real intention: may I be free from this, and may everyone else caught in something similar be free too. | **Extracting the Essence of Bodhicitta:** Without relying on worldly wealth, but by relying on this very body, I will hold Bodhicitta firmly in my heart, and make all my daily actions accord with the conduct of a Bodhisattva. Since Bodhicitta is the supreme wealth praised by the Buddha, it is the best method to extract the essence from this impure body. In all my actions today, I will strive solely for the benefit of others with the confidence and joy of being a Bodhisattva. | Hold bodhicitta as the supreme wealth and direct every daily action toward the benefit of others. |
| **Day 5** (1.12–14) | Ngulchu Thokme cites a sutra: a bodhisattva who relies on the mind of enlightenment has no fear of any enemies of negative actions. Think of something you've done that you still feel bad about — a moment of cowardice, a harm you caused that you haven't been able to shake. Try turning it into a wish: because I know how this feels, may everyone caught in regret like this find a way through. That's the brave companion the verse is pointing at. | **Ensuring Virtue is Not Wasted:** Even if I perform a minor virtue in my daily life, I will connect it with the generation of Bodhicitta, dedicating it toward the benefit of all sentient beings. By doing so, my virtue will not be exhausted but will continuously flourish like a plantain tree. | Link even the smallest virtue to bodhicitta and dedicate it to all beings so it grows inexhaustibly. |
| **Day 6** (1.15–16) | Maitreya says in the sutra: those who wish for awakening are rare, but those who actually enter the conduct are even rarer. Pick one thing in your practice or your life where you've been in the wishing phase for a long time — intending to, meaning to, planning to. Notice what keeps you there. You don't have to act on it today. But be honest about the gap. | **Putting Engaging Bodhicitta into Practice:** Merely generating the aspiration is not enough; whatever work I do during the day, I will actively engage in actions that benefit others or the practice of the Six Perfections (Paramitas), putting the engaging mind into actual practice. | Move from mere aspiration to actually engaging — benefit others or practice the six perfections in real activity. |
| **Day 7** (1.17–19) | Ngulchu Thokme adds a caveat: this continuous merit only flows if the mind of enlightenment doesn't degenerate. Commitment can erode quietly without you noticing. Today, check in with the resolve you made at the start of this practice. Is it still alive as an intention, or has it become more of a habit? Renewing a commitment is not a sign of failure — it's the practice itself. | **Striving in Engaging Bodhicitta:** Making prayers for the sake of all sentient beings is not enough; I must absolutely strive in the practice of the engaging mind—such as the practice of generosity—which directly benefits others. | Go beyond prayer and aspiration to concrete acts that directly benefit others, such as generosity. |
| **Day 8** (1.20–22) | Sazang's story turns on a single genuine thought in a moment of real pain. Think of one specific person you know who is dealing with something hard — an illness, a loss, a situation they can't get out of. Just once, form the wish as clearly as you can: may this person be free from what they're going through. Don't evaluate whether you did it right. Verse 1.21 says the intention itself is what carries the weight. | **Habituating to Small Good Intentions:** In daily life, even when noticing the minor illnesses and sufferings of others, I will naturally generate the beneficial thought, "If only I could dispel these," thereby habituating my mindstream to goodness. | Habituate good intention starting small — on seeing even minor suffering, naturally wish to relieve it. |
| **Day 9** (1.23–25) | When you notice goodwill arising toward someone today — a colleague who's overwhelmed, a friend dealing with something hard — feel for where that wish naturally stops. It probably ends somewhere around "I hope things get better for them soon." Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen distinguishes the bodhisattva's wish from that: not just better, but completely free from suffering at its root. Try catching one such moment today and extending it past its usual endpoint, all the way to: may this person be entirely free. | **Examining the Selfish Mind:** During daily activities, when the selfish thought of "I want to be happy" arises, I will remember that such a mind has never even accomplished my own purpose in the past. Reversing this, I will generate the courage to accomplish the welfare of others. | See that self-cherishing has never even secured one's own aims, and reverse it into courage for others' welfare. |
| **Day 10** (1.26–28) | The next time you do something that offers momentary relief but leaves you worse — eating past hunger, scrolling past the point of rest, saying something cutting when you wanted connection — pause and name what you were actually seeking. Ngulchu Thokme reads this verse as a teaching about confused means, not bad intention: the wanting was real, but the reach was wrong. The moth doesn't want to burn. | **Examining One's Own Conduct:** I will carefully examine my daily conduct with these thoughts: "While desiring happiness, have I accumulated its cause, which is virtue? Or, under the influence of ignorance, am I engaging in non-virtuous actions that destroy my own happiness like an enemy?" | Examine your own conduct: are you building the causes of happiness, or — through confusion — destroying it like an enemy? |
| **Day 11** (1.29–30) | The next time you do something kind for someone — hold a door, send a message checking on a friend, help a colleague with something they didn't ask about — pause for a moment afterward and look honestly at what you were hoping for. Not the big things, but the small ones: a thank-you, a feeling of being generous, a sense that you're the kind of person who helps. According to Sazang Mati Panchen, that trace of expectation is exactly what bodhicitta does not carry. You don't need to eliminate the expectation today — just see it clearly. | **Becoming an Incomparable Spiritual Friend:** Pledging to become a good spiritual friend (Kalyanamitra) or companion who dispels the difficulties of all sentient beings, I will bring this into my daily practice, starting from minor actions that benefit others. | Resolve to become a friend who relieves all beings' difficulties, beginning with small acts of benefit. |
| **Day 12** (1.31–32) | Notice the next flash of contempt you feel toward someone you have quietly written off. Before it hardens into a settled judgment, remember that you cannot see all the good they may still give rise to. | **Being an Unsolicited Friend:** Before being asked for help, as soon as I see someone falling into difficulty, I will voluntarily go to them and engage in the conduct of assisting and benefiting them. | Help before being asked — the moment you see someone in difficulty, go to them voluntarily. |
| **Day 13** (1.33–34) | If a genuinely good person comes to mind today, like a teacher, a friend, or anyone who is kind, let yourself simply admire them for a moment. Nothing more is needed. | **Investigating One's Own Mind:** During the day, as soon as I see thoughts of anger or jealousy arising in my mind, remembering, "This will lead me to the lower realms," I will rely on mindfulness to quickly pacify them. | Watch the mind, catch anger or jealousy the moment it arises, and pacify it quickly with mindfulness. |
| **Day 14** (1.35–36) | If someone irritates you today, a rude remark or a cold reply, see if you can **wish them well anyway**. Even a little is enough. | **Enduring Harm and Transforming it into Virtue:** When others speak harsh words to me or cause me harm, rather than generating anger, I will accept it, thinking, "This is an excellent condition for me to practice patience," and strive to transform it into the path of virtue. | Endure harm without anger — treat insult or injury as a welcome chance to train patience and turn it into virtue. |
