# Day 12 — The charity that earns praise, and what earns more

## Opening

Chapter 1 has been building a case for the awakening mind. Today that argument turns concrete. Verses 32 and 33 place an ordinary donor beside a bodhisattva and ask which one deserves the praise we so readily give. Verse 34 then sharpens the point: our attitude toward those who hold this mind carries more weight than we assume.

## From the Tradition

When a canteen worker snaps at the people in the food line, then serves them anyway, we still tend to call them generous. The food arrived. That counts. Sazang Mati Panchen, a 14th-century Sakya master, catalogs what makes this charity limited: a small group of recipients, ordinary food, a manner marked by contempt and disrespect, and a benefit lasting half a day. Yet society names such a person virtuous. The commentators use this not to dismiss ordinary kindness but to calibrate our sense of recognition. If we already praise that, we should be able to recognize something larger. The same logic that credits the canteen worker should, with clearer eyes, direct far more recognition toward those who give without limit, to everyone, without end.

## Today's Practice Challenge

The next time someone does something for you, notice the quality of their attention. Were they slightly put out? Distracted? Going through the motions? Sazang Mati Panchen says verse 32 is specifically pointing at that gap between the act and what is inside it. See if you can bring a different quality of attention to one small thing you do for someone today — not more effort, just more presence.
