## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the forty-seventh, forty-eighth, and forty-ninth verses of the fifth chapter. This practice is to look at our own mind for a moment, before we move our body or speak. It is very easy. Yet very few people actually do it.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following points, which are easy to understand and encouraging.

The small faults in our mind are not very obvious, so they are hard to recognize. Restlessness makes the mind scattered and unsteady. Mockery means making fun of others and putting them down. Pride fills the mind with seven kinds of pride. Arrogance means becoming haughty by relying on five things: family, looks, youth, learning, and strength. Exposing faults means searching for the faults of others and announcing them. Going back on our word means turning away from what we promised and not supporting it. So we must always check whether these small faults are in our own mind.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, before I do anything with my body or my speech, I will examine my own mind.

**Explanation:** *(Wisdom Practice)* Before we speak and before we act with our body, we must look at the intention in our mind. If we begin an action while hopes, fears and anger are in the mind, we make mistakes and feel distress. If we examine the mind first and act calmly, both we and others will benefit.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

On any occasion, whether speaking words or moving the body, as in going about, one must examine with the mind whether the basis of the action is suitable or unsuitable, and do what accords with reason.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whenever one wishes to carry out a bodily action — to go somewhere, near or far, or even merely to move a limb such as the hand — or wishes to speak, telling or saying various kinds of words to others, then even so, one should first examine in one's own mind, "Is the motivating intention behind this going and this speaking virtuous or non-virtuous?" and then the bodhisattva (or "the steady one") should act in a way that is reasonable and fitting. That is, one must give up engaging in non-virtue and engage in virtuous action.

Whenever one comes to move the body, or wishes to speak, even so, one should first examine in one's own mind the motivating intention behind these, and, without falling under the power of the afflictions, steadily act reasonably — that is, in a way accompanied by virtue.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whenever one wishes to set the activity of the body in motion and movement, or wishes to speak and utter the activity of speech, even so, before either of them one should first examine well in one's own mind whether or not an afflicted motivating intention is present; then, with the mind perfectly steady, one should abandon what is unfitting and afflicted, and accomplish virtuous actions that are fitting and reasonable.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whenever one wishes to carry out an action by moving and stirring the body, or wishes to speak the words of verbal action, even so, one should not engage in that action straight away. Rather, one should first examine well one's own mind — whether the motivating intention is virtuous or non-virtuous, afflicted or not, and so on — and, without letting oneself fall under the power of the afflictions, the steady one, the bodhisattva (or: with the mind made perfectly steady), should accomplish the actions prompted by a virtuous mind, in a way that is fitting and reasonable, and abandon the actions that are unfitting and wholly prompted by the afflictions. In brief, the meaning is that one must engage and refrain in whatever way is reasonable.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Whenever one wishes to move the body even slightly, through shifting and stirring of the hands, feet, and so on, or wishes to speak words to others — on such an occasion too, one should hold oneself with mindfulness and awareness, and first examine and investigate whether the motivating intention in one's own mind is virtuous or non-virtuous. If it is prompted by a non-virtuous mind, one should abandon that very thing; if it is prompted by a virtuous mind, one should engage in that very thing, and so on. In brief: the steady one, that is, the bodhisattva, should engage properly in bodily actions and in speaking words and the like, in a way that is reasonable and fitting — that is, in accord with the Dharma. This is the meaning.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Examine Your Motivating Intention Before Any Action of Body or Speech

This verse newly sets out, among the categories of things to be abandoned, the instruction that when one engages in an action of body or speech, one must first examine the motivating intention of one's own mind. The commentaries are in agreement that, having examined whether the intention is virtuous or non-virtuous, one should engage by the path that is reasonable and fitting for that occasion.

###### 2. Abandon an Action Prompted by a Non-Virtuous Intention; Engage in One Prompted by a Virtuous Mind

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both make clear that one decides what to engage in and what to avoid in light of the motivating intention: if an action is prompted by a non-virtuous intention or by an affliction, that very action should be abandoned; if it is prompted by a virtuous mind, one should engage in that very action, and so on.

###### 3. Even a Small Movement of the Hand or Foot Calls for Examining the Mind

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden adds in passing that the bodily and verbal actions whose motivation should be examined include even merely moving a limb such as the hand, showing that one must keep in mind even the subtle places where the afflictions arise.

###### 4. This Verse Opens a Five-Part Teaching on How to Act When Afflictions Arise

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Ngulchu Thogme both state that, from this verse onward, a new subject of five parts begins — how to act on the particular occasions when one wishes for afflictions to arise, when there is restlessness, when there is gain and honor, when there is concern for others' welfare, and when there is anger and discouragement, and the like.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* teaches that, at the very start of any movement of the body or any speech, one should first examine whether the motivating intention in one's own mind is virtuous, and engage and refrain only by way of what is virtuous, reasonable, and fitting. This is a point on which all the commentators speak with one voice.

**Key points.**

- **Examine the mind first.** When one wishes to move the body or to speak, one must first examine the state of one's own mind and whether the motivating intention is virtuous or non-virtuous.
- **What to do with the result.** If the motivating intention is afflicted and non-virtuous, that action must be abandoned; if it is virtuous, one should engage in that action.
- **Even the smallest movement.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden teaches that one must examine one's mind even for the subtlest bodily movement, as slight as moving the hand or foot.
- **The opening of a five-part section.** Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Ngulchu Thogme both teach that this verse opens a section of five parts on what to do when afflictions, restlessness, gain and honor, concern for others' welfare, anger and discouragement, and the like arise.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Whenever one comes close to meeting some basis for the arising of attachment or aversion in one's mind, at that time one should not act with the body and should not speak with speech; and one should remain like wood, without the mind sending out any conceptual thought or analysis toward that object either.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Thus, if one genuinely ensures that these twenty-seven gateways of misconduct — even the very subtlest — do not arise in one's own continuum, then the downfalls and faults of the three vows (individual liberation, bodhisattva, and tantric) will not occur. It is therefore exceedingly important to take them to heart, to understand their meaning as it is, to gain certainty, and to strive properly in adopting and discarding. Whenever one's own mind becomes attached to and fixated on what is pleasing — friends, possessions, and the like — and wishes to be angry, the mind turning hostile toward unpleasing objects such as enemies, then at that time one should not carry out, as actions, the bodily activities prompted by these states — going, moving about, and so on — and should not speak words about them with speech. Not following after that mind, with the three doors of body, speech, and mind doing nothing, one should remain able to hold firm, like wood. ##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whenever one's own mind is intensely attached to pleasing objects and wishes to be angry and averse toward unpleasing ones, at that time one should not carry out any bodily action prompted by these, nor speak any words with speech; for if one did, even the bodily and verbal expressions would arise as afflicted. Therefore, drawing in the mind that engages external objects in connection with such a motivating intention, and bearing in mind the meaning of "all phenomena are without an agent, because they are free of all activity," and so making the antidote hold firm, one should remain steady, like wood, doing nothing at all that carries the transformations of attachment and aversion.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

In properly guarding the training that accords with the Dharma, without letting it decline, there are twenty-seven gateways through which misconduct arises in one's own continuum. First, whenever one's own mind becomes attached to and fixated on friends, wealth and possessions, and the like; and second, whenever one's own mind wishes to be resentful toward others who are unpleasing, such as harmful enemies — at such times too, one should not act with the body and should not speak words with speech, and should have the mind remain like wood, able to hold firm. This is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-48]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Remaining Like a Tree When Attachment or Aversion Arises

This verse sets out the method of abandonment for the first of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, namely the occasion when attachment and aversion arise. The commentaries unanimously state that, when one meets an object that is pleasing or unpleasing, one must not act with body or speech, but must remain like wood.

###### 2. Even the Subtlest Gateways of Misconduct Must Be Stopped to Protect the Three Vows

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden gives the general meaning: ensuring that even the subtle and subtlest gateways of misconduct do not arise in one's continuum is explained to be important as a cause for the downfalls and faults of the three vows (individual liberation, bodhisattva, and tantric) not arising.

###### 3. Attachment and Anger Are Counted Separately as the First and Second Gateways

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk distinguishes the two — attachment as the first and anger as the second of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct — and sets down their numbers separately.

###### 4. Acting From Attachment or Aversion Makes Even Bodily and Verbal Expression Afflicted

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that an action linked with the motivating intention of attachment and aversion causes even the bodily and verbal expression to arise as afflicted, and he shows how to hold the antidote firm by way of the view that all phenomena are without an agent.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse begins the enumeration of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, and settles the essential point that when attachment or resentment arises in one's own mind, one must remain unmoving, like a tree or a piece of wood, doing nothing at all with body or speech.

**Key points.**

- **The first instruction.** When one's own mind is attached to what is pleasing, or wishes to be angry at what is unpleasing, one must not act with the body or speak with speech, and must have the mind remain like wood.
- **Why the subtle gateways matter.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden teaches that if these twenty-seven gateways of misconduct are allowed to arise without being stopped, even slightly, they obstruct the purity of the three vows (individual liberation, bodhisattva, and tantric mantra); so, having recognized them, it is exceedingly important to rely on the antidote.
- **Counting the gateways.** Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk counts the attachment and the anger taught in this verse as the first and second of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct.
- **The view that supports the antidote.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that, because actions prompted by attachment or anger become afflicted, one must, with the antidote of grasping all phenomena as without an agent, remain steady like a tree, free of the transformations of attachment and anger.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Restlessness, Mockery, Pride, Arrogance, Exposing Faults, and Deviousness Are Counted Separately

This verse sets down a count of seven, from the third to the ninth of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, enumerating separately the distinct kinds of affliction: restlessness, mockery, pride (of seven kinds), arrogance (of five kinds), exposing faults, and deviousness.

###### 2. The Seven Kinds of Pride and Five Kinds of Arrogance Have a Fixed Count

On the fixed count of the seven kinds of pride and the five kinds of arrogance, Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk are shown to agree in their traditions of explanation.

###### 3. ⚑ Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Ngulchu Thogme Explain "Deviousness" Differently

The commentaries set down two differing explanations of the word "deviousness" (སྐྱོར་འབྱིན). Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains it as reopening a dispute and as asking again and again for want of contentment, while Ngulchu Thogme explains it as deceiving others by means of trickery and deceit; both explain it together with the preceding thought of exposing faults.

#### Divergences Among Commentators

- ⚑ **Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Ngulchu Thogme on the meaning of "deviousness" (སྐྱོར་འབྱིན):** Two different explanations of this word appear. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden takes it to mean reopening a dispute that has already been settled, or, through not knowing contentment, asking again and again at one and the same place of giving. Ngulchu Thogme instead explains it as deceiving others by means of trickery and deceit. Both explain the term together with the thought of exposing faults mentioned just before it.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In this verse of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, among the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, the things to be abandoned are shown: restlessness, mockery, the seven kinds of pride, the five kinds of arrogance, exposing faults, and deviousness.

**Key points.**

- **The categories counted here.** Within this verse, from the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, the categories of restlessness, mockery, the seven kinds of pride, the five kinds of arrogance, exposing faults, and deviousness are counted.
- **Pride and arrogance, in their kinds.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain in agreement the categories of the seven kinds of pride (the pride of thinking "I am," mere pride, excessive pride, pride beyond pride, the pride of thinking "a little," wrong pride, and manifest pride) and of the five kinds of arrogance (arrogance through family, form, youth, learning, and power).
