## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 7 to 9 from the third chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. We turn this body of ours into medicine for the sick, food for the hungry, and a treasury for the poor. Then together we reflect on that vast loving mind which removes the suffering of beings.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a passage of scripture that is easy to understand and encouraging. The three verses of that passage match today's three verses exactly, in order: illness, famine, and poverty.

> In an intermediate eon of illness
> he becomes the supreme healing medicine.
> By that beings are set free,
> and become free of illness and happy.
>
> In an intermediate eon of famine,
> turning into food and drink,
> having taken away hunger and thirst,
> he teaches the Dharma to living creatures.
>
> For beings who are poor
> he becomes an inexhaustible treasure.
> And whoever he gives a gift to,
> he sets them to generating bodhicitta.

So Vimalakirti taught.

This clearly shows how the illness, the hunger and thirst, and the poverty of beings are removed. It also shows how they are set in bodhicitta.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will take medicine or food to someone who is ill, and help them.

**Explanation:** *(Doing good)* The *Bodhicaryāvatāra* says: for beings who are sick, until their illness is healed, may I myself become the medicine and the nurse. If today we visit someone who is ill and help them, it takes away another's suffering. It also increases the good heart of love and compassion in us.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

**For as long as beings are sick,** for just that long, **until** each one's illness **has been healed,** may I **become their medicine, and a** skilled **doctor** **himself,** and, **by acting as that** sick person's **nurse,** bring them real help and benefit. **May it be so.**

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^3-7]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Among the sufferings of birth, ageing, sickness and death, the suffering of sickness is one of the very hardest to bear. That is why this verse singles out the suffering of illness and speaks of it on its own.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^3-7]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In particular, beings — the many kinds of creatures — become objects of compassion. Through the power of their bad conduct and non-virtue the gods are defeated and the asuras win, and from the breath of that a red cloud of sickness arises, so that beings are tormented and worn down by all kinds of illness: the eighteen classes of contagious disease and the rest. For as long as those who are in such distress are not freed from illness and healed, may I myself become the good medicine that pacifies their sickness. That is the aspiration. For example, long ago the Teacher took the birth of King Padma as a rohita fish and so pacified sickness; and there is the birth story of the Gentle Creature, and others like it. In general, every form of medicine there is has arisen from the blessing of the compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas. And may I be a doctor like the boy Water-Bringer and the rest, who show that medicine and give it out. Our own Teacher scraped away with a reed the filth that soiled the body of a sick monk, changed his bedding and so on, and he also laid it down that his followers must care for the sick. So one prays, "may I be their nurse" — and one must actually do it: not abandon the sick and turn away, but nurse them and tend to them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

For as long as beings who are sick are not healed of their illness, may I be their medicine, their doctor, and their nurse — such is the aspiration made here. And immediately after this, he says, comes the verse of dedication for those who are hungry and thirsty.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

In particular, at times such as the intermediate aeon of disease, beings tormented by all kinds of illness become objects of compassion. For as long as they are not freed from illness and healed, may I myself become the good medicine that pacifies their sickness, and the doctor who brings them benefit; and may I do for them whatever is needed — nursing them, tending them, and the rest. That is the aspiration.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

By the power of whatever virtue I have gathered: beings tormented and worn down by the pangs of all kinds of illness become objects of compassion — in general, and in particular at the time when the human lifespan is twenty years, when for seven months and seven days a violent epidemic that cannot be treated strikes beings and most of them die, which the Bhūmi section explains as the intermediate aeon of disease. For as long as the sick of such a time are not freed from illness and healed, may I myself become the great medicine that removes their sickness, and a skilled and good doctor who heals the sick; and may I benefit them by acting, with gentle conduct, as that sick person's nurse and attendant. So he prays: that one may become the medicine that pacifies illness, as in the birth story of the Gentle Creature, and the doctor who gives out medicine, like the king's daughter Jñānavatī.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

In the realms of the world, whatever beings are struck by illness — of wind, of bile, of phlegm, of their combinations and so on — for as long as the illnesses of their bodies are not healed and cured, may I become the medicine that heals those illnesses; and may I become the skilled doctor who prescribes the medicine that heals; and for those sick people who have no one to nurse them and are in such a frightening state, may I also be their nurse. This is the aspiration he makes.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:kunkhyen-tsona -->
##### The Story of the Gentle Creature

Long ago an intermediate aeon of great disease came upon Jambudvīpa. Many beings were struck by all kinds of illness — swellings and suppurating sores and the rest — and many thousands of doctors could not heal them. At that time the Blessed One Shakyamuni was Indra, lord of the gods, called Fair-Eyed. With his divine eyes and ears he saw and heard the suffering of those beings, and great compassion arose in him. In the middle of Jambudvīpa, near the city of Kuru, he took miraculous birth as a creature called the Gentle One and remained in the sky, proclaiming: "Whoever eats my flesh will be freed from illness; take as much of my flesh as you wish, without fear." Then all the sick went there and cut away his flesh and carried it off; yet his body was never used up, and however often they cut it, it grew back again — for by a declaration of truth he had blessed his body so that it would not be exhausted. As soon as they ate the flesh all the sick were freed from illness, and in the end the illness of every being in Jambudvīpa was removed. When those beings wished to make offerings to him, the Gentle Creature said: "I have no need of offerings of wealth or food. Give up the ten non-virtues, make aspiration prayers for enlightenment, and generate love for one another." And so those beings came to abide in the paths of virtuous action. By giving away one body completely, it is recorded, he brought countless beings to full ripening. This illustrates the aspiration in the words "may I be their nurse" — to become medicine oneself and so benefit all who are ill.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC13_KTB_bo.md#^3-7]] [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^3-7]]

<!-- story:wangchuk-rinchen -->
##### The Story of Princess Jñānavatī

Long ago the master called Yenlak Lodrö (also Yangdak Lodrö), the Dharma teacher of King Yeshe Tob, was seized by a black burning pox. The doctors gave him up, and he came to the point of death. Then the king's protectress, the goddess Yeshe Chok, appeared in a dream and prophesied that if he were anointed with the fresh blood of a living human being and with a paste made from living human flesh, he would be healed. No one else was willing; but the king's daughter Jñānavatī, who was fifteen years old, opened her own great vein and anointed him with her blood, cut her own flesh and made it into the paste, and brought it to the monk. He was freed from his illness, and by his teaching the Dharma he set many living beings in the fruit of irreversibility. The girl had no regret at having given her body, and by a declaration of truth her body became as it had been before. This is given as the example of nursing the sick and giving medicine without even sparing one's own body.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^3-7]]

<!-- story:kunpal -->
##### How the Teacher Himself Nursed a Sick Monk

Our own Teacher scraped away with a reed the filth that soiled the body of a sick monk, changed his bedding and so on — doing this himself, in person — and he also laid it down that others must care for the sick. This story is given to show that the words "may I be their nurse" are not merely an aspiration: one must not abandon the sick and turn away, but must actually nurse them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-7]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Until Beings Worn Down by Illness Are Healed, One Prays to Become Their Medicine, Their Doctor and Their Nurse

This is the main point of the verse. All the commentaries agree in the end that, for as long as the sick are not freed from illness and healed, one prays to become all three at once: the good medicine that pacifies illness, the doctor skilled in healing, and the one who nurses the sick.

###### 2. Every Form of Medicine There Is Comes From the Blessing of the Compassion of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that in general every form of medicine there is has arisen from the blessing of the compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

###### 3. This Aspiration Is Illustrated by Birth Stories of a Compassion That Goes as Far as Giving the Body — the Gentle Creature and Jñānavatī

The birth story in which all illness was pacified by eating the flesh of the Gentle Creature, and the birth story in which the king's daughter Jñānavatī gave the flesh and blood of her own body to remove the illness of a Dharma teacher, are both the furthest reach of this aspiration to become medicine and nurse for the sick.

###### 4. The Story of the Teacher Nursing a Sick Monk Himself Shows That This Aspiration Must Be Acted On, Not Merely Thought

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden cites the story of the Teacher scraping away with a reed the filth on a sick monk's body and changing his bedding, and explains from it that one must not abandon the sick and turn away, but must actually nurse them.

###### 5. "The Sick" Means Beings Struck by Illnesses of Wind, Bile, Phlegm and the Rest

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk identifies "the sick" as beings struck by illnesses of wind, of bile, of phlegm, of their combinations and so on.

###### 6. The Suffering of Illness Is the Hardest of the Four — Birth, Ageing, Sickness and Death — and So Is Singled Out Here

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that because the suffering of illness is the hardest to bear of birth, ageing, sickness and death, this verse singles it out and speaks of it on its own.

###### 7. The Severity of Illness Is Shown by the Intermediate Aeon of Violent Epidemic, Lasting Seven Months and Seven Days, When the Human Lifespan Is Twenty Years

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the account given in the Bhūmi section of the epidemic of the intermediate aeon at the time when the human lifespan is twenty years, and so shows how severe an object of compassion this is.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse makes the aspiration that, for as long as beings who are sick are not freed from illness, one may become all three at once: the medicine that pacifies illness, the doctor skilled in healing, and the one who nurses the sick. Many commentaries explain it in just this one way, with no disagreement.

**Key points.**

- Until beings are freed from illness, one prays to become their medicine, their doctor and their nurse.
- Every form of medicine there is comes from the blessing of the compassion of the buddhas and bodhisattvas.
- The birth stories of the Gentle Creature and of the king's daughter Jñānavatī show the furthest reach of a compassion that helps the sick without even sparing one's own body.
- The story of the Teacher himself nursing a sick monk shows that this aspiration is not merely a thought: it must actually be acted on.
- The suffering of illness is the hardest of the four — birth, ageing, sickness and death — and so it is singled out here.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

On those in the realms of the world who are tormented by the suffering of having nothing to eat or drink, may a ceaseless rain of food of a hundred tastes and of nectar-like drink fall, so that every harm of hunger and thirst is cleared away and they are set in the happiness of being completely satisfied.

When the intermediate aeon of famine falls, and there appear those of the hidden store, those who search with a skewer, and those of the white bones — at that time may I become excellent food and drink, and become the very thing that pacifies all hunger and thirst without exception. So he explains the aspiration.

Taking that as representative, he explains that one should pray in the same way for the other aeons too: in the aeon of disease, that one become medicine and the rest; and in the aeon of weapons, that by my power a rain of weapons be turned into a rain of flowers.

He says that nowadays, so that we ourselves and others may not be born in those intermediate aeons of disease, weapons and famine, we should offer in due order to the precious field of the Three Jewels — medicines of every kind, weapons of every kind, and food and drink to the Sangha — and make the aspiration that we and all other beings may not be born in those times.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

He explains that a rain of food and drink is made to fall on those who are hungry and thirsty, and that by all kinds of things to eat and to drink every harm of hunger and thirst can be cleared away.

Of the three intermediate aeons, he says, in the intermediate aeon of famine may I become the food and drink of those beings, and be able to pacify their hunger and thirst.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Raining down food and drink, the harm of hunger and thirst is to be cleared away. In the intermediate aeon of famine, may I become food and drink and be able to remove the hunger and thirst of those beings. So he explains it.

He also applies it in the same way to the intermediate aeon of weapons: one should say there, "may a rain of flowers fall."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

On beings threatened by the suffering of hunger and thirst, may a ceaseless rain of food and of nectar-like drink fall, so that every harm of hunger and thirst is cleared away and they are set in the happiness of being completely satisfied.

As is said in the *Abhidharmakośa*, through the power of non-virtue, when the human lifespan is ten years there comes an intermediate aeon of famine that lasts a long time — seven years and so on. At that time may I become excellent food and drink, and become the very thing that pacifies all hunger and thirst.

Taking that as representative, he says that one should also pray that in the intermediate aeon of weapons, by my power, weapons may be turned into a rain of flowers.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

On beings worn down by the suffering of hunger and thirst, may an unfailing stream of excellent food to eat and drink to drink — like the nectar of the realm of the gods — rain down, so that every harm of hunger, starvation and thirst is cleared away and they are made full and satisfied. So he explains the aspiration.

In particular, the Bhūmi section explains that at the time when the human lifespan is thirty years there comes an intermediate aeon of famine lasting seven years, seven months and seven days.

When it comes, he explains, there are those of the hidden store, who keep a single grain of seed in a *gab-tse* box and guard it like a precious jewel; and those of the white bones, who take the white, greaseless bones of the dead, boil them in water and drink it; and those who live by the skewer, who dig out single grains from a vessel and the like with the tip of a skewer, boil them in water and drink it. Having no food or drink, they grow hungry and worn down, until they fall to the ground and die without being able to rise again.

At such a time, may I become food and drink of every kind that beings could want, and be able to pacify all the suffering of hunger and thirst. So he explains the aspiration: to become like the Teacher in his birth story, who took up the wish-fulfilling jewel and made a rain of food and drink fall.

In the same way, when the human lifespan is ten years there comes an intermediate aeon of weapons: for seven days, whenever people see one another a violent wish to kill arises, and whatever they take in their hands becomes a sharp weapon. At that time, may the power of my merit be able to turn those weapons into a rain of flowers — so one should pray. He says that although this is not stated in the root words, it is implied by the earlier lines, and so it must be applied in this way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

For those in want through hunger and thirst — and this is said by way of dedication and aspiration — may I in the future become the very nature of the food that sustains beings' bodies and of the drink that is to be drunk; and may I rain down before them, so that all the harm, or the suffering, of hunger and thirst is cleared away.

At the end of time, he explains, at the close of a declining aeon, there come the aeon of disease, the aeon of famine, the aeon of weapons and the rest.

When the intermediate aeon of famine comes — that is, when famine runs its full course — may I myself also become, right before those beings, the food that is to be eaten and the drink that is to be drunk. So he explains the aspiration.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-8]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:kunkhyen-tsona -->
##### The Generosity of King Moonlight

Long ago, when a great terror of famine came upon beings, a bodhisattva named King Wangchen De of the land of Dritoha gave beings food and drink for many years. When the granaries were exhausted and there was no other means left, he made this aspiration: "If the Dharma of cause and effect is true and my intention is wholly pure, may I become a great fish and be the sustenance of beings." And he threw himself into the sea.

By the power of that wholly pure intention he became a rohita fish five hundred leagues in size. He said to the beings who were destitute, "Eat my flesh and be freed from the suffering of famine." By giving away his own flesh and body, it is explained, many beings were freed from the suffering of famine.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC13_KTB_bo.md#^3-8]]

##### The Story of the Elephant

Long ago, when the Teacher was born as an elephant, he saw in a desert plain many people whose strength was exhausted by hunger and weariness. He told them of the flesh of an elephant's corpse lying at the foot of a mountain, to serve as provisions for their road, and showed them the way to safety. Then he himself went quickly by another road, climbed to the top of the mountain, and made this aspiration: "By the merit of rescuing these worn-out people, may I become the protector and refuge of all beings." ##### The King Suvarṇavarṇa Story

Long ago, in the land of King Suvarṇavarṇa, no rain fell for twelve years, and by the eleventh year all the nourishment of the grain was gone. #### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Aspiration to Become Food and Drink Oneself in the Intermediate Aeon of Famine and So Pacify the Suffering of Hunger and Thirst

All the commentaries agree in the end that the heart of this prayer is to make a rain of food and drink fall before beings threatened by hunger and thirst in the time of the intermediate aeon of famine, and to become the very nature of food and drink oneself, so that every harm is cleared away.

###### 2. The Intermediate Aeon of Famine Is One of the Three That Come at the End of an Aeon, Together With the Aeons of Weapons and of Disease

Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both cite the *Abhidharmakośa*: through the increase of the paths of non-virtuous action, when the human lifespan shrinks to ten years, the aeon of weapons (seven days), the aeon of disease (seven days) and the aeon of famine (seven days, seven months and seven years) run their course one after another. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk likewise explains that the three aeons of disease, famine and weapons come at the close of a declining aeon.

###### 3. This Same Aspiration Must Be Applied Equally to Becoming Medicine in the Aeon of Disease and Turning Weapons into Flowers in the Aeon of Weapons

Kunzang Pelden and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both explain that this very aspiration, which rains down food and drink, is representative: the aspiration to become medicine in the aeon of disease, and to turn weapons into a rain of flowers in the aeon of weapons, must be applied in just the same way.

###### 4. How Wretched Beings Are Without Food and Drink in the Intermediate Aeon of Famine — Those of the Hidden Store, Those Who Live by the Skewer, Those of the White Bones

Kunzang Pelden sets out the three names. Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains them in detail: keeping a grain of seed in a *gab-tse* box and guarding it like a jewel; boiling bones in water and drinking it; digging out single grains with the tip of a skewer, boiling them in water and drinking it. And he says that such people fall to the ground and die without being able to rise again.

###### 5. The Practice for Now — Offering Medicine, Weapons, Food and Drink to the Three Jewels and Praying Not to Be Born in the Aeons of Disease, Weapons and Famine

Kunzang Pelden explains that, so that we ourselves and all other beings may not be born in those intermediate aeons of disease, weapons and famine, we should offer medicines, weapons, food and drink to the precious field of the Three Jewels and to the Sangha, and make the aspiration.

###### 6. This Aspiration Is Made by Way of Dedication and Prayer for Those in Want Through Hunger and Thirst — That in the Future One Become the Very Nature of Food and Drink

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that this very aspiration is made by way of dedication and prayer for those in want through hunger and thirst, and that in the future one becomes the very nature of the food and drink that sustain beings' bodies and rains down before them.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows the bodhisattva's aspiration to make a rain of food and drink fall before beings threatened by hunger and thirst in the time of the intermediate aeon of famine, and to become the very nature of food and drink oneself, so that every harm is pacified. Many commentaries explain that it is to be applied to the intermediate aeon of famine, which comes at the end of an aeon together with the aeons of weapons and of disease.

**Key points.**

- In the intermediate aeon of famine, one prays to become food and drink oneself and so pacify every suffering of hunger and thirst.
- The intermediate aeon of famine is one of the three intermediate aeons described in the *Abhidharmakośa* that come at the end of an aeon, along with the aeons of weapons and of disease.
- This same aspiration must be applied equally to becoming medicine in the aeon of disease and to turning weapons into a rain of flowers in the aeon of weapons.
- The harm of famine is described in detail — those of the hidden store, those who live by the skewer, those of the white bones — to show the measure of the suffering.
- As a practice for now, one should offer medicine, weapons, food and drink to the Three Jewels and pray not to be born in the aeons of disease, weapons and famine.
- The general sense of this aspiration is illustrated by three stories: King Wangchen De becoming a great fish and saving beings from famine; the elephant giving his own body to the people of the desert plain; and King Suvarṇavarṇa offering even his last food, so that a rain of food and clothing fell.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In this world, for whatever beings are destitute and poor — lacking food and drink, lacking clothing for their backs, lacking wealth in hand and the means of living — may I become a great inexhaustible treasure of whatever they want: food for those who want food, drink for those who want drink, and the rest. ##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

For beings destitute and poor for want of the necessities of life, may I become a treasure that is never used up however much it is enjoyed; may I become all kinds of necessities, whatever is wished for; and may they be present close at hand, right before them, with no effort needed to produce them. In the outline he shows that this verse belongs to the part that teaches the manner of giving — one of the three: training the mind in giving away body, possessions and roots of virtue; praying that one's virtue not be wasted; and praying to have the means of enjoyment.

For beings destitute and poor, may I become a treasure that is never used up however much it is used, and may I be present close at hand, right before those beings, as all kinds of necessities. This, he explains, is the meaning. He shows that this verse belongs to the general presentation of giving everything away, in the section on training the mind for the sake of others.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For beings who are destitute through not knowing contentment and poor through having no wealth, may I become a great treasure of enjoyments that is never used up, appearing as all kinds of necessities — food, clothing, wealth, medicine, dwellings, mounts and the rest — present close at hand right before each of them. <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For beings who have become destitute and poor for want of wealth, possessions and the other necessities, may I become a great treasure that is never used up however much it is enjoyed, and may I become all the many kinds of necessities that each one needs — food, clothing, dwellings and bedding, grain, medicine, ornaments, mounts and the rest — so that, with no effort at all needed to produce them, whatever is needed is present without effort close at hand before each one. This, he explains, is the meaning. He also says that dedication makes even a small virtue increase into a great one, and makes virtues that would otherwise be spent once their temporary fruit has ripened become inexhaustible; and this dedication is taught in brief as the skill in means of the bodhisattvas.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

For those beings who have become poor and destitute, may I become the very nature of an inexhaustible treasure of every kind of wealth and enjoyment, so that all the many kinds of necessities they need are present close at hand right before them, in whatever form each one wishes. This, he explains, is the meaning.
