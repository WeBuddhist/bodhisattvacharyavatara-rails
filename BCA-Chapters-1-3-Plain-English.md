# A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life
### Chapters One through Three — Plain English Translation (Grade 8)
*Translated from the Sanskrit of Śāntideva*

---

## Chapter One: The Benefits of the Mind of Enlightenment

With deep respect, I bow to the Blissful Ones — those who have found lasting happiness — along with their heirs and the Truth Body of their teaching. I bow to all who deserve reverence. Following the scriptures, I will briefly explain the path of entering the way of life of the Awakened Ones' heirs. ^1-1

There is nothing new being said here, and I have no skill in writing. So I am not thinking about benefiting others. I wrote this only to strengthen my own mind. ^1-2

Through these writings, my own love for goodness may grow for a little while. And if someone of equal good fortune reads this, perhaps it will benefit them too. ^1-3

This precious human life — a rare chance to do something truly meaningful — is very hard to get. If we don't use it for good right now, when will this chance come again? ^1-4

Just as lightning on a dark, cloudy night lights up the world for one brief moment, so too — by the power of the Buddhas — a mind drawn toward goodness sometimes appears for an instant in the world. ^1-5

Because of this, goodness is always weak. The power of harmful deeds, on the other hand, is huge and frightening. Nothing can overcome harmful deeds — except the Mind of Enlightenment. ^1-6

The greatest sages spent countless ages thinking carefully about this. They all saw that the Mind of Enlightenment is the one thing that truly helps. Through it, endless numbers of beings can easily reach the highest happiness. ^1-7

Anyone who wants to escape the many sufferings of the cycle of life, who wants to remove the pain of other beings, and who wants to enjoy great happiness — let that person never give up the Mind of Enlightenment. ^1-8

The moment the Mind of Enlightenment is born in someone, even a wretched person trapped in the prison of the cycle of life is immediately called a child of the Blissful Ones. Gods and humans alike will honor them. ^1-9

Like a rare and powerful elixir that transforms base metal into gold, this Mind of Enlightenment takes this very body — even though it is impure — and turns it into the priceless body of a Victor. So hold firmly to this thing called the Mind of Enlightenment! ^1-10

The wise guides of wandering beings have examined this with their vast wisdom and found it to be more valuable than any jewel. Those who want to leave behind the cycle of wandering — hold firmly to this precious Mind of Enlightenment! ^1-11

All other goodness is like a banana tree — it gives its fruit and then dies. But the tree of the Mind of Enlightenment keeps giving fruit forever and never runs out. ^1-12

Even if someone has done truly terrible, harmful deeds, they can be freed in an instant by holding on to this — just as people escape great danger by relying on a brave hero. Why don't those who understand this take refuge in it? ^1-13

Like the fire at the end of time, it can burn even the greatest wrongdoing in an instant. The wise Maitreya taught its endless benefits to the young seeker Sudhana. ^1-14

In short, the Mind of Enlightenment has two aspects: the wishing mind of enlightenment and the acting mind of enlightenment. ^1-15

The difference between wishing to go somewhere and actually going — a wise person should understand the same kind of difference exists between these two, in that order. ^1-16

The wishing mind of enlightenment brings great results even within the cycle of life. But it does not produce the same unbroken flow of goodness that the acting mind does. ^1-17

From the moment a person takes up this intention with a firm and unwavering mind — the intention to free every single being in the vast realm of existence — ^1-18

from that point on, even while sleeping or being careless, an endless stream of goodness flows, as vast as the sky. ^1-19

This was taught with clear reasoning by the Buddha himself, at the request of Subāhu, for the sake of beings with modest aspirations. ^1-20

Even the thought, "I want to remove the headache of just one being," is a kind intention — and it brings immeasurable goodness. ^1-21

What then of the wish to wipe out the limitless suffering of every single being, and to give each one limitless good qualities? ^1-22

Does any mother or father carry such a wish to help others? Do gods, sages, or even the greatest divine beings carry it? ^1-23

Those beings have never even dreamed of such a mind for their own sake — so how could it arise in them for the sake of others? ^1-24

This extraordinary jewel of the mind — the wish to help others — does not even arise in most beings for their own sake. How remarkable that it arises for the sake of others! ^1-25

This jewel of the mind is the seed of happiness for all beings and the cure for the world's suffering. How could anyone ever measure its goodness? ^1-26

Even just wishing to benefit others outweighs making offerings to the Buddhas. What then of actually working for the happiness of all beings without exception? ^1-27

People run straight toward suffering while wishing to escape it. In their confusion, they destroy their own happiness as if they were their own worst enemy. ^1-28

For those who are starved of happiness and weighed down by pain — imagine the one who satisfies them with every joy and cuts away all their suffering — ^1-29

and who also removes their ignorance. Where can you find a friend like that? Where can you find such kindness? Where can you find such goodness? ^1-30

Even someone who simply repays a kindness that was done for them is praised. So what is there to say about one who helps others without being asked at all? ^1-31

If someone feeds just a few people a single simple meal for half a day — even doing it with a bit of contempt — people call that a good act and honor it. ^1-32

But what about someone who gives endless beings, without ever stopping, the greatest happiness, fulfilling their every wish? What is there to say about such giving? ^1-33

If someone grows angry toward such a generous Hero of Enlightenment, the Buddha has said they will dwell in hellish suffering for as many ages as there were moments of that anger. ^1-34

But if someone feels genuine joy toward a Hero of Enlightenment, the reward grows even greater. Even when difficult things happen to these heroes, no wrongdoing builds up in them — only goodness naturally grows. ^1-35

I bow with deep respect to those in whom this precious Mind of Enlightenment has arisen. I take refuge in this source of happiness — even those who have harmed it find connection with joy. ^1-36

_Thus ends Chapter One: "The Benefits of the Mind of Enlightenment."_

## Chapter Two: Confessing Wrongdoing

To truly take hold of that precious Mind of Enlightenment, I now make offerings to the Blissful Ones, to the unspoiled Teaching, and to the Heroes of Enlightenment — those oceans of good qualities. ^2-1

All the flowers and fruits that exist, all kinds of medicines, all the jewels found in the world, and all the clear, beautiful waters — ^2-2

the jeweled mountains and the quiet, lovely forests, the vines adorned with flowers and shining with beauty, the trees weighed down with their fine fruit — ^2-3

the incense and perfumes found among gods and spirits, the wish-fulfilling trees and jeweled trees, the lakes adorned with lotuses, the swans singing their beautiful songs — ^2-4

unplanted crops and all other fine things worthy of offering, everything that extends to the farthest reaches of infinite space — all of it unclaimed by anyone — ^2-5

I take all of this up in my mind and offer it to the greatest sages and their heirs. Please accept this offering, you worthy ones, with your great compassion, caring for me. ^2-6

I have very little goodness and am greatly poor. I have almost nothing else to offer. So please, protectors who work for others' good, accept this through your own power, for my sake. ^2-7

I give myself completely to the Victors and to all their heirs. Please take me as your own, greatest beings. With deep respect, I enter your service as a servant. ^2-8

By being taken in by you, I will have no fear in the cycle of life and will do good for beings. I will leave behind my past wrongdoing and commit no further harmful deeds from now on. ^2-9

In bathing rooms with wonderful fragrance, with floors of shining crystal, with beautiful pillars of precious gems and canopies glittering with pearls — ^2-10

I fill many fine jeweled vases with pleasant, fragrant water and flowers. To the music of song and melody, I bathe the Blissful Ones and their heirs. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with unmatched cloths, clean and scented with the finest perfume. Then I offer them beautiful robes with lovely colors and sweet fragrance. ^2-12

With fine, soft, varied garments and the best ornaments, I adorn the great Heroes of Enlightenment and other noble ones. ^2-13

With the finest scents that fill the entire world, I anoint the bodies of all the greatest sages — bodies that shine like refined and polished gold. ^2-14

I honor the most honored sages with mandarava flowers, blue lotuses, jasmine, and all kinds of sweet-scented flowers, along with beautifully arranged garlands. ^2-15

I offer them dense clouds of the finest incense, its lovely fragrance drifting everywhere. I offer them many kinds of food and drink. ^2-16

I offer rows of golden lamps set upon golden lotuses. On floors sprinkled with perfume, I scatter beautiful flower petals. ^2-17

I offer boundless palaces hung with garlands of pearls and jewels, shining beautifully and lighting up every direction of the sky, to those whose very nature is compassion. ^2-18

I hold up beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles, their borders adorned with gems, lovely to look at — I hold these always before the greatest sages. ^2-19

Beyond this, may pleasing clouds of offerings arise, along with clouds of music and melody that bring joy to every being. ^2-20

May a continuous rain of jewels, flowers, and precious things fall on all the sacred objects of the Teaching, on reliquary mounds, and on images. ^2-21

Just as Mañjughoṣa and the other Heroes of Enlightenment made offerings to the Victors, in the same way I make offerings to the Blissful Ones — the protectors — together with their heirs. ^2-22

I praise the oceans of good qualities with vast oceans of songs of praise. May clouds of hymns always arise for them, never ending. ^2-23

With prostrations as numerous as the atoms in all the worlds, I bow to all the Buddhas of the past, present, and future, together with the Teaching and the finest communities. ^2-24

I bow to all sacred monuments and to everything that supports the Heroes of Enlightenment. I pay my respects to the teachers, to those who deserve honor, and to all who hold their commitments. ^2-25

Until I reach the heart of enlightenment, I go for refuge to the Buddhas. I go for refuge to the Teaching and to the community of Heroes of Enlightenment. ^2-26

With palms pressed together, I address the fully awakened ones throughout every direction, and the Heroes of Enlightenment with their great compassion. ^2-27

From beginningless time in the cycle of life — in this life and in past lives — I, acting like an animal, have done harmful things and caused others to do harmful things. ^2-28

And whatever I foolishly approved of, hurting only myself through confusion — all of that wrongdoing I now confess, burned by deep regret. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have done to the Three Jewels — the Buddha, the Teaching, and the community — or to my parents or teachers or others, through body, speech, or mind, driven by disturbing emotions — ^2-30

all the terrible wrongdoing that I, a guilty person filled with many faults, have done — I confess every bit of it to the guides. ^2-31

Before my harmful deeds are cleared away, I may die. Please protect me quickly — show me how to be free from this! ^2-32

Death, the great destroyer, cannot be trusted. It doesn't wait to check whether you have finished what you planned. Whether you are healthy or sick, it can strike at any moment without warning. ^2-33

I didn't understand that I would have to leave everything behind. For the sake of friends and enemies, I committed many kinds of harmful deeds. ^2-34

My enemies will be gone. My loved ones will be gone. I myself will be gone. Everything will be gone. ^2-35

Everything we experience is like a dream. Once it has passed and we look back at it, it is gone and cannot be seen again. ^2-36

Even within this one life, so many loved ones and enemies have already come and gone. But the harmful deeds I did because of them — those remain, right in front of me. ^2-37

I didn't realize that I was just passing through. Driven by confusion, attachment, and anger, I did many kinds of harmful deeds. ^2-38

Day and night, without a pause, this life is always running out. No new time is coming to add to it. How could death not come to someone like me? ^2-39

Even while lying in bed, surrounded by family, I alone will have to feel the pain of my life ending. No one can bear that in my place. ^2-40

When grabbed by the messengers of death, what good are relatives? What good are friends? Only the goodness I have built up can protect me then — and I haven't cultivated even that. ^2-41

In my carelessness, protector, I didn't see this danger coming. For the sake of this short life, I committed many harmful deeds. ^2-42

Even someone being led away to have a limb cut off grows terrified. Their mouth goes dry and their eyes go strange. Everything looks different. ^2-43

What then of someone seized by the terrifying messengers of death — overwhelmed by great fear and illness, utterly broken? ^2-44

With wide, frightened eyes, looking in all four directions, searching desperately — who can protect me well from this great fear? ^2-45

Seeing no protection in any direction, I fall into total despair. If there is no refuge anywhere, what will I do in that moment of great danger? ^2-46

So today, right now, I go for refuge to the powerful guides of the world — the Victors who work to protect all beings and who clear away every fear. ^2-47

I also go for true refuge to the Teaching that they fully realized, which removes the fears of the cycle of life, and likewise to the community of Heroes of Enlightenment. ^2-48

Filled with fear, I offer myself to Samantabhadra. I offer myself again to Mañjughoṣa. ^2-49

To Avalokita — the protector who acts with unfailing compassion — I cry out in pain and distress: please protect me, a person full of wrongdoing! ^2-50

To noble Ākāśagarbha, to Kṣitigarbha, and to all the greatly compassionate ones — I cry out from the heart, looking for refuge. ^2-51

To Vajrapāṇi — the sight of whom sends the messengers of death and other hostile forces fleeing in all directions — I go for refuge. ^2-52

I have already gone beyond your words — but now, seeing great danger before me, I come to you for refuge in fear. Please remove this danger quickly! ^2-53

Even when frightened by an ordinary illness, you still have to follow the doctor's advice. What then when you are constantly struck by hundreds of disturbing conditions? ^2-54

Even just one of these alone can destroy every person on earth — and no cure for it can be found anywhere in any direction. ^2-55

And yet I ignore the advice of the all-knowing doctor who can remove every thorn of pain. What utter foolishness on my part! ^2-56

You have to be very careful even at a small, ordinary cliff edge. What about a cliff that drops for thousands of miles and goes on for ages without end? ^2-57

Sitting comfortably with the thought "I won't die today" makes no sense. The moment will absolutely come when I will no longer exist. ^2-58

Who has given me safety from this? How will I escape? I will definitely cease to exist one day. How can my mind be at ease? ^2-59

From past experiences that have come and gone, what remains? And yet, clinging to those things, I broke the words of my teachers. ^2-60

I will have to leave this living world, leave relatives and close friends, and go somewhere completely alone. When that happens, what good are loved ones and enemies? ^2-61

So this alone is what I should think about, day and night: "Wrongdoing leads to suffering without fail — how do I escape from it?" ^2-62

Whatever harmful deeds I, a foolish and ignorant person, have done — whether harmful by their very nature or harmful according to the rules — ^2-63

all of that I confess while standing before the protectors, pressing my palms together, bowing again and again, frightened by suffering. ^2-64

May the guides accept my wrongdoing as wrongdoing. This was not good, protectors — I will never do it again. ^2-65

_Thus ends Chapter Two: "Confessing Wrongdoing."_

## Chapter Three: Taking Up the Mind of Enlightenment

I rejoice with great joy in all the goodness done by every being — goodness that brings relief from the pain of lower states. May suffering beings find happiness! ^3-1

*[Note: Verse 3-2 is absent from this edition of the Sanskrit text.]*

I rejoice in beings being freed from the suffering of the cycle of life. I rejoice in those who protect others becoming Heroes of Enlightenment and fully awakened Buddhas. ^3-3

I rejoice in the vast awakening that brings happiness to every being, and in the helpful acts of the teachers that benefit all beings. ^3-4

With palms pressed together, I ask the fully awakened ones in every direction: please light the lamp of the Teaching for those who are falling into suffering through confusion. ^3-5

With palms pressed together, I ask the Victors who wish to pass into final peace: please stay for endless ages — don't let this world be left in darkness. ^3-6

Through all this goodness I have gathered, may I be a source of relief from all suffering for every being. ^3-7

May I be medicine for the sick, and also a doctor, and the doctor's assistant as well — until illness never comes back. ^3-8

May I put an end to the pain of hunger and thirst by raining down food and drink. During ages of famine, may I myself become food and drink for others. ^3-9

For poor and needy beings, may I be an endless treasure. May I appear before them in every kind of helpful form. ^3-10

Without any expectations, I give up my body, my possessions, and all the goodness of past, present, and future — all for the sake of helping every being. ^3-11

Giving everything away leads to liberation, and my mind seeks liberation. Since I must give up everything in the end, it is better to give it to other beings. ^3-12

I have already given this body of mine over to serve all living beings. Let them beat it, insult it, or throw dirt on it — I don't need to worry about it. ^3-13

Let them play with my body, laugh at it, or have fun with it. I have already given it to them. Why should I worry? ^3-14

Let them make me do whatever makes them happy. May no one ever come to harm because of me. ^3-15

Whether anyone thinks of me with anger or with kindness, may that very thought always become a cause for all their wishes to be fulfilled. ^3-16

Those who speak badly of me, who harm me, who mock me, or who wrong me in other ways — may all of them have a share in enlightenment. ^3-17

May I be a protector to those who have no one to protect them. May I be a guide to travelers. May I be a boat and a bridge to those who wish to cross over. ^3-18

For those who need a lamp, may I be a lamp. For those who need a bed, may I be a bed. For those who need a servant, may I be a servant — for all living beings. ^3-19

May I be a wish-granting jewel, a jar of good fortune, a powerful teaching, a great medicine, a wish-fulfilling tree, and a cow of plenty for all living beings. ^3-20

Just as the earth and the other elements serve the countless beings that fill all of space in every way they need — ^3-21

may I likewise be of service, in every way, to the vast realm of beings that reaches as far as the sky, until every single one of them has reached peace. ^3-22

Just as the Blissful Ones of the past took up the Mind of Enlightenment and then trained step by step in the practices of Heroes of Enlightenment — ^3-23

in the same way, I too will bring forth the Mind of Enlightenment for the good of the world, and in the same way I will train in those practices one step at a time. ^3-24

Having taken up the Mind of Enlightenment with clarity and joy, a wise person should further uplift their mind by thinking joyfully about this: ^3-25

Today my birth has become fruitful. This human life has been well obtained. Today I have been born into the family of the Awakened One — I am now a child of the Buddha. ^3-26

Now I must act in ways that are fitting for this family. I must not bring any stain upon this pure family. ^3-27

Just as a blind person might somehow find a jewel among a pile of trash, so too — somehow — this Mind of Enlightenment has arisen in me. ^3-28

This is the elixir that defeats death for the whole world. It is an endless treasure that removes the world's poverty. ^3-29

It is the supreme medicine that heals the world's sicknesses. It is the resting tree for beings worn out from wandering the long road of the cycle of life. ^3-30

It is the bridge that all travelers use to cross over from suffering into better states. It is the rising moon that soothes the world's confusion and brings cool relief. ^3-31

It is the great sun that drives away the darkness of ignorance. It is the fresh butter churned from the milk of the true Teaching. ^3-32

For travelers on the road of existence who are starving for happiness, this is a grand feast — ready and waiting to satisfy every being who arrives. ^3-33

Today I invite the whole world to enjoy the happiness of enlightenment. Until then, may gods, spirits, and all beings rejoice before those who protect others. ^3-34

_Thus ends Chapter Three: "Taking Up the Mind of Enlightenment."_
