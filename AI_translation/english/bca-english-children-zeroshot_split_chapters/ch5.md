## 5. Chapter Five: Watching Your Mind Carefully ^5-0


If you want to keep your training well,
you must watch your mind very carefully.
If you do not watch this mind of yours,
you cannot keep your training at all. ^5-1


A wild, untamed elephant, running loose,
does not cause as much harm in this world
as a wild, untamed mind
that has been let loose without any control. ^5-2


But if you tie the elephant of your mind
tightly with the rope of mindfulness,
every danger disappears,
and every good thing comes within reach. ^5-3


Tigers, lions, elephants, and bears,
snakes, and every kind of enemy,
the guardians who punish beings in hell,
evil spirits, and demons too — ^5-4


all of these are tamed
simply by taming this one mind of yours.
And all of them are controlled
simply by controlling this one mind. ^5-5


The truthful Buddha himself taught
that every fear
and every measureless suffering
comes from the mind alone. ^5-6


Who actually made the weapons
used to harm beings in hell?
Who made the burning iron ground?
Where did all those fires come from? ^5-7


The Buddha, the great sage, taught
that all of these come from
a mind that has done wrong.
So in all three realms of this world,
there is nothing more frightening than the mind. ^5-8


If giving generously simply means
making poverty disappear from the world,
then since there are still poor beings today,
how did the earlier Buddhas ever complete this practice of generosity? ^5-9


Generosity means having the wish
to give away everything you own, along with its results, to every being.
That is why it is said to be a state of mind —
generosity is really the mind itself. ^5-10


Even if you cannot stop others from killing fish
and other creatures for food,
if you develop the sincere wish to give up harming them yourself,
that alone is called the practice of moral discipline. ^5-11


Beings who cause harm are as endless as the sky;
there is no way to defeat every one of them.
But if I defeat just this one thing — the anger in my own mind —
it is the same as defeating every one of those enemies. ^5-12


Where could enough leather ever be found
to cover the whole surface of the earth?
But with leather just the size of the soles of your shoes,
it is as if the whole earth were covered. ^5-13


In the same way, I cannot possibly go around
controlling every single thing outside myself.
But if I simply control this mind of mine,
what need is there to control anything else? ^5-14


A mind made pure and clear brings a wonderful result,
like becoming a Brahmā, in the ancient teachings.
But an action of body or speech, without that same mind behind it,
does not bring a result as strong, because the effort itself is weaker. ^5-15


Even reciting prayers and practicing hardship
for a very long time
mean nothing, the wise say,
if your mind was wandering elsewhere the whole time. ^5-16


If someone does not understand this secret of the mind,
which is the very heart of the whole teaching,
even though they wish for happiness and want to end suffering,
they will wander about pointlessly, achieving nothing. ^5-17


That is why I must hold onto
this mind of mine, and guard it well.
Without the practice of guarding the mind,
what use is any other kind of discipline? ^5-18


Just as someone standing among an unruly crowd
takes special care to protect a wound,
so too, while living among difficult and unkind people,
I should always protect this wound-like, tender mind of mine. ^5-19


If I am careful about a small wound
just because a little pain scares me,
then why would I not protect this wound-like mind
from being crushed by the mountain-sized fears of terrible rebirths? ^5-20


If I live and act carefully like this,
then whether I am among difficult people
or even living among great temptation,
my firm commitment will never weaken. ^5-21


I would rather lose everything —
my wealth, my reputation, my body, even my very life,
and lose any other kind of goodness too —
than ever let this precious mind of bodhicitta weaken, even for a moment. ^5-22


To everyone who truly wants to guard their mind,
I say, with my hands pressed together:
please, with all your effort,
guard your mindfulness and your careful awareness always. ^5-23


People troubled by sickness
have no strength left for any task.
In the same way, a mind troubled by confusion
has no strength for anything worthwhile either. ^5-24


Whatever someone without careful awareness
has heard, thought about, or meditated on
simply leaks away, like water from a cracked jar,
and never stays fixed in their memory. ^5-25


Even someone who has learned much, has faith,
and works very hard,
can still become stained by wrongdoing
if they lack this careful awareness. ^5-26


A thief called "lack of awareness"
follows right behind the loss of mindfulness.
Even goodness that has been carefully gathered
gets stolen away by it, just like a thief, leaving that person heading toward a hard rebirth. ^5-27


This gang of thieves, the disturbing emotions,
is always watching for the moment I let my guard down.
The instant they find their chance,
they steal my goodness and destroy my chance at a happy rebirth. ^5-28


That is why I must never let mindfulness
wander away from the door of my mind, not even for a moment.
And if it does slip away,
I must remember the dangers of a bad rebirth, and bring it right back. ^5-29


Mindfulness grows easily and naturally
for those who live close to a good teacher,
who are guided carefully by a wise guide,
and who act with respect out of a healthy sense of caution. ^5-30


The Buddhas and bodhisattvas
see everything, everywhere, without any obstacle.
I should always remember
that I am living right in front of all of them. ^5-31


Thinking this way, I should feel
a healthy sense of shame, respect, and caution, always.
And because of this,
thoughts of the Buddha will arise in me again and again. ^5-32


When mindfulness stands guard
at the door of my mind, watching carefully,
then careful awareness naturally arrives too,
and even what has slipped away comes back to me. ^5-33


First, I must understand clearly
that this ordinary, untrained mind is full of faults.
Then, like fixing something firmly in place,
I must keep my mind steady and unmoving. ^5-34


I will never let my eyes
wander around pointlessly.
Instead, I will keep them
lowered, and look with clear purpose, always. ^5-35


But to rest my eyes sometimes,
it is fine to glance around a bit.
And if someone happens to come into view,
I should look at them and say kindly, "Welcome." ^5-36


To check for dangers on the road and elsewhere,
I should look in every direction now and then.
When resting, I should look behind me first,
before turning to go on. ^5-37


After checking both ahead and behind,
I should go forward or turn back accordingly.
In every situation like this,
I should understand what is needed and act accordingly. ^5-38


After deciding, "My body should stay like this,
doing this particular task,"
I should check now and then
to see how my body is actually positioned. ^5-39


Just as one carefully watches a wild elephant
tied to a great post to keep it from wandering,
in the same way, I should watch, with full effort,
this wild elephant of my mind, tied to the task of thinking about the true teachings, so it never slips away. ^5-40


I should check, again and again,
whether my mind stays focused on its practice
without wandering away, even for a single moment,
asking myself, "Where is my mind right now?" ^5-41


If, during moments of real fear or celebration,
I truly cannot keep this constant watchfulness, it is fine to relax it a little.
It is said that during times of generosity,
it is also fine to set the rules of discipline aside a little. ^5-42


Whatever task I have thought through and decided to begin,
I should not let my mind wander off to something else.
I should focus my whole intention on that one task
and complete it first, before moving on. ^5-43


If I do this,
everything gets done well; otherwise, neither task is done properly.
And this careless lack of awareness,
if I act this way, will not keep growing stronger. ^5-44


Whenever there is idle gossip of all kinds,
or many kinds of exciting entertainment happening all around,
I should let go of any craving
to get caught up in them. ^5-45


If I find myself pointlessly digging in the dirt,
cutting grass, or drawing lines in the ground for no reason,
I should remember the Buddha's teaching
and, feeling a healthy caution, stop right away. ^5-46


Whenever I want to move,
or whenever I want to speak,
I should first check my own mind,
and then act steadily and wisely. ^5-47


Whenever I notice
that my mind is becoming attached or wanting to get angry,
I should not act, and I should not speak.
I should stay still and quiet, like a piece of wood. ^5-48


Whenever my mind becomes restless and mocking,
or filled with pride and arrogance,
or wants to point out others' faults,
or wants to trick or deceive someone, ^5-49


whenever I want people to praise me,
or want to criticize others,
or feel like being harsh and then later regretting it —
at those times too, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-50


Whenever I crave wealth, respect, or fame,
or want followers and attention just for myself,
or want others to serve me,
at that time too, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-51


Whenever my mind wants to ignore the needs of others
and only look after my own interests,
or wants to speak carelessly,
at that time too, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-52


Whenever impatience, laziness, fear,
rudeness, or thoughtless chatter arise,
or a stubborn attachment to my own opinion,
at that time too, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-53


So, having noticed my mind filled with disturbing emotions
or pointlessly restless like this,
I, like a brave warrior,
should firmly apply the right remedy against it. ^5-54


I should be fully determined, full of genuine faith,
steady, respectful, and considerate,
have a healthy sense of shame and caution,
be calm, and work hard to bring joy to others. ^5-55


I should not become discouraged
by the conflicting wishes of ordinary, immature people.
Instead, understanding that their disturbing emotions
have simply arisen in them, I should feel compassion for them. ^5-56


For things that are not truly wrong in themselves,
I should act freely, for my own sake and for others,
without clinging to a rigid sense of "me,"
as if I were like a magical illusion — I should hold my mind this way, always. ^5-57


After a very long time, I have finally found
this rare and precious human life.
Remembering this again and again,
I should keep my mind as steady and unmovable as a great mountain. ^5-58


Even when vultures fight over a piece of flesh,
pulling it this way and that,
if you, my mind, do not feel upset about that,
then why do you feel so protective and anxious now? ^5-59


Mind, why do you guard this body so carefully,
as if it truly belonged to you?
Since you and this body are actually separate things,
what real use is this body to you? ^5-60


Foolish mind, why do you not instead hold onto
something pure, like a wooden figure?
Why bother protecting this impure, rotting machine
made of unclean parts? ^5-61


First, with your own understanding,
separate this body's layer of skin from what lies beneath.
Then, with the sword of wisdom,
separate the flesh from the web of bones. ^5-62


Then take apart the bones themselves too,
and look all the way down to the very marrow inside,
and ask yourself honestly:
"What real essence is there to be found here?" ^5-63


Even after searching this carefully,
if you cannot find any real essence there,
why, mind, do you still cling to this body
so tightly, as if it truly mattered? ^5-64


You would never eat something unclean,
and you would never want to drink its blood,
and you would never want to suck at its intestines —
so what real use is this body to you? ^5-65


It would make more sense to protect this body
for the sake of feeding jackals and vultures.
For an ordinary human being,
this body is really only useful as a tool. ^5-66


And even though you protect it so carefully,
when the merciless Lord of Death
seizes it and gives it away to the birds and dogs,
what will you be able to do then? ^5-67


You would not keep a servant around
if they were useless and you gave them nothing, not even clothing —
so why do you feed and care for this body so much,
when in the end it just leaves and goes elsewhere anyway? ^5-68


So having paid this body its fair wage,
I should now let it work for a truly worthwhile purpose.
I should not give it everything
just because it happens to want something, if that something brings no real benefit. ^5-69


I should think of my body only as a kind of boat,
a support for coming and going,
and transform it into a wish-fulfilling vessel,
used entirely for the benefit of other beings. ^5-70


Since I have this freedom, I should always
keep a gentle, smiling face,
letting go of frowns and scowls,
and be a warm, sincere friend to everyone. ^5-71


I should not carelessly set down chairs and other things
with a loud, thoughtless bang.
I should not throw doors open roughly either.
I should always take quiet pleasure in gentleness. ^5-72


A heron, a cat, or a thief
moves quietly, silently, carefully,
in order to achieve exactly what they intend.
A wise person should act with that same quiet care, always. ^5-73


I should respectfully and humbly accept,
as if placing it on my own head,
the skillful advice of someone who guides others well
and who helps without even being asked, and always try to learn from everyone. ^5-74


Whenever anyone speaks well,
I should say, "Well said."
And when I see someone doing something good,
I should praise them and help their joy grow. ^5-75


When someone is not present, I should speak of their good qualities.
And when someone else praises those qualities, I should agree warmly.
But when someone praises my own good qualities,
I should recognize that they truly do know good qualities when they see them. ^5-76


Every effort people make is really for the sake of joy,
and joy like that is rare, even if you tried to buy it.
So I should take real, happy delight
in the good qualities that others have achieved through their own effort. ^5-77


There is no real loss to me in feeling this way,
and it brings great happiness in future too.
Being resentful, on the other hand, only brings unhappiness and suffering now,
and brings even greater suffering later. ^5-78


I should speak in a way that flows warmly and connects with others,
with clear, meaningful, and pleasant words,
free from both attachment and anger,
speaking gently and appropriately. ^5-79


Even simply looking at other beings with my eyes,
I should look at them honestly and with kindness,
thinking, "It is because of these very beings
that I will one day become a Buddha." ^5-80


Doing good deeds all the time, whether inspired by
strong sincere devotion or by a deliberate remedy against faults,
brings great goodness, especially when it involves
good qualities, helping others, or caring for those who are suffering. ^5-81


I should always act wisely,
with real energy and enthusiasm.
And in whatever I do,
I should not simply wait around, depending on others to start. ^5-82


I should practice generosity and the other perfections,
each one more important than the one before it.
I should never give up something greater for the sake of something smaller.
The main thing to always keep in mind is the benefit of others. ^5-83


Understanding this clearly, I should always
work hard, with real effort, for the sake of others.
Because of great compassion and far-reaching wisdom,
even the ordinary rules can be set aside a little for such a person, when truly needed. ^5-84


I should share my food with those who have fallen into wrongdoing,
those without protection, and those living a life of strict discipline,
keeping just enough for myself,
and give away everything except my three robes. ^5-85


I should never harm this body, which practices
the true teachings, for the sake of something small and unimportant.
If I act this way,
the wishes of every being can quickly be fulfilled. ^5-86


I should not give away this body of mine
unless my compassion is truly pure and sincere.
Instead, in this life and in others too,
I should use it as a cause for achieving something truly great. ^5-87


I should not teach the true teachings to someone who is not respectful,
such as someone with their head wrapped like they are sick,
or someone carrying a parasol, a stick, or a weapon,
or someone with their head covered. ^5-88


I should not teach deep, profound teachings to those not ready for them,
and should not teach lesser paths to those who need the vast path.
And I should not teach women without a man present.
I should teach both simple and profound teachings equally, with respect. ^5-89


I should not offer a lesser teaching
to someone who is truly ready for a vast one.
I should not lead someone away from their ordinary practice,
and should not mislead anyone with mere sūtras or mantras out of context. ^5-90


I should cover up when spitting
or discarding a used tooth-cleaning stick.
And it is considered improper
to relieve oneself in water or on land that others use or enjoy. ^5-91


I should not eat with my mouth stuffed full,
noisily, or with my mouth wide open.
I should not sit with my legs stretched out carelessly,
and I should not rub my hands together needlessly. ^5-92


I should not travel, rest, sit, or lie down
alone together with another person's spouse.
Whatever the world finds inappropriate,
I should notice, ask about, and avoid. ^5-93


I should not point with just one finger
to show directions.
Instead, I should always use
my whole right hand, respectfully, to show the way. ^5-94


I should not wave my arms about wildly.
A small gesture, along with a quiet word,
is usually enough to get someone's attention.
Otherwise, I risk seeming undisciplined. ^5-95


I should lie down to sleep in the position
the Buddha himself used when he passed into peace,
facing the direction I intend to go,
and firmly resolve, right from the start, to wake up quickly and with clear awareness. ^5-96


The practices of a bodhisattva
are said to be truly limitless.
But the practice of training the mind
should definitely be kept up for as long as needed. ^5-97


Three times during the day
and three times during the night,
I should recite the "Confession in Three Parts,"
relying on the Buddhas and on bodhicitta, to help calm any remaining faults. ^5-98


Whatever I am doing, for my own sake or for the sake of others,
in any situation at all,
I should apply real effort to train
in whatever training applies to that particular situation. ^5-99


There is truly nothing
that a child of the Buddha should not train in.
For someone who lives this way, wisely,
there is nothing that fails to become a source of goodness. ^5-100


Whether directly or indirectly,
I should do nothing except what benefits beings.
And purely for the sake of beings,
I should dedicate everything toward full awakening. ^5-101


I should never, even for the sake of my own life,
give up a true spiritual friend
who is skilled in the meaning of the great vehicle,
and who upholds the highest bodhisattva discipline. ^5-102


I should train myself according to how, in the Life of Śrī Sambhava,
one is taught to properly rely on a teacher.
This teaching, and other instructions given by the Buddha,
can be learned by reading the sūtras. ^5-103


The trainings can be found within the sūtras themselves,
so I should read the sūtras.
The Ākāśagarbha Sūtra in particular
should be looked at first. ^5-104


Because the Compendium of Trainings
explains, in great detail, what should always be practiced,
I should also look at it
again and again, without fail. ^5-105


Or, for now, I could look
at the shorter Compendium of Sūtras,
and I should also make the effort to study
the two works composed by the noble Nāgārjuna. ^5-106


Whatever is not forbidden in these texts,
I should practice.
And in order to protect the minds of ordinary people around me,
after seeing what the training truly requires, I should practice it properly. ^5-107


Checking, again and again,
the state of my body and my mind —
that, in short,
is the very definition of careful, watchful awareness. ^5-108


All of this should be put into practice with the body.
What can mere words alone accomplish?
Does simply reading a medical text
actually help a sick person get well? ^5-109


This is the end of the fifth chapter, called "Watching Your Mind Carefully," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^5-a
