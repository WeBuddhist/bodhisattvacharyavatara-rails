# Day 12 — A gift that never runs out, and one that lasts half a day

## Opening

Verses 32 and 33 measure two kinds of giving against each other. The ordinary donor appears in verse 32: few recipients, plain food, given with disrespect, lasting half a day. Verse 33 pivots to the bodhisattvas. The contrast turns on a single word in the Tibetan, rtag tu, meaning "always," and what the commentators say it actually means.

## From the Tradition

You give someone a ride. You help a colleague meet a deadline. You lend money once. These acts have an end point. The benefit runs its course and the ledger resets. Ngulchu Thokme Zangpo, the author of the Thirty-Seven Practices of a Bodhisattva, pauses at the word "always" in verse 33 and unpacks it. Classical commentators, he notes, read it to mean "never exhausted as long as space and wandering beings remain." He is not using this as a figure of speech. He is describing a structural difference between ordinary giving and the bodhisattva's giving. The ordinary benefactor gives more of the same thing the world already has. The bodhisattva gives access to a capacity for liberation that does not deplete. One kind of generosity runs down; the other does not.

## Today's Practice Challenge

After you do something kind for someone today, pause and ask yourself honestly: is this an act with an end point, or do you actually care what happens to this person a year from now? You do not need to care about everyone without limit. Ngulchu Thokme Zangpo is making a narrower point: notice the difference between an act that closes and care that stays open. See if that distinction changes anything in how you approach the next interaction.
