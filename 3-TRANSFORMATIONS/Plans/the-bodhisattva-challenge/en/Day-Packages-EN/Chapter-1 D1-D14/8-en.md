## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:notification -->
### Notification

**Title:** Wish one good thing for someone struggling  
**Detail:** Even the smallest wish to help carries great merit. Today, make one clear wish for someone.

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's verses start with a worry many people feel: the path looks impossibly large, so why even begin? Shantideva answers with a small example. Even wishing to cure one person's headache is worth more than you would think. And if a wish that small matters, then the wish to free every being from all pain is beyond measuring.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These three verses use clear reasoning to establish the boundless merit of the awakening mind that the Buddha himself spoke of. There is a story that shows this. Once, a being was suffering in hell, with a burning iron wheel spinning on his head. In that moment he gave rise to a single kind thought: "May all the head-pain and suffering of other beings ripen upon me instead." Just from that one thought, he was reborn in the realm of the gods.

If there is boundless merit even in wishing to remove the mere headache of a few beings, then think how much greater the merit must be in the awakening mind — the mind that wishes to remove every last bit of suffering from each and every being, and to set every one of them in the countless good qualities of buddhahood. Reasoning makes this completely clear.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice**: Today, I will spend five minutes sincerely wishing that all living beings be free from suffering.

**Explanation**: Today's verse explains that simply having the kind thought to cure a minor pain, like a headache, creates immeasurable good merit. By spending just five minutes genuinely wishing to take away the suffering of others, you are practising true compassion.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### kunpal — Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

That the engaging vow, once obtained and unimpaired, carries such benefit — this is taught with four reasonings, because whether one considers the number of beings it is aimed at, the varieties of suffering to be removed, the qualities of buddhahood to be attained, or the length of time in eons, [the merit] is immeasurable. So, in the Sūtra Requested by the Bodhisattva Subāhu, the Tathāgata himself taught it — not merely as a turn of phrase, but so that beings devoted to the lesser path of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas would truly see and know the certain qualities and benefits of bodhicitta.

**Brief introduction.** This verse sets out the scriptural proof that much merit arises from the engaging mind; with its reasoning, [the point] was taught by the Tathāgata himself in the Sūtra Requested by Subāhu.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### kunpal — Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Second, establishing it by reasoning has two parts: establishing the benefits of the aspiring mind by reasoning, and establishing the benefits of the engaging mind by reasoning. First: although the presentation of its cause and effect is an extremely hidden matter — the sphere of an omniscient buddha alone and not of others, so that, strictly, it should be adopted with confidence in the pure word of the Victor rather than established by reasoning — nevertheless, since the benefits of bodhicitta are exceedingly vast and are praised again and again by the Victor, they can also be established by reasoning if one sets out to do so; and so he establishes them by reasoning.

Long ago, when the daughter of a merchant-chief's friend was setting out to sea to fetch jewels, her mother caught her by the hem of her garment and wept. Saying, "You make a bad omen for my sea voyage by weeping," the daughter struck her mother's head with her foot and left. At sea her ship wrecked; clinging to a plank she reached an island, and passing in stages through the City of Joy and the rest, in the end an iron wheel spun on her head and she felt unbearable suffering. Through that, compassion arose in her, and she thought: "May the suffering of all those who experience the fruit of striking their mother's head with a foot ripen upon me, so that they need not undergo it." The moment she thought this, she was freed from suffering and dwelt for a while in bliss; when she died she was reborn among the gods.

Thus, with a mind of compassion, even if one merely thinks, "I will cure by medicine and the like the mere headache of a few beings," and does not actually cure it by such means, one is still endowed with a mind that wishes to benefit them; and thereby one comes to possess immeasurable merit.

*[Grouping note: verses 1-21 and 1-22 are explained together here; the wording covers both. The merchant-chief's friend's daughter's story is not told in full here, only mentioned above.]*

On this occasion the commentaries [tell of] the one called the merchant-chief's friend's daughter, who, seeing a limited hell-being undergo the suffering of a blazing iron wheel spinning on his head, thought, "May his suffering ripen upon me," and, dying, was reborn among the gods; joining example to meaning, they explain that if even such a small-scope focus yields immeasurable merit, then, as above, [the merit of] wishing that immeasurable beings be free of all suffering and endowed with a buddha's happiness — a far vaster focus — need not be spoken of.

#### Stories and Illustrations

**The merchant-chief's friend's daughter — grouping note.**

All commentaries rely on this story as the chief illustration for verse 1-21. The fullest versions come from Kunzang Pelden (KKP) and Khenpo Kunga (NKW); Gyaltsab (GDR), Ngulchu Thogme (NTS), and Minyak Kunzang Sonam (MKS) cite it only in brief; Sabzang (SMPLG) and Khenpo Kunga (NKW) tell it in detail. According to Khenpo Kunga, it is also known as a birth-story of the Teacher found in a sūtra.

**The sequence of the story.**
1. When the merchant-chief's friend's daughter was forbidden by her mother to go to sea, she struck her mother's head with her foot.
2. She went to sea; the ship wrecked, and clinging to a plank she reached an island.
3. Welcomed by a goddess, she passed in stages through the City of Joy and the rest (the goddess, and the gold of the Karṣāpaṇa, increasing step by step), and finally arrived at a limited hell.
4. There she saw an iron wheel of blazing fire spinning on a being's head, and understood it to be the fruit of her own striking her mother's head with her foot.
5. Compassion arose, and she prayed: "May I alone experience the suffering of these beings who struck their mothers' heads with their feet, and may they be freed."
6. The moment the prayer arose, [without directly undergoing the fruit of the deed] she was freed from suffering, and, dying, was reborn among the Thirty-Three gods.

**The point of the story.** It shows that even a small-scope aspiring thought ("I will merely cure a headache") — once the wish to benefit becomes manifest — gives rise to immeasurable merit. The greater reasoning continues in verse 1-22.

##### BCACXX_WR_bo — The Merchant-Chief's Daughter's Story

The story given to illustrate this verse's phrase "the mere headache of beings." The Teacher told it while teaching the monks about honoring one's parents. After a merchant-chief's friend's son died, a daughter — the merchant-chief's friend's daughter — was born. Her father's ship having been lost, her mother kept it secret, guarding and raising her. Though the daughter offered her earnings to her mother, [once] she struck her mother's head with her foot. Later her ship wrecked, and clinging to a raft she was saved; on the four islands a goddess welcomed her, and she enjoyed happiness for many years. Though told not to go south, she went, and inside an iron house she saw a wheel of blazing fire spinning on a man's head. Understanding this too to be the fruit of striking [a mother's] head with a foot — and that she had done a like deed — the wheel passed onto the daughter's head. Hearing that she would have to endure it for sixty thousand years, she despaired; yet compassion for others arose, and the moment she prayed that she might take on the karmic fruit herself, the wheel lifted, and she was reborn in Tuṣita.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^1-6]]

- **The example of medicine and mantra** (སྨན་དང་སྔགས་ཀྱི་དཔེ།) — Khenpo Zhenga's annotation gives the example of curing a mere headache by mantra and medicine as the illustration of a small-scope focus. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **Establishing it by reasoning.** After the scriptural proof (verse 1-20), verse 1-21 opens the section that establishes [the benefits] by reasoning. Kunzang Pelden explains that although the presentation of karma is an extremely hidden matter, the domain of an omniscient buddha alone, the immeasurable benefits of bodhicitta can also be established by reasoning.

2. **Immeasurable merit arises even from a small-scope focus.** Even when the focus is small and there is only a wish — praying to cure the mere headache of a few beings — the mere becoming-manifest of the wish to benefit can generate immeasurable merit. This lays the ground for the greater reasoning of verse 1-22.

3. **The emphatic manner of explanation.** Ngulchu Thogme says that some commentaries explain this verse by attaching the merchant-chief's friend's daughter's story. Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains clearly that the chief point of the greater reasoning is: since a small focus yields immeasurable merit [1-21], therefore the vaster focus of bodhicitta yields even more immeasurable merit [1-22].

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse opens the establishing of the engaging mind's benefits by reasoning: it shows that even thinking one will cure the mere headache of a few beings gives rise, through the power of the wish to benefit, to immeasurable merit.

**Key points.**

- All commentaries, relying on the merchant-chief's friend's daughter's story, establish that even a small-scope focus yields immeasurable merit.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam, to make the emphatic manner of explanation definite, joins the small and the vast focus as example and meaning.
- Because this verse bears the burden of certainly connecting with the following verse, it relates chiefly to the emphatic manner of explanation.

<!-- sub:metaphors -->
#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The emphatic manner of explanation** — the reasoning that proceeds from the small to the great. Verse 1-21 sets up the minor premise (that even a small prayer focused on a few beings becomes immeasurable merit). Verse 1-22 draws the conclusion: it shows the far greater measure of the bodhisattva's prayer — wishing to remove the immeasurable suffering of each of measureless beings and to accomplish immeasurable qualities for each. Therefore ("what need is there to speak?") it is established that the merit arising from bodhicitta is beyond all measure. All commentaries attach the same concluding phrase, "what need is there to speak?"

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **The culmination of the emphatic manner of explanation.** Verse 1-22 completes the emphatic reasoning begun in 1-21. From the minor point (that a small focus gives rise to immeasurable merit), the conclusion follows: the vast prayer of bodhicitta — focused on each of measureless beings, removing each one's immeasurable suffering and accomplishing each one's immeasurable qualities — surpasses all measure of merit ("what need is there to speak?").

2. **Focusing on each being individually.** All commentaries stress the manner of focusing on each being individually: the bodhisattva takes up the thought focused on each and every being, not on beings in general. Both the wish to remove each one's immeasurable discomfort and the wish to accomplish each one's immeasurable qualities are considered one by one; and this individual, enumerated manner of focus is what makes the merit truly boundless.

3. **The scriptural point.** Khenpo Zhenga also adds a scripture: that bodhicitta exhausts former misdeeds and gathers an ocean of merit, and is explained as supreme even over the qualities of the shravakas. This adds a scriptural basis to the reasoning-conclusion of verses 1-21 and 1-22.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse completes the emphatic reasoning set out in the previous verse (1-21): it is a concise final establishing that immeasurable merit arises from the vast-focus aspiring mind.

**Key points.**

- That the bodhisattva's intention — to remove each being's suffering and to accomplish each one's qualities — gives rise to endless merit is established by the certainty of the emphatic reasoning.
- That by the power of bodhicitta misdeeds are exhausted and an ocean of merit is drawn together is also established by scripture.
- This verse opens the way for the following verses (from 1-23) that present [the bodhisattva] as truly worthy to behold.
