# Day 70 — Enduring like the fisherman: never turning back from my own afflictions

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 40 to 42 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Fishermen and farmers endure the hardship of cold and heat for the sake of mere food and clothing. Let us likewise join together and truly live up to our own promise, rather than talking big like a madman who does not know his own limits.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a saying that is easy to understand and encouraging.

"One who has not crossed cannot ferry others across. One who is not free cannot free others. A blind person cannot show the way. Only one who is free can set others free. Only one with eyes can show a blind person the way."

The meaning of this saying is this: unless we ourselves have crossed the water, we cannot carry someone else to the other side. A blind person who cannot see cannot show another blind person the way. In the same way, before we can free others from suffering, we must first free ourselves from our own afflictions.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, in order to help others, I will endure whatever hardship or discomfort of cold and heat comes, and I will help them.

**Explanation:** *(Patience Practice)* Fishermen and farmers endure the hardship of cold and heat for the sake of their own livelihood alone. We should endure even a small hardship for the happiness of all beings. If we act like this and help others, the strength of our patience will grow.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Undertaking hardship in order to accomplish the Dharma has great purpose, so it is worth doing. There are many who take on suffering for no purpose at all. Take, for example, those of us who have settled as refugees in Switzerland: we have to set out early in the morning while it is barely light, and during the working day we cannot relax even for a moment — we spend the whole time hurried and pressed, only to come home again in the same rush. People whose whole lives pass by in this constant hurry are doing it only to save up money; it is not for the sake of giving rise to any lasting, wholesome peace of mind in their own being. Even for the mere purpose of making a living in this one life, one has to take on such exhaustion. If fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and so on, thinking only of securing their own livelihood, endure every harm of cold, heat, and the like — taking on suffering, sustaining such hardship — then for me, who is training on the path for the happiness of all beings, why should the suffering that comes from that same kind of hardship not be endurable? It certainly must be endured. As Gungthangpa has said, "By this comfort-seeking Dharma, it is hard to gain liberation" — just so, when one does battle with the afflictions, one has to fight bearing hardship with fortitude; it is hard for things to go slowly and easily.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-40]]

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Even fishermen and outcastes — hunters, farmers, and the like — who intend to accomplish only their own bare livelihood, must, without rest by day or sleep by night, wear their feet down to the stone and their hands down to the wood, disregarding the harm of the suffering of cold, heat, rain, wind, and so forth, and endure it. If that is so, then for beings' sake — for the temporary happy states and the ultimate great happiness of buddhahood — why should someone like myself not endure the harm of hardship on the path? I must certainly endure it.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(In this source, 4-39's and 4-40's explanations form one continuous unbroken passage, with the real transclusion anchor sitting under 4-40; 4-39's page gives only the opening portion, cited here to this verse's own anchor. The passage continues through to its own end, trimmed of a trailing heading that belongs to the next verse's topic.)*

Further on it will also be said, "One should feel joy only in this — in dispelling the suffering of beings," as will be explained later. *(this couplet is Minyak Kunzang Sonam's own forward cross-reference to a later BCA verse, not an external scriptural citation)* If one thinks: although one must generally strive to overcome the afflictions, it is not fitting to strive in a way that conflicts with one's own comfort, given the many kinds of suffering and harm involved in fitting striving — consider this: in the world, the hunter such as the fisherman who lives by netting fish, or the outcaste such as the butcher who kills livestock, or the thief and the like who steal others' wealth, and the farmer who lives by farm work such as sowing seed and plowing, and — by "and so on" — the merchant who does all kinds of trade to gain great profit, and the servant who lives by depending on others merely for food and clothing, and the like: all of these, intending and accomplishing only the bare livelihood — the food and clothing — of this present life for themselves and their own wife, sons, and daughters, and pursuing that lowly worldly purpose, brace themselves against every harm of cold, heat, wind, rain, venomous snakes, and wild beasts, staking their very lives, and endure all such difficult, exhausting work day and night. If that is so, then for the sake of the supreme person who accomplishes the benefit and happiness of the boundless multitude of beings without exception, why should I not endure the suffering of hardship for the sake of the Dharma? I must endure it — and so one should generate a mind unafraid of the suffering of hardship. On this point, the Ornament [Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra] says:

> "By constantly recognizing suffering that benefits others as beneficial, one finds joy in it."

And so on, as it says. This teaches how, by always regarding it in this way, one generates the strength of a joyful mind, without regarding the hardship of abandoning the afflictions as a burden. The point explained above — the faults of the afflictions and the fittingness of enduring the hardship of abandoning them — is also stated in the Śikṣāsamuccaya, in its passage on enduring suffering, which says:

> "Thus, even the very foolish — porters, fishermen, farmers, and the like — through habituation to suffering, are not overcome by the hardness and discouragement of mind that arises over a long time even for things of very small result..."

— up to "...it is nothing but a change." These passages, too, should be applied here as is fitting.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

*(Trimmed of the verbatim root-verse repetition preceding "this means"; the mid-passage repetition of the verse's final line is kept, since it marks Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk's own transition from explaining the first three lines to explaining the fourth.)*

In the world, the fisherman, who makes his living by killing fish; the outcaste, who makes his living by killing water buffalo, yaks, pigs, and the like; the farmer, who makes his living by farm work in the fields; and others who do crafts, trade, and so forth — in short, all those who do such work — accomplish nothing whatsoever resembling benefit for others. Yet even those who think of or intend only that much — the mere livelihood of themselves alone, or of their own spouse and children — endure, whatever comes their way, whether it be winter's cold, summer's heat, hunger for lack of food, thirst for lack of drink, or illness and pain, bracing themselves against every such harm and, through sheer effort, applying themselves in every way to accomplishing their own particular work. If that is so, "why should someone like myself not endure it for the happiness of beings?" — meaning: since my present practice of the trainings of the bodhisattva's vow of bodhicitta is for the sake of establishing other sentient beings in benefit and happiness, both temporary and ultimate, why should someone like myself not endure a little cold or heat, hunger or thirst, illness or pain, should it arise? I must endure it, meditating on patience, and diligently accomplish irreversible practice of the bodhisattva's trainings without turning back — that is the meaning.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree: Since Ordinary People Endure Much Harm for Mere Livelihood, One Must Endure Hardship for the Great Benefit of All Beings

When the ordinary people of the world endure much severe harm even for the sake of the bare livelihood of themselves alone or of a small household, then for someone like a bodhisattva, who strives to accomplish the great happiness of all sentient beings, hardship on the path must all the more certainly be endured — this basic point is taught from the outset.

###### 2. Sabzang Mati Panchen Adds the Merchant and Other Occupations to the Case of the Fisherman and Farmer

Not only hunters, thieving outcastes, and farmers, but also merchants who travel long distances are added, showing that since even they endure severe harms, the bodhisattva's hardship is all the more fitting to endure.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Sets Out Every Kind of Occupation in Detail and Explains the Need for a Fearless Mind Toward Hardship, Relying at Length on the *Ornament* and the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*

Having shown that fishermen, outcastes, farmers, merchants, servants, and all others endure every harm even for a lowly worldly purpose, he explains that for the sake of the supreme being one must generate a mind unafraid of the hardship of the Dharma.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Sets Out the Example of Ordinary Work in Detail and Applies It to the Bodhisattva's Vow and Training

Having shown that those who do the world's various kinds of work endure every harm for the sake of their own or their household's livelihood alone, he explains that one who practices the training of bodhicitta for the benefit and happiness of all beings should all the more endure even the slightest harm and accomplish irreversible practice through meditating on patience.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Draws on a Modern Example of Working Life to Show the Difference Between Purposeless and Purposeful Hardship

Drawing on the example of working people who take on suffering for no purpose at all, he explains, citing Gungthangpa's words, that hardship undertaken for the sake of accomplishing the Dharma has great purpose and must certainly be endured.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse takes as its example how worldly fishermen, outcastes, and the like endure severe harms such as cold and heat for the sake of their own bare livelihood in this life, and teaches that, for the great purpose of accomplishing the benefit and happiness of every being without exception, the bodhisattva must certainly endure the hardship of the path.

**Key points.**

- Just as fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and the like endure every harm — cold, heat, venomous snakes, and so on — merely to secure the food and clothing of themselves or their household in this life, so too, for the sake of accomplishing the great happiness of every being without exception, it is fitting for someone like myself to endure the hardship of the path.
- When ordinary people, for the sake of a small purpose, endure harm without resting day or night, one must all the more generate a mind unafraid of the hardship of the Dharma for the sake of establishing beings in temporary and ultimate great happiness — the state of buddhahood.
- Just as the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* explains that even the very foolish — porters, fishermen, farmers, and the like — through habituation to suffering are not overcome by hardness or discouragement of mind, so too, in the bodhisattva's case, patience toward suffering undertaken for others' sake should be applied as is fitting.
- Just as the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* says that one constantly finds joy through recognizing benefit in suffering that benefits others, one should generate a mind that constantly rejoices in suffering that arises for others' sake.
- Since "one's own mere livelihood" refers to the lowly purpose of merely securing the food and clothing of this life, one must, all the more, certainly endure the hardship of practicing the training of bodhicitta, which has great purpose, rather than taking on suffering for no purpose.

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Gyaltsab quotes only the bare root verse under this verse's own transclusion, with no explanatory prose; his actual explanation for 4-41 is a joint explanation with 4-42, given immediately after the 4-42 transclusion, and is cited here to that anchor.)*

One has indeed committed to overcoming the afflictions in others' mindstreams — but is it not unfitting to strive for the sake of overcoming the afflictions in one's own mindstream, since doing so would mean falling into the extreme of peace? If one thinks this: having generated the intention and vowed to free from the afflictions all beings of the ten directions, reaching to the very limits of space, if at the time when I made that vow I myself am not even free from the afflictions — let alone accomplishing the welfare of others, I cannot even fully accomplish my own welfare — then, without even knowing my own measure as one fallen under the sway of the afflictions, how is it not madness to speak of freeing everyone else, one and all, from the afflictions? Since being bound in cyclic existence myself prevents me from fully accomplishing others' welfare, I must strive to overcome the afflictions in my own mindstream.

Having vowed to free all beings, reaching to the very limits of space in the ten directions, from the afflictions, if, at the time when I made that vow, I myself am not even free from the afflictions, then, without even knowing my own mind's or my own capacity's true measure, to speak in that way — how is that not madness? <!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

*(Sabzang quotes only the bare root verse under this verse's own transclusion. Earlier, at the time of generating the awakening mind, I vowed: "I shall free from cyclic existence — comprised of the three kinds of affliction — all beings without exception who pervade the ten directions, reaching to the very limits of space." Now, if, at the time when I made that vow, I myself remain not free from afflictions such as craving, then, without even knowing my own true capacity, to speak so recklessly — how is that not madness? Since it would amount to being like a madman, I must not turn back from my vow.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-42]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes only the bare root verse under this verse's own transclusion. His real explanation appears after the 4-42 transclusion and covers 4-41 and 4-42 jointly — cited here to that anchor. The verbatim scriptural quotations and the Ratnakūṭa simile he adduces there are broken out separately below under Scriptural Quotations and Metaphors.)*

One has indeed committed to freeing others from the afflictions, and it is fitting to strive for that. But is it not unfitting to strive specifically for the sake of overcoming the afflictions in one's own mindstream, since striving only for that would mean falling, like a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, into the extreme of peace? If one thinks this: having, earlier, at the time of generating the awakening mind, proclaimed aloud and vowed, "I shall free from the bonds of the afflictions all beings without exception, reaching to the very limits of space in the ten directions" — if now, at the present time, let alone accomplishing others' welfare by freeing others from the afflictions, I myself am not even free from the bonds of afflictions such as craving, having fallen under the sway of the afflictions such that I cannot even fully accomplish my own welfare — then, without even knowing the measure of my own mind and capacity, to say recklessly, as I please, that I will free every single being from the afflictions — how is that not madness? Since it is no different from madness, I must, from the very outset, strive in every way to overcome the afflictions in my own mindstream, without letting myself fall under their power. Why is this? Because it is only by being free oneself that one can free others; if one is not free from one's own afflictions and has not tamed one's own mindstream, one cannot accomplish the purpose of freeing others from their afflictions. For this reason, one must, from the very start, in order to overcome the enemy of one's own afflictions, view all suffering as insignificant through one's intention, and, relying in practice on the antidotes, apply diligence that never turns back, striving to tame one's own mindstream.

As for how to answer the objection stated above: the sūtras also say that a bodhisattva delights in cyclic existence, but that the same is not so with regard to nirvāṇa; this refers to blocking delight in that nirvāṇa which leans definitively toward taking rebirth in existence, and it praises, through the power of aspiration and compassion, constant delight and joy in taking rebirth in existence unstained by karma and afflictions — it does not mean blocking delight in that nirvāṇa in which weariness with cyclic existence and the cutting-off of karma and afflictions have come to an end. As for the point that one who is not free from one's own afflictions cannot free others from theirs, the Vinaya scriptures likewise state that there is no basis for taming others while oneself untamed, and so on. Since this shows that in order to tame and free others, one must first be tamed and freed oneself, one should become skilled in this sequence of practice as thus taught, and strive to abandon the afflictions.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Central Point of This Verse: Having Vowed to Free All Beings from the Afflictions, One Still Remains Unfree from One's Own

At the time of generating the awakening mind, one vows to free every sentient being reaching to the very limits of space from the bonds of the afflictions; yet at the present time, one shows that one has not become even slightly free from afflictions such as craving oneself.

###### 2. Speaking of Freeing Everyone Else While Under the Sway of One's Own Afflictions, Without Even Knowing One's Own Measure, Is No Different from the Speech of a Madman

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Ngulchu Thogme, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, and His Holiness the Dalai Lama — all five agree, with the words "how is this not madness," in singling out this contradiction.

###### 3. The Point That One Who Is Not Free from One's Own Afflictions Cannot Free Others Is Established by Scripture

Just as one who has not crossed cannot ferry others, and one who is blind cannot show the way, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the *Ratnakūṭa* and the Vinaya scriptures, explains that in order to guide and free others one must first become free and gain sight oneself.

###### 4. Therefore the Bodhisattva Must Strive Without Turning Back to First Overcome the Afflictions in His Own Mindstream

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen states clearly that, since being bound in cyclic existence prevents one from fully accomplishing others' welfare, one must strive to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream; Minyak Kunzang Sonam states clearly that, from the very outset, one must strive in every way, without letting oneself fall under the power of the afflictions, to overcome them in one's own mindstream.

###### 5. The Vow Is Explained as the Pledge Made at the Time of Generating the Awakening Mind, Calling the Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, and One's Guru of the Ten Directions to Witness

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that this vow was made in this way at the time of taking the vow of generating bodhicitta.

###### 6. Overcoming the Afflictions in One's Own Mindstream Is Not Falling into the Extreme of Peace but the Very Means of Fully Accomplishing the Vow Made for Others' Sake

Minyak Kunzang Sonam raises the doubt, "Would this not mean falling, like a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, into the extreme of peace?" and explains that, since one cannot accomplish the purpose of freeing others without being free oneself, taming one's own mindstream is itself part of accomplishing others' welfare.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The commentators agree in showing that, although one earlier vowed to free all beings of the ten directions from the bonds of the afflictions, at present one remains, oneself, not yet free from the afflictions — this is the chief contradiction the verse points out. Speaking of freeing others while under the sway of one's own afflictions and without knowing one's own capacity is like the speech of a madman; for the sake of fully accomplishing one's purpose for others, one must first strive to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream.

**Key points.**

- **The nature of the vow.** The vow made at the time of generating the awakening mind is the pledge, made calling the buddhas, bodhisattvas of the ten directions, and one's guru to witness, to free all beings of the ten directions from the afflictions.
- **The madman simile.** To speak of freeing others from the afflictions while not yet free from one's own is no different from the reckless speech of a madman who does not know his own mind or capacity.
- **What is established by scripture.** Just as one who has not crossed cannot ferry others across, and one who is blind cannot show the way, scripture establishes the point that one who is not free from one's own bondage cannot free others.
- **Not falling into the extreme of peace.** Striving to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream is not falling, like a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, into the extreme of peace — it is the very means of fully accomplishing one's purpose for others.
- **The essence of the practice.** Without turning back from the vow, the bodhisattva must, from the very outset, strive to overcome the afflictions in his own mindstream rather than falling under their power.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Having vowed that one will free all beings of the ten directions, reaching to the very limits of space, from the afflictions — even while such courage is arising in oneself — if, right now, one cannot even face down a single affliction, having fallen under the affliction's power, then, with no measure of one's own continuum beyond that, still to say that one will free the afflictions in the continuum of every sentient being — how is that not madness? It is simply the words of a madman.

"Therefore, in overcoming the afflictions, I must never turn back" — having seen a reason of just this kind, one must overcome the afflictions always in a spirit of joy and delight, rather than turning away and retreating from the task of overcoming them.

Without even knowing the measure of my own capacity — how much strength and majesty I actually have — to vow and proclaim, as I please, that I will free other sentient beings from the afflictions that are the cause of cyclic existence and the lower realms: one should recall or reflect that this is exactly as though I myself were a madman uttering the deranged ravings of madness.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Speaking of Freeing Everyone Else from the Afflictions Without Knowing One's Own Measure Is Like Madness

Even though bound by the afflictions and not free, to profess, without examining one's own capacity, that one will free every being from the afflictions is, all the commentaries agree, no different from a madman's speech — like vowing to free another from the water while oneself remains unfree of it.

###### 2. One Must Therefore Strive Always and Without Turning Back to Overcome the Afflictions

Because what one has vowed must be accomplished, one must apply oneself diligently and continuously, without postponing, to relying on the antidotes for the sake of overcoming or abandoning the affliction-enemy.

###### 3. Overcoming the Afflictions in One's Own Mindstream Is Not Only Compatible with the Vow Made for Others' Sake but Is Its Very Foundation

Since being bound in cyclic existence and not free from the afflictions prevents one from even fully accomplishing one's own welfare, let alone others', one must first strive to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream — and it is explained clearly that doing so does not amount to falling, like a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, into the extreme of peace.

###### 4. Though It May Resemble a Madman's Words, One May Not Abandon Training in the Bodhisattva's Precepts

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains clearly that, since one must properly accomplish what one has already vowed, it is not acceptable to leave the vow of bodhicitta untrained even though the earlier vow may resemble the reckless speech of a madman.

###### 5. The Basic Principle That One Who Is Not Free from One's Own Afflictions Cannot Free Others Is Established at Length by Scripture

Just as the *Ratnakūṭa* says that one who has not crossed cannot ferry others across, and one who is not free cannot free others; just as Nāgārjuna explains that one who is not tamed oneself cannot tame others; and just as the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* says that one must generate a hundredfold diligence since bound by the manifold bonds of self and other — Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains clearly that one must first become skilled in the sequence of taming and freeing one's own mindstream before abandoning the afflictions.

###### 6. The Manner of Overcoming the Afflictions Should Be Sustained, Without Turning Back, in a Spirit of Joy and Delight

Having seen a reason of this kind, His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that one must overcome the afflictions always in a spirit of joy and delight, rather than turning away and retreating from the task.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** While not yet free from the bonds of one's own afflictions, to speak of freeing every other sentient being from the afflictions is, all the commentaries agree, like the speech of a madman who does not know his own measure. In keeping with the vow one has already made, one must, in order to fully accomplish others' welfare, first strive without turning back, at all times, to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream.

**Key points.**

- **Speaking without knowing one's own measure is like madness.** To speak of freeing all beings from the sway of one's own afflictions while not yet free of them oneself is, by speaking recklessly without knowing one's own mind and capacity, no different from the words of a madman.
- **Taming and freeing oneself is the foundation for others' welfare.** Since, bound in cyclic existence and untamed, one cannot accomplish the purpose of freeing others from the afflictions, one must first tame and free oneself before taming others.
- **⚑ Answering the doubt of falling into the extreme of peace.** If one thinks that striving to overcome the afflictions in one's own mindstream means falling, like a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, into the extreme of peace: ⚑ since one cannot fully accomplish others' welfare unless one is free oneself, overcoming the afflictions in one's own mindstream is itself undertaken for others' sake, and so there is no contradiction.
- **One must not turn back from what one has vowed.** Even though the earlier statement may resemble a madman's words, one may not abandon the bodhisattva's precepts; what one has already vowed must be accomplished properly, without postponement.
- **One must strive, without turning back, in a spirit of joy.** For this reason, one must strive diligently, always in a spirit of joy and delight, without turning back, to overcome the affliction-enemy.
