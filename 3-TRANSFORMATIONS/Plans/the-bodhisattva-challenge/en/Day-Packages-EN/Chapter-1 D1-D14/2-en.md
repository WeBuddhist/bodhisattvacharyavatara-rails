## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:notification -->
### Notification

**Title:** Name one thing you keep putting off  
**Detail:** This human life is rare. Today, name one thing that matters but you keep delaying.

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Shantideva makes his first argument: a human life with the freedom to practise is extraordinarily rare. Today's two verses say how rare it is, and how briefly the wish to do good tends to flicker in us.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These two verses use a beautiful example to show how hard it is to find both supports: a good body and a good mind.

**Verse 4 (how hard it is to find a human body).** This human body, complete with the eight freedoms and ten riches, is very hard to find. The commentary gives an example: imagine a blind turtle at the bottom of the sea who comes up to the surface only once every hundred years. On the surface, the wind pushes around a single piece of wood with a hole in it. It is easier for that turtle to put its neck through the hole than it is to find a human body. With this human body we can reach every goal, both for now and for the long run. So if we do not practice virtue now, we may never find such a chance again. This is a gentle push to make good use of it.

**Verse 5 (how hard it is to find a good mind).** On a dark night thick with black clouds, a flash of lightning lets you see everything clearly for just a moment. In the same way, in the darkness of samsara, and through the blessing of the Buddhas, a good "wish to do good" arises in us only now and then, for just a moment, and very rarely. So when that good thought does appear, we should not waste it — we should use that moment and put our energy into doing good.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice**: Today, I will spend one hour doing something good. I will not spend this hour on my phone or other distractions.

**Explanation**: Today's verse says: a human life is very special and hard to get. If we do not do good things now, we may not get this chance again. Today, if you use one hour to do something good, you follow this teaching. You act now — you do not wait.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This support of leisure and opportunity is the unsurpassed basis for meditating on bodhicitta; it is extremely hard to find, and when found it is an excellent basis on which one can easily accomplish great aims, both temporary and ultimate. So now, while we have obtained this good support, we must make it meaningful — not squandering it through the laziness of procrastination or the ignorance of not knowing better. These days, among the many people in the world, very few have both faith in the Buddha's teaching and loving concern for others; so we should recognize how rare it is to have gained a human body with sound faculties and to have met the Dharma, and, casting off the discouragement of thinking "I can't do anything," strive to practice the Dharma as much as we can.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

**Explaining the eight freedoms and ten riches.** Seeing that this excellent support — the perfect leisure that is freedom from the **eight unfree states**, and the **ten riches** (the five of oneself and the five from others) — is extremely **hard to find**; and that even when this body is found, it is supremely suited **to accomplish a person's aims,** higher rebirth and liberation. **If you do not accomplish something beneficial with it,** how **could you come by such a perfect endowment again?** So, having obtained it, one must not let the time slip idly away and waste it.

As an illustration of the difficulty, the Kadam scriptures compare [the rarity of a human birth] to the fine dust of the great earth set against the little that would fit on a fingertip. ⚑ (The remainder of this passage is obscure in the source; it appears to say that, through the weight of wrongdoing, even a benefactor of the six perfections may fail to obtain a human body — reading uncertain.)

Dīpaṃkara [Atiśa] also said: "Life's span is short, and the things to be known are many."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

**Locating the passage within the text's outline.** From the standpoint of the structure, this shows how the meaning of the text's ten chapters accomplishes the generating, guarding, and increasing of bodhicitta, and its fruit. Both **leisure and opportunity** are explained. Leisure is freedom from the eight unfree states — [birth in] the hells, as a hungry ghost, as an animal, as a long-life god, as a barbarian, with wrong views, in a time empty of a buddha, and as one who is mute — the **eight freedoms**; and the **ten riches**, the five of oneself and the five from others, are explained at length. Because such a basis is **hard to find,** and even when found, if one does not accomplish something beneficial toward a person's aims, how could one come by it again? — thus he counsels us to generate bodhicitta, the valid measure of entering the path.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

**Leisure and opportunity in brief.** In verse 4, for the **eight freedoms** he settles the wording by collating the scriptural lists of the learned and accomplished, and explains the **ten riches** as the five of oneself and the five from others. This is extremely hard to find; and if one does not accomplish something beneficial with it — as a sutra says, **"Make [this body] a vessel for virtue"** — one should, without wasting the opportunity of this life, strive to generate bodhicitta, the valid measure of entering the path.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

**Prajñākaramati ⚑ cites at length in the great commentary** (⚑ ཤེས་རབ་འབྱུང་གནས་; identification of the commentator provisional):

> The hells, hungry ghosts, and animals,
> the long-life gods and barbarians,
> those with wrong views, [a time] empty of a buddha,
> and the mute — these are the eight unfree states.

Being born in any of these leaves no room to practice the Dharma, so they are "unfree." Free of them are the five leisures, and:

> Being human, born in a central land,
> with faculties complete, not reversed into wrong livelihood,
> and with faith in the [proper] ground —

these are the five riches of oneself; and:

> A buddha has come, has taught the Dharma,
> the teaching remains, there are followers,
> and others, out of loving concern, bring benefit —

these are the five riches from others. Such a jewel-like body, endowed with these qualities of opportunity —

a sutra says, **"Even to be human is rare — what need to speak of a perfect leisure?"** — is extremely hard to find. Now that one has obtained a special basis suited **to accomplish a sublime person's aims,** higher states and definite goodness, if **one does not accomplish the virtue of higher rebirth and liberation that benefits oneself and others,** how could one come by such a perfect endowment again in another life?

A sutra says: "In the world it is hard to find; / a buddha's arising, too, is very hard; / harder still are faith and going forth, / and accomplishment — very hard to find." So it is right to strive to take the essence of leisure and opportunity. Sabzang Mati adds his own words of counsel:

> Whoever gains it is carried across the ocean of birth,
> and sows too the virtuous seed of supreme awakening —
> this human birth, whose qualities surpass even the wish-fulfilling jewel:
> who would let it go without fruit?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

**How the human body is hard to find, at length.** He explains the **eight freedoms** — freedom from the eight unfree states — and the **ten riches** (five of oneself, five from others) at length. He shows the difficulty under four headings: its **cause** (⚑ the discipline of a pure [mental] continuum), its **scope** (a body superior to those of other realms), its **example** (the simile of the turtle's neck and the floating yoke), and its **number** (like the little that fits on a fingertip against the dust of the whole earth). ⚑ (The parenthetical glosses are terse in the source.)

**The human body is supreme for generating bodhicitta.** Now that one has obtained this basis for the buddhas' blessings and for progress, if one does not accomplish benefit with it, how could one come by it again?

Having thus shown how **the bodily support** is hard to find, the fifth verse then shows how **the mental support** is hard to find.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

**The eighteen [factors of] leisure and opportunity, explained at length.** "**Leisure**" means having the occasion — being free from the eight unfree states. The **eight unfree states** are: the hells, hungry ghosts, animals, non-Buddhists, barbarians, the mute [or dull], [a time when] no buddha has come, and birth in the realm of the long-life gods. The **five riches of oneself**: born human, in a central land, with faculties complete, not reversed into wrong livelihood, and with faith in the [proper] ground. The **five riches from others**: a buddha has come, has taught the Dharma, the teaching remains, there are followers, and others benefit one out of loving concern — each explained in detail.

This body is **extremely hard to find** and supremely suited **to accomplish a person's aims,** higher rebirth and definite goodness. **If you do not accomplish the benefit of Dharma with it, how will you ever come by such an endowment again?** — thus he urges us.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^1-4]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Eight Freedoms

Having the occasion, being free from the eight unfree states. All commentaries establish the eight: the hells, hungry ghosts, animals, long-life gods, barbarians, wrong views, a time empty of a buddha, and the mute.

###### 2. The Ten Riches

The five of oneself (born human, central land, faculties complete, not reversed into wrong livelihood, faith in the ground) and the five from others (a buddha has come, has taught the Dharma, the teaching remains, there are followers, benefit through loving concern) — all commentaries explain at length.

###### 3. The Cause, Scope, Example, and Number of Its Rarity

Minyak Kunzang Sonam shows the rarity of leisure and opportunity under four headings (cause = pure discipline; scope = superior to bodies of other realms; example = the turtle and the yoke; number = like the little on a fingertip against the dust of the whole earth).

###### 4. One Must Engage Without Wasting It

All commentaries agree: "if you do not accomplish something beneficial with it, how could you come by it again?" — urging effort in generating bodhicitta.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This fourth verse shows that the bodily support for generating bodhicitta — the human body of leisure and opportunity — is hard to find. All commentaries explain both the eight freedoms and the ten riches under this single verse, and the accomplished masters chiefly show its rarity through cause, scope, example, and number.

**Key points.**

- The eighteen [factors of] leisure and opportunity — all commentaries establish the eight unfree states and the ten riches, settled by citing Prajñākaramati's great commentary.
- All commentaries agree it is hard to find; Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains this under the four headings of cause, scope, example, and number.
- A person's aims — this body is supremely suited to accomplish both higher rebirth and definite goodness; and "if you do not accomplish benefit with it, how could it come again?" urges us on.
- Placement in the text's structure — Gyaltsab presents this verse as the foundation of the ten-chapter outline (the generating, guarding, and fruit of bodhicitta); Minyak Kunzang Sonam presents this verse as the rarity of the "bodily support" and the fifth verse as the rarity of the "mental support."

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

**Just as,** on a **night** with no sun, in the **pitch dark** with no moon, amid **cloud-banks** with no stars, a **flash of lightning** for a mere **instant lights up** all the things that are there — **so too,** through the **power** of the **buddhas'** blessing, **once in a great while** a slight flicker of the **mind's inclination toward merit** — that is, toward what brings benefit and happiness to the minds of **worldly** beings — **arises.**

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^1-5]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

From beginningless time, through the force of habituation to the afflictions, we spend most of the twenty-four hours of the day gripped by attachment, aversion, and confusion, carried along amid all sorts of wrongdoing and stray thoughts. In their midst, through outer and inner conditions, a mind of faith or a Dharma thought arises only now and then, for a moment — and even that is mostly difficult. Many people outwardly wear Dharma robes and call themselves practitioners, or sit fingering prayer beads reciting mani, but real virtue has to be accomplished within the mind; merely holding beads will not produce it. As Je Rinpoche said in the Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path, "there is no way to tame others while oneself is untamed" — taming oneself is extremely hard; and in the mind of someone who has neither known nor heard how things really are, all sorts of bad thoughts are bound to arise, so one must be watchful then.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-5]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

**The example.** Just as, on a sunless night, in the moonless pitch dark, amid starless cloud-banks, a flash of lightning for a single instant reveals forms — **the meaning:** likewise, in this world that is like a night where the sun of omniscient wisdom has not risen, a darkness that does not know what to adopt and what to reject, a cloud-bank overcast by the three or five mental poisons, through the combined power of two forces — the light-rays of the buddhas' aspirations and one's own previously gathered merit — once in a great while, in the minds of ordinary worldly beings shrouded in the darkness of confusion, there arises a glimmer of the mind inclined to accomplish merit, a wish to strive for virtue. And since it comes only about once in a hundred or twice in a thousand times, not often and not for long, it is extremely rare.

**A Kadam counsel.** When such a wish to accomplish virtue arises even once, in order not to let it fade but to increase it, one should narrow one's focus — not asking oneself or others for permission, but deciding firmly and making the commitment. Relying on the "ten innermost jewels" of the early Kadam masters and the "three fierce mantras" of Dharma-lord Tsangpa Gyaré, one is counseled to genuinely accomplish the sublime Dharma.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^1-5]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Explained together with the sixth verse.)* Just as, on the pitch-dark night amid cloud-banks, a flash of lightning for a single instant reveals forms, so too, through the power of the buddhas, once in a great while a brief glimmer of the mind that wishes to accomplish merit for worldly beings arises, and does not arise continuously. For this very reason — recognizing that virtue is feeble and lasts only a moment, while wrongdoing is powerful and constant — one should strive to accomplish virtue and to abandon wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^1-5]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Explained under the heading **"the mental support, the mind of merit, is rare."** Just as, on a pitch-dark night amid cloud-banks, a flash of lightning for a single instant reveals forms, so too, through the power of the buddhas, once in a great while a brief glimmer of the mind that wishes to accomplish merit — virtue — arises in worldly beings dwelling in the darkness of the suffering caused by wrongdoing; and since it does not arise often or for long, one should strive to generate and increase the wish to accomplish virtue.

He also gives supplementary lines of his own intent: "Though endowed with the two wings of leisure and opportunity, / and having eyes that see the faults and virtues of vice and goodness, / have we not, seized by the nets of gain, honor, and fame, / fallen into the trap of wrongdoing?"

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^1-5]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Under the heading **"reflecting that even a Dharma-inclined mind is rare":** just as, on a night after sunset with not even moonlight, in the deep gloom overcast by dense summer rain-clouds without even starlight, a flash of lightning for a brief instant clearly reveals some form — **the meaning:** through the power of the light-rays of the buddhas' compassion, once in a great while a glimmer of the mind of merit — the thought "I will enter the sublime Dharma for my own and others' sake" — may arise in worldly beings dwelling in samsara's darkness; but mostly it does not arise, and even when it does it does not last long, so it is extremely rare.

The *Heart of the Middle Way* (Madhyamaka-hṛdaya) says "...together with the lamp of the sublime Dharma," and so on, establishing that it is extremely hard to find. So one is counseled to take to heart, in this human life, the wish to accomplish the Dharma.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^1-5]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

**Outline: second, the mental support.** After the bodily support, he shows how the mental support is rare.

**Three-level reading of the simile:**
- a night with no sun = the absence of the sun-like omniscient wisdom of a buddha
- moonless pitch dark = the absence of the moon-like wisdom of the noble bodhisattvas realizing selflessness
- a starless cloud-bank = the absence of the star-like minds of shravakas and pratyekabuddhas

**The framework of the "mind of merit."** The "mind of merit" has three levels: the lesser person (the mind wishing for the merit of higher rebirth), the middling person (the mind concordant with liberation), and the great person (the aspiring and engaging bodhicitta that wishes to accomplish complete awakening for others' sake). The supreme mental support is bodhicitta itself.

**It arises from faith and compassion.** A sutra says: "Those whose devotion is especially vast / are always blessed by all the victors; / and those blessed by all the victors / give rise to the mind set on awakening." — so the buddhas continually bless those of great faith. Great compassion, too, is the root of generating [bodhicitta]; and a great compassion distinguished by the noble resolve is extremely rare to arise.

**Placement in the text's structure, in brief.** Having now shown that both the bodily and the mental supports are hard to find, he counsels that one must strive, wasting neither, to keep the virtuous mind from declining and to increase it.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

**Outline: second, showing how the mental support is hard to arise.**

**Applying the three-part simile:**
- a night with no sun = samsara, like a night lacking the sun-like omniscient wisdom
- moonless pitch dark = like darkness, lacking the moon-like wisdom of a bodhisattva realizing selflessness
- a starless cloud-bank = like a cloud-bank, lacking the star-like minds of shravakas and pratyekabuddhas

**The meaning.** The **mind of merit** is the mere aspiring mind, or engaging mind, that wishes to attain the state of complete buddhahood for the sake of other beings; such a thing arises only about once in a great while — it is extremely rare, almost as though it could not arise at all.

**The exhortation.** Reflecting on this, if one has found even a little bodhicitta, one must grow familiar with it again and again. Having now shown both how the bodily support is hard to find and how the mental support is hard to arise, he next shows the benefits of generating [bodhicitta].

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Both the Bodily and the Mental Supports Are Hard to Find

Gyaltsab, and especially Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Khenpo Kunga, treat the placement in the text's structure: verse 4 shows the bodily support (leisure and opportunity) is hard to find, and verse 5 shows the mental support (the mind of merit) is hard to find.

###### 2. The Framework of the "Mind of Merit"

The chief commentary (Minyak Kunzang Sonam) divides it into three levels; every mind wishing to accomplish any wholesome quality is a "mind of merit," and the supreme is aspiring/engaging bodhicitta.

###### 3. It Arises Only Once in a Great While

All commentaries agree; the phrase "a brief flicker" marks that it comes only about once in a hundred or twice in a thousand times and does not last (Khenpo Kunzang Pelden: "once in a hundred, twice in a thousand").

###### 4. It Arises Through the Power of the Buddhas

All commentaries agree; this mind of merit does not arise from one's own capacity alone but through the power of the buddhas' blessing; Minyak Kunzang Sonam glosses this clearly as "the light-rays of blessing."

###### 5. Once Arisen, It Must Not Fade but Be Increased

All commentaries give the same exhortation; Khenpo Kunzang Pelden — with the phrase "like a sign made by a blind person's hand" ⚑ (idiom uncertain) — urges that it not fade but be increased, relying on the early Kadam masters' ten innermost jewels and three fierce mantras.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** After showing that the bodily support (verse 4) is hard to find, this fifth verse chiefly shows that the **mental support** for generating bodhicitta — the mind of merit — is also extremely hard to find. The simile of a lightning flash amid the pitch-dark, clouded night shows that even with a human body, bodhicitta is hard to give rise to.

**Key points.**

- This verse shows that the **bodily and mental supports are equally hard** to find: even having gained the bodily support (leisure and opportunity), if the mental support (the mind of bodhicitta) does not arise, the opportunity is wasted.
- The **mind of merit** is very hard to arise, coming only as a **brief flicker once in a great while** and not lasting; all commentaries agree, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives a detailed reading of the simile (dividing it by the absence of the omniscient, bodhisattva, and shravaka-pratyekabuddha wisdoms).
- It is established that it **arises through the power of the buddhas**; Minyak Kunzang Sonam also cites scripture that the victors continually bless those of faith.
- Once **obtained, it must not fade but be increased.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden folds in the counsel of the early Kadam masters' ten innermost jewels and three fierce mantras; Ngulchu Thogme too, in his supplementary lines, warns against being caught — for gain, honor, and fame — in the trap of wrongdoing.
