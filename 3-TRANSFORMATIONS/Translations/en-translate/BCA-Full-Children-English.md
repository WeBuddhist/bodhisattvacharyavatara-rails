# A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life
### Beginner English Translation
Homage to the Buddha. ^I-1

## Chapter 1: The Benefits of Bodhichitta

I bow with deep respect to the Buddha, who has found perfect peace. I bow to his teaching, and to all his heirs who carry it forward. I bow to everyone else who deserves it too. Now, following the old teachings, I will explain simply how to live as a *bodhisattva* — someone who has taken up the wish to reach enlightenment in order to help everyone. ^1-1

Nothing I say here is new. I'm not a skilled writer either. So this isn't really meant to help other people. I wrote it just to make goodness grow in my own mind. ^1-2

By writing this, my own trust in doing good may grow a little. And if someone else, just as fortunate as me, happens to read it, maybe it will help them too. ^1-3

This life we have right now is precious and free, and it's very hard to get. If we don't use it well today, when will a chance like this come again? ^1-4

On a dark, cloudy night, a flash of lightning lights up the sky for one instant. In the same way, by the power of the Buddhas, people's minds turn toward goodness only for a moment, now and then. ^1-5

That's why goodness is always weak, while the power of harmful deeds is huge and terrifying. Nothing can overpower it — except *bodhichitta*, the wish to reach enlightenment in order to help everyone. ^1-6

The great Buddhas thought about this carefully for countless ages. They saw that this alone truly helps: through it, endless numbers of beings can reach the highest happiness with ease. ^1-7

Anyone who wants to escape the many sufferings of life, who wants to end the pain of others, and who wants to enjoy every kind of happiness, should never let go of bodhichitta. ^1-8

The instant bodhichitta is born in someone, even a miserable person trapped in the prison of the cycle of life is called a child of the Buddha. Gods and humans alike will bow to them. ^1-9

Like a rare potion that turns metal into gold, bodhichitta takes this ordinary, imperfect body and turns it into something priceless — the body of a Buddha. So hold on to this thing called bodhichitta, and hold on tight. ^1-10

The wisest guide of all beings looked closely at this and judged it more valuable than anything else. So if you want to be free from wandering through painful lives, hold firmly to this precious bodhichitta. ^1-11

Every other kind of goodness is like a banana tree: it bears fruit once, and then it's finished. But the tree of bodhichitta keeps bearing fruit forever. It never runs dry — it only keeps growing. ^1-12

Even someone who has done truly terrible things can be freed in an instant by relying on bodhichitta — the same way a person facing great danger is saved by relying on a hero. So why wouldn't a careful person rely on it? ^1-13

Bodhichitta burns away even huge wrongdoing in a single instant, like the fire that ends an age of the world. The wise and loving Maitreya taught its endless benefits well. ^1-14

In short, bodhichitta comes in two forms. There is the wish to reach enlightenment, and there is actually acting on that wish. ^1-15

Think of the difference between wanting to go somewhere and actually going there. In the same way, the two kinds of bodhichitta are different — one leads to the other. ^1-16

The wishing kind of bodhichitta brings great results, even while we're still caught in the cycle of life. But it doesn't produce the same steady, unbroken stream of goodness that the acting kind does. ^1-17

But from the moment someone takes up that acting mind with real determination — determined never to turn back until every single being, without exception, is set free — ^1-18

from that moment on, an unbroken stream of goodness flows out, as vast as the sky itself. It keeps flowing even while that person sleeps or loses focus. ^1-19

The Buddha himself taught this, with clear reasons, when Subahu asked him about it — for the sake of beings who aim a little lower. ^1-20

Even the simple thought, "I wish I could take away just one person's headache," is a kind thought. And a kind thought like that already carries great goodness. ^1-21

So what about wishing to remove the endless suffering of every single being, and wishing that every one of them could have endless good qualities? That goodness is beyond measure. ^1-22

Does any mother or father carry a wish like this? Do the gods carry it? Do the great sages? Does even Brahma carry it? ^1-23

Those very beings have never, even in their own private dreams, wished such a thing for themselves. So how could it ever arise in them for someone else's sake? ^1-24

Most beings don't even wish this kind of good for themselves. So this rare and precious jewel of a mind — the wish to help others — is something truly new and amazing when it appears. ^1-25

This jewel of a mind is the seed of joy for every being and the medicine for the whole world's suffering. How could anyone ever measure how good it is? ^1-26

If simply wishing to help others is worth more than worshiping the Buddhas, then what is actually working for the happiness of every single being worth? ^1-27

People want to escape suffering, yet they run straight toward it. They want happiness, yet, confused, they destroy their own happiness as if it were their worst enemy. ^1-28

Imagine someone who takes those who have no happiness and are full of suffering, and fills them with every joy, and cuts away every one of their pains — ^1-29

and who also clears away their confusion. Where could you find a friend like that? Where could you find such kindness? Where could you find such goodness? ^1-30

Even someone who simply repays a favor is praised for it. So what more should we say about a bodhisattva who helps others without even being asked? ^1-31

If someone gives a few people a simple meal just once — even barely enough to last them half a day, and even if they do it grudgingly — people already call that a good deed and honor them for it. ^1-32

So what should we say about someone who gives countless beings, forever, the highest happiness there is, fulfilling their every wish completely? ^1-33

The Buddha said that anyone who feels ill will toward such a generous bodhisattva will stay in a hellish state for as many ages as the number of harmful thoughts they had. ^1-34

But if someone's mind turns bright and glad toward a bodhisattva instead, the good result grows even greater than that. Even when terrible things happen to them, bodhisattvas don't fall into wrongdoing — their goodness only grows naturally. ^1-35

I bow before the body of anyone in whom this precious, sacred mind has arisen. I go for refuge to this source of happiness — even someone who harms it ends up connected to joy. ^1-36

*Thus ends Chapter One: "The Benefits of Bodhichitta."* ^1-a

## Chapter 2: Confessing Wrongdoing

To truly hold on to that precious bodhichitta, I now make offerings — to the Buddha, to his pure teaching, and to the bodhisattvas, who are like oceans of good qualities. ^2-1

I offer every flower and fruit that exists. I offer every kind of medicine, every jewel in the world, and every clear, lovely stretch of water. ^2-2

I offer the mountains made of jewels, the quiet, beautiful forests, the trees covered in blossoms, and the branches bending under ripe fruit. ^2-3

I offer the sweet scents of heaven, incense, wish-granting trees, and trees made of jewels. I offer crops that grow without anyone planting them, and every other beautiful thing worth giving. ^2-4

I offer lakes covered in lotus flowers, where swans sing beautifully. I offer everything, all the way out to the edges of endless space — everything that belongs to no one at all. ^2-5

I hold all of this in my mind and offer it to the Buddha and to his heirs, the bodhisattvas. Great, compassionate ones, please think of me kindly and accept these gifts. ^2-6

I have so little goodness of my own, and I am truly poor — I have nothing else worth giving. So please, you who care about helping others, accept this offering anyway, for my sake, through your own great power. ^2-7

I give my whole self, forever, to the Buddhas and their heirs. Please, greatest of beings, take me as your own. With deep respect, I will serve you. ^2-8

Now that you have taken me in, I will have no fear, even in this hard life, and I can truly help others. I will leave my past wrongdoing behind me, and from now on, I won't do such things again. ^2-9

Picture a bathing hall that smells wonderful, with a floor of clear, shining crystal. Its pillars glow with jewels, and pearls hang shimmering from a canopy above. ^2-10

There, with jeweled vases full of sweet-smelling water, and with music and singing all around, I offer a bath to the Buddhas and to their heirs. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with the finest cloths, clean and beautifully scented. Then I offer them lovely robes in beautiful colors, filled with sweet fragrance. ^2-12

With soft, fine clothes of every kind, and with hundreds of beautiful ornaments, I also dress the great bodhisattvas — Samantabhadra, Manjushri, Avalokiteshvara, and all the rest. ^2-13

With the finest perfumes, whose scent spreads through the whole universe, I anoint the bodies of every Buddha — bodies that shine like polished gold. ^2-14

I offer the Buddhas, who deserve the highest respect, beautiful flowers of every kind — soft blossoms, lotuses, and sweet-smelling garlands woven with care. ^2-15

I offer clouds of the finest incense, its beautiful scent drifting through the air. And I offer heavenly food and drink of every kind. ^2-16

I offer rows of jeweled lamps set upon golden lotus flowers. And on ground perfumed with incense, I scatter petals of beautiful flowers. ^2-17

I offer great palaces filled with beautiful songs of praise, hung with strings of pearls and jewels that fill the whole sky — I offer all of this to those whose very nature is compassion. ^2-18

I offer beautiful jeweled umbrellas with golden handles, decorated all around, raised high and graceful — I offer these always to the Buddhas. ^2-19

And beyond all these, may there always be — everywhere — great clouds of offerings, filled with beautiful music, that soothe every being's suffering and bring them peace. ^2-20

May flowers and jewels rain down endlessly on the true teaching, on the *Three Jewels* — the Buddha, his teaching, and the community that follows it — and on every sacred shrine and image. ^2-21

Just as Manjushri and the other great bodhisattvas once made offerings to the Buddhas, I now make the very same offerings to the Buddhas and their heirs. ^2-22

With endless songs of praise, I praise these beings whose good qualities run as deep as the ocean. May clouds of beautiful praise rise up before them, again and again, forever. ^2-23

I bow down before every Buddha of the past, present, and future, and before the teaching and the whole community of practitioners. I bow with as many bodies as there are specks of dust in the universe. ^2-24

I bow to the places where bodhichitta first arose, and to every sacred shrine. I bow to the teachers, the masters, and to those who live by the highest discipline. ^2-25

Until I reach full enlightenment myself, I take refuge in the Buddhas. In the same way, I take refuge in the teaching, and in the whole community of bodhisattvas. ^2-26

To the perfect Buddhas everywhere, and to their compassion and their students' rising bodhichitta, I join my palms together and make this request. ^2-27

Throughout this endless cycle of life, in this life and in every life before it, whatever wrong I have done myself, or caused someone else to do, ^2-28

or whatever wrong I felt glad about — overpowered as I was by confusion — now that I see these mistakes clearly, I confess every one of them, from the bottom of my heart, before the Buddhas. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have caused — through my body, my speech, or my mind, driven by disturbing emotions — to the Three Jewels, to my parents, or to any other teacher, ^2-30

whatever terrible wrong I, someone marked by so many faults, have done — I confess all of it now, before every Buddha who has ever guided beings. ^2-31

Maybe death will come for me before I've cleared away this wrongdoing. Please protect me quickly, so I can truly be freed from it in time. ^2-32

Death is not something we can trust or predict. It doesn't wait until our work is finished. Whether we're healthy or sick, we can never be sure of tomorrow. ^2-33

I never truly understood that I would have to leave everything behind one day. Because of that, I've done all kinds of wrong — for people I love, and against people I dislike. ^2-34

The people I dislike will be gone one day. The people I love will be gone too. I myself will be gone. Everything, in the end, disappears. ^2-35

Everything I've ever enjoyed becomes just a memory, like something from a dream. Once it's gone, it's gone — we can never see it again. ^2-36

Even in this one lifetime, many people I loved, and many I disliked, have already died and gone. But the wrong I did because of them is still right here, waiting for me. ^2-37

I never really understood that I too am only passing through this life. Because of that, driven by confusion, attachment, and anger, I've done wrong again and again. ^2-38

Day and night, without ever stopping, my life is running out. No extra time is ever added back in. So how could I possibly avoid dying one day? ^2-39

When I lie there dying, even if everyone I love is gathered around me, I will be completely alone in feeling that pain — no one else can feel it for me. ^2-40

When death finally comes for me, what good will family do? What good will friends do? Only goodness built up over a lifetime can protect me then — and I haven't even built that up. ^2-41

I have been so careless, Buddhas. I never really understood a danger this serious. And because of that, I've piled up so much wrongdoing, just for the sake of this short life. ^2-42

Think of someone being led away to lose an arm or a leg. Even before it happens, they're already terrified — mouth dry, eyes wide with fear, completely changed from how they looked before. ^2-43

So imagine, then, how much worse it is to be seized by the terrifying messengers of Death, and struck by fear and sickness beyond anything we know. ^2-44

"Who can truly save me from this terrible fear?" — with eyes wide open in panic, that person searches every direction, looking for anyone who can help. ^2-45

Seeing no help anywhere, in any direction, that person falls into complete despair. And if there really is no refuge anywhere — what will I do, when my own time comes? ^2-46

That is why, starting today, I take refuge in the Buddhas — the protectors of the world, who work so hard to keep every being safe, and whose great power can dispel any fear. ^2-47

In the same way, I take refuge in their teaching too — the teaching that clears away all the fears of this life — and in the whole community of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Trembling with fear, I offer myself to the bodhisattva Samantabhadra. I offer my whole self to the bodhisattva Manjushri. ^2-49

And to the compassionate Avalokiteshvara, whose kindness never fails, I cry out in desperation: "Please, protect me, even though I have done wrong." ^2-50

I cry out from the depths of my heart, seeking refuge in Akashagarbha, Kshitigarbha, and every great and compassionate protector there is. ^2-51

I take refuge in Vajrapani, the bodhisattva so powerful that even the terrifying messengers of Death flee in fear at the mere sight of him. ^2-52

In the past, I ignored what you taught. But now that I truly see this danger, I take refuge in you. Please, clear away this fear quickly. ^2-53

If we're afraid of an ordinary illness, we still listen to the doctor's advice. So how much more should I listen to good advice, when I'm sick again and again with faults like craving and anger? ^2-54

Just one of these harmful states of mind is enough to destroy every person on earth. And no other medicine for it can be found anywhere in the world. ^2-55

So refusing to follow the advice of the one doctor who knows everything, and who can heal every kind of pain — that would be the height of foolishness. ^2-56

If we're careful even standing near a small, everyday cliff, how much more careful should we be about a fall that could last for ages, into the deepest suffering there is? ^2-57

It's not okay to relax and think, "I definitely won't die today." The time when I will cease to exist is absolutely certain to come. ^2-58

Who can promise me safety from death? How can I be sure I'll escape it? Since it's certain that I won't be here forever, how can my mind possibly feel at ease? ^2-59

What's actually left of all the things I once enjoyed, now that they're gone? And yet, clinging to them so tightly, I even went against the good advice of my teachers. ^2-60

Since I must one day leave this life behind — leaving every friend and relative — and travel on completely alone, what does it really matter, in the end, who I loved and who I disliked? ^2-61

"How can I be free from the suffering that wrongdoing brings?" This is the one thought that deserves my attention, day and night, all the time. ^2-62

Whatever wrong I have done, out of not knowing any better — whether it was wrong by its very nature, or wrong because it broke a commitment I made — ^2-63

standing right there before the Buddhas, palms joined together, afraid of the suffering it will bring, I bow again and again, and I confess every bit of it. ^2-64

Buddhas, please accept that I have truly done wrong. None of it was good — so from now on, I will never do it again. ^2-65

*Thus ends Chapter Two: "Confessing Wrongdoing."* ^2-a

## Chapter 3: Taking Hold of Bodhichitta

I rejoice with joy in every bit of goodness that gives beings relief from the sufferings of the lower realms, and in every suffering being who finds happiness instead. ^3-1

I rejoice in every bit of goodness gathered as a cause for enlightenment. I rejoice when any being who has truly been freed from the cycle of life's suffering. And I rejoice in the enlightenment of the Buddhas, and in every stage a bodhisattva reaches on the way there. ^3-2

I rejoice with joy in every vast, ocean-like intention that brings happiness to beings, and in every deed that truly helps them. ^3-3

With my palms joined together, I ask the Buddhas everywhere: please light the lamp of your teaching for beings who are lost in the darkness of suffering. ^3-4

With my palms joined, I ask the Buddhas who wish to pass into final peace: please don't leave this world in darkness — please stay with us for countless ages instead. ^3-5

By doing all of this, whatever goodness I have gathered — may it clear away all the suffering of every single being. ^3-6

For as long as any being is sick, until they are fully healed, may I become their medicine. May I become their doctor. May I become the one who cares for them. ^3-7

May I rain down food and drink and clear away the pain of hunger and thirst. And in times of terrible famine, may I myself become the food and the drink beings need. ^3-8

For beings who are poor and have nothing, may I become an endless treasure. May I always be right there for them, giving them everything they need. ^3-9

My body, everything I own, and all the goodness I've ever done — I give it all away completely, without holding anything back, so that I can truly help every being. ^3-10

True peace comes from giving up everything, and my mind is set on that peace. Since I have to give everything up eventually anyway, the best thing is to give it all to other beings, right now. ^3-11

I've already given this body of mine to every being, to use however makes them happy. They can hurt it, insult it, or do whatever they want with it — it's theirs now. ^3-12

Even if they play games with my body, or make fun of it, or joke about it — I've already given it away, so why should I mind? ^3-13

Let them do whatever doesn't actually harm it. And may no one who ever thinks of me end up worse off because of it, ever. ^3-14

Whether someone feels angry at me or has faith in me, may that very feeling always become a cause for everything good in their life. ^3-15

Whoever insults me, whoever harms others, or mocks me — may all of them still find their way to enlightenment in the end. ^3-16

May I become a protector for those who have no one to protect them, a guide for travelers on their journey, and a boat, a ferry, and a bridge for anyone who needs to cross to safety. ^3-17

May I become an island for those who need one, a bed for those who need rest, and a servant for every being who needs help. ^3-18

May I become a wish-granting jewel for beings, a magic vase, a powerful medicine, a wish-fulfilling tree — everything beings could ever wish for. ^3-19

Just as the earth and space itself quietly support every living thing, always, without end, may I too become something that supports every being's life, in every way I can. ^3-20

And just as space reaches everywhere, may I support every single being, everywhere, all the way until every one of them finds true peace. ^3-21

Just as the Buddhas of the past first gave rise to bodhichitta, and then trained, step by step, in the way a bodhisattva lives — ^3-22

in that same way, for the sake of every being, I too now give rise to bodhichitta. And in that same way, I too will train, step by step, exactly as they did. ^3-23

Someone wise, having taken up bodhichitta with a clear and joyful mind, should keep encouraging that mind to grow even further, like this: ^3-24

Today my life has finally borne fruit. I have truly made the most of this human life. Today I am born into the family of the Buddhas — today, I become one of their children. ^3-25

So now, no matter what I do, I must act in a way that fits this family. I must never bring shame to this pure, noble lineage. ^3-26

It's like a blind person stumbling upon a precious jewel in a heap of trash. By some strange stroke of good fortune, this bodhichitta has arisen in someone like me. ^3-27

This is the greatest medicine there is — it defeats even death itself. It's also an endless treasure that ends the poverty of every being. ^3-28

It's the best medicine there is — it cures every sickness beings suffer from. It's the resting tree for beings who are worn out from wandering this long road of life. ^3-29

It's the bridge that carries every being safely away from the lower realms. It's like the moon rising in the mind, cooling the burning pain of harmful emotions. ^3-30

It's the great sun that clears the fog of confusion from beings' eyes. It's like the finest butter that rises when the milk of the true teaching is churned. ^3-31

For every traveler journeying along the road of life, hoping for a taste of happiness, bodhichitta sits right there like a great feast — it satisfies every single guest completely. ^3-32

Today, in front of every Buddha, I have invited every single being to be my guest — on the way to becoming a Buddha themselves, and enjoying happiness along the way. Gods, spirits, and every kind of being: rejoice! ^3-33

*Thus ends Chapter Three: "Taking Hold of Bodhichitta."* ^3-a

## Chapter 4: Carefulness

Once a child of the Buddha has firmly taken up bodhichitta like this, they should always try their best, without ever slacking off, to never stray from this training. ^4-1

If you start something in a rush, or without really thinking it through, it's fine to reconsider — even if you'd already promised to do it — and decide whether to continue or let it go. ^4-2

But bodhichitta was examined carefully by the Buddhas and their wisest heirs. And I've examined it carefully myself too. So why would I hold back now? ^4-3

If I make this promise and then don't actually follow through, I will have let down every single being. What kind of future could that possibly lead to? ^4-4

It's said that even someone who just thinks about giving away some small thing, and then doesn't, can end up reborn as a hungry ghost because of it. ^4-5

So if I sincerely invite every being to share in the highest happiness there is, and then let them all down — how could I possibly expect a good future for myself? ^4-6

It's said that even beings connected to someone who abandoned bodhichitta can still somehow find freedom — but how that actually works is beyond ordinary understanding. Only a Buddha truly knows. ^4-7

So for a bodhisattva, breaking this promise is the most serious mistake there is. It means failing every single being, all at once. ^4-8

Anyone who gets in the way of a bodhisattva's good work, even for one moment, is harming every being's welfare — and that leads to endless suffering in the lower realms. ^4-9

If just ruining one person's happiness already causes harm to yourself, imagine the harm caused by ruining the happiness of beings as countless as the whole of space. ^4-10

So when the power of these mistakes and the power of bodhichitta keep going back and forth inside me, reaching a truly advanced stage on the path just gets delayed, again and again. ^4-11

So I really do need to carry out this promise, with real care and dedication. If I don't put in the effort starting today, I'll just keep falling lower and lower. ^4-12

Countless Buddhas have already come into this world to help every being. And yet, because of my own faults, I somehow missed out on being healed by any of them. ^4-13

And if I keep acting the same way now, I'll just keep experiencing sickness, being trapped, being cut and torn apart, again and again, in the lower realms. ^4-14

A Buddha appearing in the world, having real faith, getting a human life, and actually being able to practice goodness — all of that together is incredibly rare. When will I ever get a chance like this again? ^4-15

Even a good day like today — healthy, well-fed, free of harm — can trick us. Life passes by in an instant. This body is only ours to borrow for a little while. ^4-16

If I keep behaving the way I have been, I won't even get a human life again next time. And without a human life, there's only room for more wrongdoing — no chance for goodness at all. ^4-17

If I don't practice goodness now, even when I have the perfect chance to do so, then what will I do later, when I'm lost and confused in the suffering of the lower realms? ^4-18

If I don't do good, and keep piling up wrongdoing instead, I won't even hear the words "happy rebirth" again for a hundred million ages. ^4-19

That's exactly why the Buddha said gaining a human life is incredibly rare — as rare as a blind turtle in a vast, stormy ocean happening to poke its neck through the one hole in a piece of driftwood floating on the surface. ^4-20

If even one moment of wrongdoing can lead to an entire age spent in the deepest hell, imagine what all the wrongdoing built up since beginningless time could do. How could that possibly lead anywhere good? ^4-21

And it's not like experiencing that suffering just once clears the slate. While going through it, we tend to create even more wrongdoing at the same time. ^4-22

Having found a chance this rare and precious, if I still don't practice goodness, there's no greater way to fool myself, and no greater foolishness, than that. ^4-23

And if I understand all this clearly, and still slip back into laziness out of confusion, then when death finally comes, a terrible grief will rise up in me for sure. ^4-24

When my body is burning for ages in the unbearable fires of hell, an equally unbearable fire of regret will be burning in my mind too. ^4-25

Having somehow, by pure good fortune, found this rare chance to do something truly beneficial, how could I knowingly lead myself straight back into hell? ^4-26

It's like I've lost my mind entirely, as if someone put a spell on me. I don't even know what's confusing me. What is actually going on inside me? ^4-27

Enemies like disturbing emotions don't even have arms or legs. They aren't brave, and they aren't clever. So how in the world have they managed to make a slave out of me? ^4-28

They sit right there inside my own mind, happily causing me harm, and I still don't push back against them. Putting up with that isn't real patience — it's just misplaced tolerance. ^4-29

Even if every god and every demigod turned against me as enemies, none of them could actually drag me into the fires of the deepest hell. ^4-30

But this powerful enemy — disturbing emotions — could throw me straight into that fire in a single instant. It's so strong it wouldn't leave even ashes behind, even if it struck the greatest mountain itself. ^4-31

My real enemy, these disturbing emotions, has been with me since beginningless time — no start, no end in sight. No other enemy could ever last that long. ^4-32

Every other kind of relationship, when cared for well, tends to bring help and happiness back. But disturbing emotions are the opposite — the more I indulge them, the more suffering and harm come back to me. ^4-33

So if I let this constant enemy — the one true cause of all my growing troubles — settle permanently in my own heart, how could I ever feel safe or truly happy? ^4-34

And if these emotions — the very jailers of this life, the ones who act as executioners in hell — are allowed to stay tangled up in my own clinging mind, how could I ever find real happiness? ^4-35

So until I've truly defeated this enemy, once and for all, I won't give up this effort. Even a proud person who gets angry over some small insult won't rest until they've settled the score — so how much more should I keep going here? ^4-36

Think of soldiers going into battle. Even though death and pain are a natural part of it, they don't turn and run the moment they're wounded — not until the battle is actually won. ^4-37

So for me, determined to truly defeat this real enemy — the constant source of all suffering — why would I ever lose heart over a little hardship along the way? ^4-38

Soldiers wear the scars enemies gave them almost like badges of honor. So why should the difficulty I face while striving for something truly important bother me at all? ^4-39

If fishermen and farmers can put up with cold, heat, and hardship just to survive day to day, then surely I can put up with the same, for the sake of the happiness of every single being. ^4-40

I promised to free every being everywhere — to the very edge of space — from these harmful emotions. But look at me — I'm not even free from them myself. ^4-41

Wasn't it a bit reckless to make a promise that big without even knowing my own limits? Well, since I've already said it, I must never give up on defeating these emotions now. ^4-42

I will throw myself fully into this fight, holding on with real determination. There's only one enemy I will never make peace with: the emotions that keep destroying my own peace. ^4-43

Even if I were burned alive, or killed, or beheaded, I will never bow down to my real enemy — these disturbing emotions — no matter what. ^4-44

An ordinary enemy, even if driven away, can just settle somewhere else, build up strength, and come back later. But disturbing emotions aren't like that at all — ^4-45

once I truly clear a disturbing emotion from my mind using wisdom, where could it possibly go to hide, and come back from later? It only ever seems to return because my own mind hasn't made the effort to root it out completely. ^4-46

These emotions don't actually live out in the world, or in our senses, or anywhere in between, or anywhere else at all — so where exactly are they, that they can cause every being so much harm? They're like an illusion. So let go of the fear in your heart, and instead put your effort into understanding this clearly. Why would you let yourself suffer needlessly in the lower realms? ^4-47

Having thought all of this through carefully, I will make every effort to actually carry out this training. After all, if a sick person won't even listen to the doctor's advice, how can medicine alone ever make them well? ^4-48

*Thus ends Chapter Four: "Carefulness."* ^4-a

## Chapter 5: Guarding Awareness

If you want to keep your training solid, you first have to protect your own mind, very carefully. Without protecting the mind, there's no way to keep any training at all. ^5-1

A wild, untamed elephant let loose can cause a lot of damage. But this untamed mind of ours, when let loose, can cause damage far worse — even leading us into the deepest hell. ^5-2

But if we tie this "elephant" of the mind down firmly with the rope of mindfulness, every danger disappears, and every kind of goodness comes easily within our reach. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears, snakes, enemies of every kind, the guardians of hell, evil spirits, and demons — ^5-4

all of them are tamed the moment this one mind is tamed. All of them are held in check just by holding this one mind in check. ^5-5

The Buddha himself taught this clearly: every fear, and every kind of endless suffering, comes from nowhere else but the mind. ^5-6

Who actually forged the weapons used in hell? Who built that burning iron ground? Where did all that fire come from? ^5-7

The Buddha taught that all of it comes from nothing but a mind full of wrongdoing. So really, in this whole world, there is nothing more dangerous than an untamed mind. ^5-8

If truly perfecting generosity meant actually ending everyone's poverty, then since there are still poor people today, how could any past Buddha have ever perfected it? ^5-9

The perfection of generosity actually comes from the intention to give everything away to everyone, along with whatever good comes from it. So really, it's a matter of the mind, not of the outcome. ^5-10

Where could you even send all the fish in the world so none of them are ever killed? The perfection of ethical discipline doesn't come from actually stopping every harm out there — it comes from truly giving up the intention to harm. ^5-11

There are endless numbers of difficult beings out there — as many as the sky is vast — and I could never overcome them all. But if I overcome just this one thing, my own anger, it's as if I've overcome every enemy there is. ^5-12

Where would you even find enough leather to cover the whole earth? But with just a small patch of leather under your feet, it's as if the whole earth is covered for you. ^5-13

In the same way, I can't possibly control everything that happens around me. But if I control this mind of mine, I don't need to control anything else at all. ^5-14

Just as one clear, virtuous state of mind alone can lead to a wonderful rebirth, an action of body or speech without that same clear intention behind it just doesn't have the same power. ^5-15

Even chanting and doing hard spiritual practices for a very long time is pointless, the wise tell us, if the mind is distracted somewhere else the whole time. ^5-16

Anyone who doesn't understand this secret — that everything really comes down to the mind — will just wander around pointlessly, even while trying hard to find happiness and avoid suffering. ^5-17

That's why I must hold onto this mind of mine and guard it carefully. Without that one discipline — protecting the mind — what good would any other discipline do? ^5-18

Just as someone with an open wound is extra careful moving through a rough crowd, I should always protect this "wound" of mine — my own mind — while I live among difficult people. ^5-19

If we're that careful about a small physical wound, afraid of even a little pain, how much more careful should we be protecting the mind — when what's at stake is being crushed under the weight of the lower realms? ^5-20

Living with this kind of care, whether surrounded by difficult people or by temptation, a truly dedicated practitioner's commitment never weakens. ^5-21

Let me lose my possessions, my reputation, even my body and my life. Let any other kind of goodness decline. But never — never — let this mind of mine decline. ^5-22

To everyone who wants to protect their mind, I say this, with my palms joined together: please, guard mindfulness and awareness with everything you've got. ^5-23

Just as someone sick in bed has no strength to do anything, someone whose mind is clouded by confusion has no real strength to do anything meaningful either. ^5-24

Whatever someone without real awareness has heard, thought about, or practiced just doesn't stick — it leaks right out of their memory, like water from a cracked jar. ^5-25

Even people who are well-educated, full of faith, and hard-working can still fall into mistakes — simply because they lack this basic awareness. ^5-26

This "thief" — lack of awareness — sneaks in right after mindfulness slips, even a little. It steals away all the goodness someone has carefully built up, and they end up in a bad rebirth anyway, just like someone robbed blind. ^5-27

This gang of thieves — our disturbing emotions — is always on the lookout for a way in. The moment they find one, they steal our goodness and ruin any chance of a good rebirth. ^5-28

So I should never let mindfulness wander away from the door of my mind. And if it does wander off, I should remember the dangers of the lower realms and bring it straight back. ^5-29

Mindfulness comes easily to people who are fortunate enough to stay close to a good teacher, follow their guidance, and act with real respect and a healthy sense of caution. ^5-30

"The Buddhas and bodhisattvas see everything, everywhere, without any obstruction at all. I am always standing right there in front of them" — ^5-31

thinking this way naturally brings up a sense of conscience, respect, and healthy caution — and it makes remembering the Buddha come back to us, again and again, all on its own. ^5-32

When mindfulness stays right there at the door of the mind, standing guard, awareness naturally comes along with it — and even if it slips away for a moment, it comes right back. ^5-33

First, I should recognize clearly that this untamed mind of mine is full of faults. Once I see that, I should hold it steady — as firmly as if I were fixing a plank of wood in place. ^5-34

I shouldn't let my eyes wander around pointlessly. Instead, I should keep my gaze low, and look at things with real intention, all the time. ^5-35

To give my eyes a rest now and then, it's fine to glance around. And if I happen to see someone, I should look at them warmly and say, "Welcome, I'm glad you're here." ^5-36

To watch for danger on the road, I should check around me from time to time. And when I stop to rest, I should look behind me too, before looking forward again. ^5-37

After checking both ahead and behind, I should then move forward, or turn back, as needed. In every situation, I should act only once I really understand what's needed. ^5-38

Once I've decided how my body should be positioned for some task, I should check in on it now and then, and see how it's actually doing. ^5-39

Just as you'd watch a wild elephant very carefully once it's tied to a strong post, I should watch this wild "elephant" of my own mind just as closely, so it never breaks free and wanders off. ^5-40

"Where has my mind wandered off to now?" — I should check in on myself like this, again and again, so that my focus never slips, not even for a single instant. ^5-41

In an emergency, or during a celebration, where that level of focus just isn't possible, it's fine to relax a bit — even the teachings say that, in moments of real generosity, we can set the formal rules aside for a moment. ^5-42

Once I've thought something through and started doing it, I shouldn't let my mind wander off to something else. I should stay focused, and see that one thing through first. ^5-43

Doing things this way, one task at a time, everything actually gets done well. Otherwise, nothing gets done properly, and small distractions and disturbing emotions just keep growing. ^5-44

When there's idle gossip and all kinds of flashy entertainment going on around me, I should just let go of any pull toward it. ^5-45

If I catch myself pointlessly digging in the dirt, pulling up grass, or scratching lines on the ground for no reason, I should remember the Buddha's training and stop right away. ^5-46

Whenever I feel like moving, or whenever I feel like speaking, I should first check in with my own mind, and then act steadily, with real awareness. ^5-47

Whenever I notice my mind starting to feel attached, or starting to get angry, I should stop — no action, no words. I should just stay still, like a block of wood. ^5-48

Whether my mind is being mocking, or arrogant, or filled with pride and conceit, or wanting to point out someone else's faults, or being sneaky and manipulative — ^5-49

or whenever I notice myself craving praise, wanting to put someone else down, or being harsh in a way I'll later regret — at those times too, I should just stay still, like a block of wood. ^5-50

Whenever my mind wants wealth, respect, fame, or attention and service from others — at those times too, I should just stay still, like a block of wood. ^5-51

Whenever my mind wants to ignore what others need and only chase my own interests, or feels a sudden urge to speak without thinking — at that moment too, I should just stay still, like a block of wood. ^5-52

Whenever I notice impatience, laziness, fear, careless talk, or stubbornly clinging to my own side of things — at those times too, I should just stay still, like a block of wood. ^5-53

Having checked my mind like this and found it full of disturbing emotions, or busy chasing something pointless, I should be brave and firmly apply the right remedy to settle it down. ^5-54

Full of real conviction, deep trust, steadiness, respect, and courtesy — with a healthy sense of conscience and caution, staying calm, and dedicated to bringing others joy — ^5-55

not getting discouraged by the constantly conflicting wishes of ordinary people, and understanding with compassion that it's really their own disturbing emotions causing all of that — ^5-56

acting always in ways that cause no harm, for my own sake and for others', and holding my mind lightly, almost like an illusion, without clinging to any solid sense of "me" — I should keep my mind like this, always. ^5-57

After such a long time, having finally gained this rare and precious chance, and reminding myself of that again and again, I should keep my mind as steady and unshakeable as a great mountain. ^5-58

When vultures fight over a scrap of meat, dragging it back and forth, my mind doesn't feel any need to complain. So why, mind, are you suddenly so possessive about this body of mine now? ^5-59

Mind, why are you so protective of this body, as if it belonged to you? You and this body are two completely different things — so what is it really doing for you? ^5-60

Confused mind, why don't you cherish a clean wooden statue instead? What's the point of protecting this decaying, messy machine of a body? ^5-61

Start by mentally peeling away the layer of skin. Then, using the blade of wisdom, separate the flesh from the bones underneath. ^5-62

Break apart the bones too, all the way down to the marrow inside. Then ask yourself honestly: is there anything solid or essential in here at all? ^5-63

If, after searching this carefully, you still find nothing solid there at all — why, mind, are you still clinging so tightly to this body? ^5-64

You wouldn't eat what's inside it, you wouldn't drink its blood, you wouldn't chew its insides — so what real use is this body to you? ^5-65

It would honestly make more sense for jackals and vultures to guard this body — at least they'd get a meal out of it. A human body like this is really only good for one thing: being put to use for something worthwhile. ^5-66

And even after all that protecting, when death eventually comes and takes it away — feeding it to birds and dogs — what will you do then? ^5-67

If you wouldn't keep paying a servant who's no longer any use to you, why do you keep pampering this body, when it's just going to leave you behind anyway? ^5-68

So, having given this body just enough to keep it going, put it to work for something that actually matters. Don't give it more than that when it's not doing anything truly useful. ^5-69

Think of this body simply as a vehicle to get you from one place to another. Take care of it just enough to help other beings, and let it become something genuinely useful. ^5-70

With that kind of self-control, keep a warm smile on your face. Let go of frowning and scowling, and be an open, friendly presence to everyone you meet. ^5-71

Don't slam chairs down noisily, and don't throw doors open roughly. Always find pleasure in being gentle and calm instead. ^5-72

A heron moves quietly to catch its fish. A cat moves quietly to catch its prey. In that same careful, quiet way, a practitioner should go about accomplishing what truly matters. ^5-73

When someone skilled at giving good advice offers it, even without being asked, accept it respectfully and gratefully. Always stay open to learning from everyone. ^5-74

Whenever someone says something wise, tell them, "Well said." And when I see someone doing something good, I should praise them warmly, so their joy grows even more. ^5-75

Speak well of people behind their backs. If someone's good qualities are already being talked about, agree and add to it. And if my own qualities are ever praised, I should just notice that praise for what it is, without getting carried away. ^5-76

Everything we do, we do hoping for some real satisfaction — and that kind of satisfaction is rare, something money could never buy. So I should find that same joy simply by appreciating the good qualities I see in others. ^5-77

This way, I lose nothing here and now, and I gain great happiness later. Being critical and jealous, on the other hand, brings unhappiness now, and even more suffering down the road. ^5-78

When I speak, I should speak in a way that's warm and easy to connect with — clear, free of hidden agendas or hostility, and gentle and fitting for the moment. ^5-79

When I look at people, I should look at them directly and warmly, thinking, "It's through beings just like this one that I'll be able to reach enlightenment." ^5-80

Whether I'm acting out of genuine devotion, or simply working to counter my own bad habits, cultivating good qualities and helping others — especially those who are suffering — always brings great goodness. ^5-81

Being capable and full of energy, I should always take on my tasks myself, rather than leaving them for someone else to do. ^5-82

Practice generosity and the other perfections, each one building on the last. Don't give up something more important for the sake of something smaller. Always think first about what truly helps others. ^5-83

Understanding all this, I should always stay dedicated to helping others. Even things normally not allowed become permitted for someone acting out of real compassion and wisdom, when it truly helps. ^5-84

Share your food with those who have gone astray, those with no one to protect them, and those living a disciplined spiritual life. Eat only what you need, and give away everything else except what you truly need to keep going. ^5-85

Don't harm this body of yours — which is here to practice the true teaching — for the sake of small, unimportant things. Do this, and you'll be able to fulfill beings' wishes all the sooner. ^5-86

Don't give this body away unless your compassion is genuinely pure. But when it is, give it — in this life and every other — toward something that truly matters. ^5-87

Don't teach the true teaching to someone who isn't showing basic respect — for example, someone with their head wrapped like they're sick, or holding an umbrella, a stick, or a weapon, or with their head covered. ^5-88

Don't teach the deep, expansive teachings to someone not yet ready for them. Don't teach them to women when no men are present. *(This reflects monastic rules from the author's time. Many Buddhist communities today no longer apply it.)* Approach both simple and profound teachings with equal respect, and practice both fully. ^5-89

Don't hold back someone capable of the deeper teachings by only giving them something simpler. Don't cast aside good conduct, and don't try to attract people just by dazzling them with scriptures and mantras. ^5-90

When you throw away a used toothpick or spit, cover it up afterward. And it's considered disrespectful to relieve yourself in water, on a path, or anywhere people commonly use. ^5-91

Don't eat with your mouth stuffed full, or noisily, or wide open. Don't sit with your legs carelessly stretched out, and don't fidget by rubbing your hands together. ^5-92

Avoid situations that could easily be misunderstood, like traveling or sitting alone with someone in a way that could damage trust. Pay attention to anything that might cause people to lose faith in the teaching, and steer clear of it. ^5-93

Don't point rudely with just one finger. Instead, out of respect, always show the way, and gesture, using your whole right hand, open and relaxed. ^5-94

Don't wave your arms wildly to get someone's attention — a small gesture and a small sound are enough. Anything more just comes across as undisciplined. ^5-95

Sleep the way the Buddha did when he passed into final peace — lying comfortably, but with a clear intention, right from the start, to wake up quickly and stay aware. ^5-96

Since a bodhisattva's way of life is said to be truly limitless, this training of the mind should also be something I practice without any limit. ^5-97

Three times during the day and three times at night, recite the confession of downfalls. By relying on the Buddhas and on bodhichitta, calm whatever mistakes remain. ^5-98

Whatever situation you find yourself in, whether it concerns you or someone else, make the effort to apply whatever training fits that moment. ^5-99

There's really nothing a bodhisattva doesn't need to train in. For someone wise who lives this way, absolutely everything becomes a source of goodness. ^5-100

Whether directly or indirectly, do nothing except what actually benefits others. And for the sake of every being, dedicate everything you do toward enlightenment. ^5-101

Never give up a good spiritual friend — someone wise in the deeper teachings and committed to living as a bodhisattva — even if it were to cost you your own life. ^5-102

Learn how to rely properly on a teacher, following the example set in a classic story about spiritual friendship. And read the sutras to learn this, along with everything else the Buddha taught. ^5-103

The trainings can all be found within the sutras themselves, so I should read them. And the very first one I should look at is the sutra on clearing away hidden wrongdoing. ^5-104

Since it explains, in real detail, exactly what to practice all the time, I should also look, again and again, at the Compendium of Trainings. ^5-105

Or, at the very least, I should look at the shorter Compendium of Sutras. And I should also make the effort to study the other text composed by the great teacher Nagarjuna. ^5-106

Whatever these texts don't forbid, I'm free to practice. And in order to protect people's trust and faith, I should act properly, once I've truly understood what the training requires. ^5-107

Checking in again and again on the state of my own body and mind — that, in short, is really what it means to protect awareness. ^5-108

All of this needs to be actually put into practice, not just talked about. What good does reciting words alone really do? Does simply reading a medical textbook ever cure a sick person? ^5-109

*Thus ends Chapter Five: "Guarding Awareness."* ^5-a

## Chapter 6: Patience

Whatever generosity, offerings to the Buddhas, and good deeds have been gathered over a thousand lifetimes — a single flash of anger can destroy it all. ^6-1

There's no wrongdoing like anger, and no discipline like patience. So I should meditate on patience with real effort, in every way I can. ^6-2

Someone holding onto the pain of anger never experiences peace of mind. They find no joy or happiness, they can't sleep, and they become unsettled. ^6-3

Even people who depend on someone for their wealth and favor will turn and try to kill that very person, once anger takes hold. ^6-4

Because of anger, friends grow tired of us; even when we're generous, they don't want to stay close. In short, there's no such thing as living happily while angry. ^6-5

The enemy called anger brings on this kind of suffering. But whoever works hard to defeat anger finds happiness both now and later. ^6-6

When something unwanted happens, or something we want gets blocked, that becomes food for our unhappy mind, and anger grows strong and destroys us. ^6-7

So I need to destroy the very food this enemy feeds on. This enemy has no other job except to harm me. ^6-8

No matter what happens to me, I shouldn't let it disturb my happy mind. Being upset about it doesn't get me what I wanted anyway — it just wears away my goodness. ^6-9

If something can be fixed, why be upset about it? And if it can't be fixed, what good does being upset do? ^6-10

We don't want suffering, insult, harsh words, or unpleasant talk aimed at ourselves or our friends — but somehow we're fine with the opposite happening to our enemies. ^6-11

The causes of happiness appear only occasionally; the causes of suffering are extremely common. So, my mind, hold steady — without suffering, there's no way to find true freedom. ^6-12

Devotees of harsh spiritual practices willingly endure being burned or cut, for no real reward at all. So why am I, aiming for true freedom, held back by mere cowardice? ^6-13

There's nothing that doesn't get easier with practice. So, by getting used to small pains first, I should learn to bear even great harm. ^6-14

Don't we already see this happening — with the pain of snakebites, flies, hunger, thirst, and things like skin rashes, which we bear all the time, for no particular reward? ^6-15

I shouldn't be overly sensitive to heat, cold, rain, wind, sickness, being restrained, or being struck. Acting oversensitive like that only makes the harm worse. ^6-16

Some people, seeing their own blood, become even braver and stronger. Others, seeing someone else's blood, faint and collapse. ^6-17

This difference comes from the state of one's mind — whether it's steady, or simply unused to that kind of thing. So I should look past minor harms and stay unshaken by suffering. ^6-18

Even when a wise person experiences suffering, they don't let their clear mind become clouded. Fighting with our own disturbing emotions naturally brings hardship along the way. ^6-19

The truly brave ones are those who overlook every kind of suffering and defeat enemies like anger. Everyone else is just finishing off something already dead. ^6-20

Suffering also has some good qualities: it wears away pride through humility, it gives rise to compassion for those caught in the cycle of life, it makes us shy away from wrongdoing, and it makes us take joy in doing good. ^6-21

We don't get angry at the great sources of suffering, like bodily imbalances and illness — so why do we get angry at people, who also have minds of their own? They're all driven by conditions too. ^6-22

Just as sickness arises even though we don't want it, disturbing emotions arise forcefully in us even though we don't want them either. ^6-23

People get angry without ever deciding, "I will be angry now." Anger just arises on its own, without ever being planned that way. ^6-24

All the various faults and wrongdoings that exist come about through the force of conditions — they don't happen through some free, independent choice. ^6-25

The collection of conditions that gives rise to anger doesn't think, "I will produce this." And what gets produced doesn't think, "I am being produced," either. ^6-26

Whatever people think of as a fundamental "self," or an independently existing "I," never actually arises through some deliberate intention to arise. ^6-27

If that self didn't exist before being born, then whatever desire it has to come into being would have to be fixed on some object forever — and it could never actually stop. ^6-28

And if this "self" is supposed to be permanent, then clearly it can't do anything at all, like the sky itself. Even coming into contact with other conditions, being unchanging, what could it possibly still do? ^6-29

And if that self stays exactly the same, even while acting, then what is the "acting" actually doing? What real connection could there be between "the self" and "the self's action"? ^6-30

In this way, everything is controlled by other things; nothing has power over itself alone. Understanding this, I won't get angry at anything, since all things are more like illusions, with no one solid really behind them. ^6-31

Someone might object: "Then who is there to be angry at, and who could reasonably remove that anger?" But it's not unreasonable to want to rely on patience anyway, in order to stop suffering. ^6-32

So, whether it's an enemy or a friend, when I see someone act wrongly, I should simply think, "This came about because of certain conditions," and stay calm about it. ^6-33

If things happened just because we wanted them to, no one would ever suffer, since no living being actually wants to suffer. ^6-34

Yet out of carelessness, people harm even themselves with thorns and the like. And driven by desire, some go so far as to starve themselves just to get what they want. ^6-35

Some people even jump off cliffs, harm themselves in reckless ways, eat poison or spoiled food, or act in unwholesome ways that hurt only themselves. ^6-36

When people, overpowered by disturbing emotions, even go so far as to harm the very self they cherish most, how could they possibly avoid causing harm to others too? ^6-37

Since disturbing emotions arise and drive people even to the point of harming themselves, how could I not feel compassion for them, instead of anger? ^6-38

If it's simply the nature of confused people to harm others, then getting angry at them makes no more sense than getting angry at fire for having the nature to burn. ^6-39

And if the fault is only a passing flaw, while beings are naturally gentle at heart, then getting angry still doesn't make sense — like getting angry at smoke for briefly filling an otherwise clear sky. ^6-40

If I get angry directly at the stick that hits me, rather than at the person who swung it, it would make more sense to get angry at that person's anger itself, since the anger is what's really driving the stick. ^6-41

In the past, I myself have caused this kind of harm to other beings. So it makes sense that beings who harm me now are simply the natural result of that. ^6-42

Both the enemy's weapon and my own body are causes of this suffering. The enemy brought the weapon, and I brought the body — so really, who deserves my anger here? ^6-43

If, out of pure craving and confusion, I grab onto a painful, infected wound shaped like a human body, and then it hurts when touched, who is really there to blame for that pain? ^6-44

Confused people don't want suffering, yet they crave the very things that cause it. So when they end up harmed by their own mistakes, why should I blame them for it? ^6-45

The guardians of hell and the forest of sword-like leaves, for example, are things that arise from one's own past actions. In the same way, who is really there to be angry at, when what's causing me harm is simply my own actions ripening? ^6-46

Driven by my own past actions, those who harm me now have appeared. And if this leads them into the hell realms, wouldn't I actually be the one responsible for their ruin? ^6-47

By depending on them, and by practicing patience, my own faults are worn away. Meanwhile, by depending on me, they end up experiencing prolonged suffering in the hell realms instead. ^6-48

In truth, I am the one harming them, while they're actually helping me. So why, unruly mind, do you get this so completely backwards and grow angry? ^6-49

If I actually had the good quality of a truly compassionate mind, I wouldn't end up in the hell realms myself. But if all I do is protect myself from anger, what benefit does that bring to them? ^6-50

And if I retaliate and harm them in return, then not only will I fail to protect them from a bad rebirth, my own practice of patience will fall apart too. ^6-51

The mind itself has no physical body, so it can never actually be destroyed by anyone. It's only because of our strong attachment to the body that the body ends up suffering harm. ^6-52

Since insults, harsh words, and unpleasant talk don't actually harm the body at all, why, mind, do you get so furious about them? ^6-53

If other people's dislike of me doesn't actually consume me — not in this life, and not in future ones either — then why would I refuse to accept it calmly? ^6-54

If I refuse to accept it simply because it gets in the way of some material gain, then really I'm throwing away my future good in exchange for keeping my present faults firmly in place. ^6-55

It would be better to die today than to live a long life the wrong way; even if someone like me lives a very long life, the suffering of death eventually comes anyway. ^6-56

Consider someone who enjoys a hundred years of happiness in a dream and then wakes up, compared to someone who enjoys just a moment of happiness and then wakes up. ^6-57

For both of them, once they wake up, that happiness never returns. In the same way, whether life is long or short, when death finally comes, it feels exactly the same. ^6-58

Even after gaining great wealth and enjoying happiness for a very long time, in the end we leave empty-handed, stripped bare, as if robbed by a thief. ^6-59

Someone might say: "If I survive because of the wealth I gain, I can use it to reduce wrongdoing and build merit." But if I get angry just to gain that wealth, doesn't that wear away my merit and add to my wrongdoing instead? ^6-60

If the very purpose I'm living for gets ruined anyway, then what's the point of a life spent doing nothing but wrongdoing? ^6-61

If I get angry at someone speaking badly about me because it might turn people away from goodness, then why don't I get equally angry when that same person speaks badly about someone else? ^6-62

Since others' lack of faith depends on them, and you already tolerate that patiently, why can't you tolerate unpleasant talk the same way, since disturbing emotions also arise naturally, simply from conditions? ^6-63

Even when people mock or damage sacred images, monuments, and the true teaching, it doesn't really make sense for me to get angry, because the Buddhas themselves can never actually be harmed by that. ^6-64

Even toward those who harm my teachers, relatives, and friends, I should recognize, as before, that it comes about through conditions, and let go of anger. ^6-65

Since living beings cause harm either through conscious intention or without it, it doesn't make sense to single out only those who act with intention — I should simply learn to bear the harm either way. ^6-66

Some act wrongly out of confusion, and some, out of that same confusion, get angry at them for it. Who among them can really be called faultless, and who can really be blamed? ^6-67

Why did I, in the past, ever act in a way that caused others to harm me now? Since everything depends on past actions, why should I hold a grudge about it now? ^6-68

Seeing things this way, I should make every effort to develop a mind of loving-kindness toward everyone, and dedicate myself wholeheartedly to building good qualities. ^6-69

For example, when a house catches fire and it starts to spread to another house, it makes sense to immediately clear away anything flammable nearby, like dry straw. ^6-70

In the same way, whatever I'm attached to that lets the fire of anger spread, I should let go of it right away, out of fear that it will burn away my own goodness. ^6-71

If a man about to be executed instead only loses a hand and is set free, isn't that a good outcome? And if I'm freed from the sufferings of hell through some human suffering now, isn't that a good outcome too? ^6-72

If I can't even bear this small amount of suffering right now, then why don't I get rid of anger, which is the real cause of the far greater suffering of hell? ^6-73

For the sake of trivial desires, I have already experienced being burned and worse, thousands of times over, in hell. Yet through none of that suffering did I ever accomplish anything meaningful, for myself or for anyone else. ^6-74

This present suffering isn't nearly as bad, and it can accomplish something truly great. So I should feel nothing but joy at suffering that removes harm from other beings. ^6-75

If other people feel joy when they're praised for their good qualities, why, my own mind, don't you feel that same kind of joy when someone else is praised? ^6-76

This joy of yours would be a genuine source of happiness, entirely free of fault. It's something praised by people with good qualities, and it's also the very best way to draw others toward you. ^6-77

On the other hand, if you don't want that same happiness for others, then—since you'd also refuse to reward good service—you'll end up losing out, both in this life and in future ones. ^6-78

When you talk about your own good qualities, you want others to feel happy for you. But when someone else talks about their good qualities, you refuse to feel happy for them. ^6-79

Having generated the wish for enlightenment for the sake of every being's happiness, why would you get angry when beings actually find happiness on their own? ^6-80

If you claim you want all beings to become enlightened and honored throughout the world, then why does it trouble you so much just to see them receive a little ordinary respect and support right now? ^6-81

If someone you're responsible for supporting instead gets support from their own relatives, and you get upset instead of happy about it, who's really raising them properly? ^6-82

If you don't even want that small good thing to happen for them, then how could you possibly want them to reach enlightenment? Someone who gets angry when others gain something clearly doesn't really hold the wish for enlightenment at all. ^6-83

Whether that gift came from you or was already sitting in a generous donor's home, in both cases, it was never really yours to begin with — so what does it matter whether it's given away or not? ^6-84

Why would you throw away your own goodness, faith, and good qualities? Why wouldn't you simply let go of claiming what was never yours to keep, instead of getting angry about it? ^6-85

Not only do you feel no remorse for wrongdoing you commit yourself, you even seem to want to compete with others who have done good deeds! ^6-86

Even if an enemy is unhappy, what good does that actually do you? Your mere wish for it won't actually cause them any harm. ^6-87

Even if your wish for their suffering came true, what good would that really do you? If you say it would satisfy you, what could possibly be worse than that kind of satisfaction? ^6-88

This hook, cast by the fisherman of disturbing emotions, is truly unbearably sharp. Once caught by it, I'll be thrown into the cauldrons of hell and cooked by its guardians. ^6-89

Being honored with praise and fame brings no real merit and no real strength; it doesn't cure sickness, and it doesn't bring real physical comfort either. ^6-90

If I actually understood what's truly good for me, what real benefit would fame bring? If all I want is a happy mind, then I'd have to rely on things like drink and distraction instead. ^6-91

For the sake of fame, people lose their wealth and even risk their own lives. What real good do words do? Once you're dead, who is even left to enjoy that fame? ^6-92

When children's sandcastles collapse, they cry in despair. In the same way, when my praise and reputation collapse, my own mind acts just as childish. ^6-93

Since a mere sound has no mind of its own, it can't possibly intend to praise me. Whatever true joy exists is simply because someone else feels genuinely happy about me — that's the real cause for joy. ^6-94

Whether it's someone else's joy, or even my own, what good does someone else's happiness actually do for me? That joy belongs entirely to that person alone — I don't get any share of it. ^6-95

If I were happy simply because someone else is happy, then I should feel that way about everyone's happiness equally. So why aren't I happy when someone finds happiness through liking someone else instead of me? ^6-96

So this joy that arises just from thinking, "I am being praised," doesn't actually make logical sense at all — it's really nothing more than childish behavior. ^6-97

Praise and the like just distract me, and wear away my sense of urgency in practice. They breed envy toward those with good qualities, and they can even destroy real excellence. ^6-98

So aren't those who work to undermine my praise and reputation actually helping to protect me from a bad rebirth? ^6-99

I'm seeking real freedom, and I don't need to be bound up by wealth and honor. So how could I possibly be angry at those who are actually freeing me from those very chains? ^6-100

I want to throw myself into suffering, yet, as if blessed by the Buddha himself, some door remains firmly shut against that. So how could I possibly be angry at whoever is doing that for me? ^6-101

Someone might say, "This person is blocking my chance to build merit," and think it's reasonable to be angry at them for that. But there's no hard practice equal to patience — so shouldn't I actually settle right into practicing it instead? ^6-102

If it's really my own fault that I fail to be patient with this person, then it is I myself who am blocking the very cause of merit sitting right in front of me. ^6-103

If one thing cannot arise without another, and it does arise whenever that other thing is present, then that other thing is truly its cause — so how could it possibly be called an obstacle instead? ^6-104

A beggar who arrives at just the right time isn't really an obstacle to generosity. And someone who helps a person become a monk shouldn't be called an obstacle to ordination either. ^6-105

In this world, beggars are common, but people who actually cause us harm are rare. Because if I never wronged others in the past, then truly, no one would ever wrong me either. ^6-106

So, like an unearned treasure suddenly appearing in one's own house, without any effort at all, I should feel genuine joy toward my enemy, since they've become an ally in my own spiritual practice. ^6-107

Since this good result was accomplished together, by both of us, the fruit of my patience should rightly be offered to my enemy first, since they are truly the cause of my patience. ^6-108

Someone might say, "But my enemy had no intention of helping me develop patience, so they don't deserve to be honored for it." But then, why do we honor the true teaching itself, even though it too is simply a fitting cause, without any intention behind it? ^6-109

Someone might say, "But my enemy actually intends to harm me, so they shouldn't be honored." But if my enemy instead tried to help me, the way a doctor does, how would I ever have the chance to develop patience at all? ^6-110

So it's precisely because of a mind full of intense hostility that patience is able to arise in the first place. That's exactly why my enemy is the true cause of my patience, and deserves to be honored just like the sacred teaching itself. ^6-111

That's why the Buddha himself said there are two kinds of "fields" in which goodness grows: the field of sentient beings, and the field of the Buddhas. Many people, by pleasing sentient beings in this way, have reached the very height of accomplishment. ^6-112

Since Buddhahood is attained equally through sentient beings and through the Buddhas, why is it that we show the Buddhas such respect, but not sentient beings, when the process depends equally on both? ^6-113

The quality of an intention isn't something inherent by itself — it's known by its results. In that same way, sentient beings also share in this quality, and so deserve to be regarded as equal to the Buddhas in this respect. ^6-114

Whatever offering is made to those with a loving mind is itself due to the greatness of sentient beings. And whatever merit comes from faith in the Buddha is due to the greatness of the Buddha. ^6-115

Since sentient beings also share, in part, in the process of accomplishing Buddhahood, they deserve to be considered equal in that respect — though truly, nothing is fully equal to the Buddhas, whose good qualities are like a boundless ocean. ^6-116

Even if just a small portion of that single, supreme collection of good qualities appeared in someone, it would be worth more to make offerings to that one being than to offer up the three worlds themselves. ^6-117

Since sentient beings share in what gives rise to the Buddha's supreme qualities, on that basis alone, it makes sense to treat them as worthy of real honor. ^6-118

What's more, other than pleasing sentient beings, how else could anyone possibly repay those genuine, unwavering friends who bring us such immeasurable benefit? ^6-119

Since it's by giving up their very own bodies and entering the depths of the worst suffering that the Buddhas repay that kindness, then even if sentient beings cause me great harm, I should still treat them, in every way, as something truly precious. ^6-120

My own teacher and guide doesn't even spare his own body for the sake of these beings — so how, out of sheer confusion, could I possibly look down on them as if they were mere servants? ^6-121

When beings are happy, the Buddhas themselves rejoice; when beings are harmed, the Buddhas feel sorrow. So pleasing beings brings joy to all the Buddhas, and harming them brings sorrow to the Buddhas as well. ^6-122

Just as no amount of pleasure could ever satisfy someone whose whole body is engulfed in flames, there's no way to please the greatly compassionate ones while beings are being harmed. ^6-123

So, for whatever harm I have caused to beings — which has displeased all the greatly compassionate ones — I confess that wrongdoing today, one by one, and I ask the Buddhas' forgiveness for whatever has displeased them. ^6-124

From now on, in order to please the Buddhas, I will become a servant to the world. Let crowds trample over my head, or even kill me — I will never respond with anger. May the Guardian of the World always be pleased. ^6-125

There's no doubt at all that these compassionate ones regard every being in this world as their very own self. So how could I possibly fail to show respect to beings, when to see them is to see the Guardian himself? ^6-126

This alone is what truly pleases the Buddhas; this alone is what truly accomplishes my own good; this alone is what removes the suffering of the world. So I should always devote myself to this practice. ^6-127

For example, though a single official might cause harm to many people, far-sighted citizens don't retaliate against him even if they could, ^6-128

because he isn't acting alone — the power of the king stands behind him. In the same way, I should never look down on or harm even a weak person who causes me harm. ^6-129

In the very same way, the guardians of hell, and all those endowed with great compassion, stand behind every being. So, just as ordinary people appease a fierce king, I should work to please every being. ^6-130

Even if a king became truly furious, could he inflict on me the kind of suffering I'd experience in hell, simply for having displeased sentient beings? ^6-131

And even if a king were greatly pleased with me, could he ever give me what I'd gain simply by pleasing sentient beings — Buddhahood itself? Certainly not. ^6-132

Never mind the future Buddhahood that comes from pleasing beings — don't we already see, right in this very life, that it brings great glory, fame, and happiness? ^6-133

Through patience, one gains beauty and more while still wandering through this life, along with good health, a good reputation, an extremely long life, and the vast happiness of a great and rightful ruler. ^6-134

*Thus ends Chapter Six: "Patience."* ^6-a

## Chapter 7: Diligence

Having built patience like this, I should now turn to real effort — diligence. Enlightenment rests on this kind of effort, the way nothing moves without wind. Without effort, goodness simply doesn't happen. ^7-1

Diligence means genuinely enjoying doing good. Its opposites are laziness — clinging to comfort and bad habits — feeling discouraged, and putting yourself down. ^7-2

Laziness comes from enjoying comfort and idleness too much, craving sleep, and not really feeling any urgency about the suffering built into this cycle of life. ^7-3

Caught in the net of disturbing emotions, trapped in the cycle of rebirth, and already walking straight toward death — how can anyone still not see this clearly? ^7-4

Haven't you seen people just like you dying, one after another? And yet you still sleep on, unconcerned, like a water buffalo grazing without a care. ^7-5

With every escape route closed off, and death watching your every move, how can you still enjoy your meals, and enjoy your sleep? ^7-6

Since death can come quickly, I should build up as much goodness as I can while there's still time. If I only give up laziness once death has already arrived, what good will that do — it'll already be too late. ^7-7

Thinking, "I still haven't done that," or "I've only half-finished this," death suddenly shows up, and all I can think is, "Oh no — it's over." ^7-8

With my eyes swollen and red from grief, tears streaming down, watching the hope drain from the faces of the people who love me, I will find myself staring straight at the messengers of death. ^7-9

Tormented by memories of my own wrongdoing, terrified at the mere sound of hell, and losing control of myself out of sheer panic — what will I even do in that state? ^7-10

If just the ordinary fears of this life already make you feel like a fish gasping on dry land, how much more should you fear the truly unbearable suffering of hell that comes from wrongdoing? ^7-11

Even hot water is hard for this soft body to bear. So, having already created the causes for the far greater burning of hell, how can I still feel this relaxed right now? ^7-12

Wanting good results without any real effort, being overly sensitive to hardship, acting as if you'll live forever, even while death already has you firmly in its grip — you're setting yourself up to be crushed by suffering. ^7-13

Relying on this precious human life — like a boat — I can cross the great river of suffering. Since a chance like this is so hard to find again, don't be foolish enough to fall asleep now, of all times. ^7-14

Why would you give up the greatest joy of all — delight in genuine, meaningful practice — in exchange for empty distractions and the very causes of suffering, like idle gossip and restlessness? ^7-15

Stay free of discouragement, build up your inner strength, take real charge of yourself, treat yourself and others as equally important, and be willing, when it counts, to put others before yourself. ^7-16

Don't get discouraged thinking, "How could someone like me possibly reach enlightenment?" Because the Buddha himself, who never speaks anything but the truth, has declared this: ^7-17

Even tiny creatures like gnats, flies, bees, and worms can attain full enlightenment, simply by generating real effort and diligence. ^7-18

So then, born as I am into the human race, capable of telling what helps from what harms, why wouldn't I be able to reach enlightenment too — as long as I never give up this way of life? ^7-19

Someone might say, "But I'm afraid I might have to give up my hands or feet." That fear only comes from confusion — from failing to weigh what really matters against what doesn't. ^7-20

For countless ages already, I have been cut apart, burned, and torn to pieces, over and over — yet without ever attaining enlightenment through any of it, since I wasn't practicing this way. ^7-21

But the hardship involved in truly working toward enlightenment has a clear limit — like the brief pain of lancing a wound in order to clear out a much worse infection underneath. ^7-22

Every doctor cures illness through methods that are uncomfortable at first. In the same way, to overcome much greater suffering later, I should be willing to bear a little discomfort now. ^7-23

But the Buddha, our supreme guide, doesn't rely on such harsh methods at all. Through the gentlest possible means, he heals even the deepest, most serious afflictions. ^7-24

The Buddha starts us off by having us practice giving away small, simple things, like vegetables. Once we're comfortable with that, we're gradually able to give away even more, bit by bit. ^7-25

Once we truly understand that our own body isn't something to cling to any more than a vegetable is, then what real difficulty is there in giving even that away? ^7-26

Once wrongdoing is given up, suffering fades. Once real understanding grows, unhappiness fades too. It's really our confused thinking and our wrongdoing that harm both mind and body. ^7-27

Through building goodness, the body becomes comfortable; through wisdom, the mind becomes at ease. So even while staying in this world for the sake of helping others, why would someone truly compassionate ever feel discouraged about it? ^7-28

Through the power of bodhichitta, past wrongdoing is worn away, and vast oceans of goodness are gathered instead — that's why this path is said to be even more excellent than others. ^7-29

So, riding the horse of bodhichitta, which removes all weariness, and moving happily from one joy to the next — what wise person would ever feel discouraged on a journey like that? ^7-30

The forces needed to truly help beings are: strong aspiration, steadfastness, joy in the work, and the willingness to step back and rest when needed. Aspiration itself grows by reflecting on the dangers of an unexamined life, and on the real benefits of this path. ^7-31

Having let go of everything that gets in the way, I should work to increase my effort using aspiration, healthy self-confidence, joy, and the willingness to rest, along with genuine dedication and self-command. ^7-32

I still have countless faults to overcome, both my own and others'. And for even a single fault, it could take an ocean of lifetimes just to fully work through it. ^7-33

If I can't even see myself making progress on a fraction of my own faults, why doesn't my heart break at the thought of just how much suffering still lies ahead? ^7-34

I still have many good qualities left to develop, both my own and others'. And for each one, it might take an ocean of lifetimes to truly master it. ^7-35

And yet I haven't even become familiar with a fraction of these good qualities. It's honestly astonishing how much of this precious life I've wasted, on nothing at all. ^7-36

I haven't made real offerings to the Buddha. I haven't brought joy to others in a meaningful way. I haven't done my part for the true teachings, or truly helped those in need. ^7-37

I haven't given comfort to the fearful, or real relief to the suffering. All I've really managed to do is cause pain — even to my own mother, starting from the moment I was in her womb. ^7-38

Because I've lacked real interest in genuine practice, both in the past and even now, I've ended up in this state of spiritual poverty. Who in their right mind would ever give up something so valuable? ^7-39

The Buddha taught that real interest and enthusiasm is the root of every kind of goodness. And that interest, in turn, grows from constantly reflecting on how our actions bring about real consequences. ^7-40

Suffering, unhappiness, every kind of fear, and losing the things we want — all of it comes about because of wrongdoing. ^7-41

By doing good with a truly wholehearted mind, wherever that person is reborn, they'll be met there with the good results of that very goodness. ^7-42

But someone who does wrong, even while still hoping for happiness, will end up struck down, wherever they're reborn, by the very suffering their own wrongdoing brings about. ^7-43

Through the power of goodness, one can be born inside a vast, fragrant, cooling lotus flower, nourished there by the Buddha's beautiful words like food. Growing radiant as the flower opens under the Buddha's light, one emerges with an extraordinary new body, becoming a true child of the Buddha, standing right in his presence. ^7-44

But through wrongdoing, the opposite happens: one is helplessly skinned alive, doused in molten metal boiled by raging fire, cut into pieces by blazing swords and spears, and left to fall onto a burning iron ground — all because of countless unwholesome deeds. ^7-45

So I should build real enthusiasm for doing good, and train myself to hold it in genuine respect. And I should cultivate a healthy, steady confidence in my own ability to actually follow through with this practice. ^7-46

First, I should honestly check whether I'm really ready for something, and only then decide whether to start or not. It's better not to begin at all than to start something and quit halfway. ^7-47

If I get into the habit of quitting halfway through, that pattern carries into future situations too, and only causes more trouble and suffering. And even the task I quit doesn't get properly finished either. ^7-48

I need self-confidence about three things: my actions, the disturbing emotions in me, and my own ability. When I think, "I alone should do this," that's self-confidence in action. ^7-49

This world, so often controlled by disturbing emotions, isn't really able to accomplish its own deeper good. Since others may not be able to do what I can, I should be the one to step up and take this on. ^7-50

When even others are willing to take on humbler tasks, how could I possibly sit back and do nothing? I don't say this out of arrogance — in fact, having no arrogance at all is exactly the point. ^7-51

When a snake is already dead, even a crow can peck at it like a mighty eagle would. In the same way, if I let myself feel weak and defeated, even small setbacks will end up hurting me. ^7-52

Is there really any way out of hardship for someone who simply gives up and stops trying? But by building real, steady confidence in one's own effort, even great difficulties become workable. ^7-53

So, with a steady and determined mind, I should work through any setbacks or mistakes. If I let myself be defeated by them instead, then my hope of overcoming real challenges becomes laughable. ^7-54

I should aim to rise above every challenge, and never let myself be defeated by one. As someone walking the Buddha's path, I should hold onto exactly this kind of steady confidence. ^7-55

People overcome by arrogance aren't the ones with real confidence at all — that kind of pride is just another disturbing emotion in disguise. Genuinely confident people are never ruled by an enemy; it's the arrogant who end up ruled by the very enemy of their own pride. ^7-56

Puffed up with this kind of unhealthy pride, a person gets led by it straight into a bad rebirth; robbed of any real, lasting joy in this life, becoming, in a sense, dependent on scraps from others. ^7-57

Foolish, unappealing, and weak — that's how such a person ends up seen everywhere they go. Even someone with real spiritual discipline, if puffed up with unhealthy pride, falls into this same trap — so imagine how much worse it is for someone with even less going for them. ^7-58

Whoever uses real, healthy confidence to overcome unhealthy arrogance is truly confident, and a genuine hero. And whoever fully overcomes that enemy of arrogance, even when it tries to creep back in, brings about a genuine victory — one that truly benefits others as much as themselves. ^7-59

Living right in the middle of disturbing emotions, I should stand firm against them in every way I can, like a lion standing unbothered among jackals — never letting the whole pack of disturbing emotions get the better of me. ^7-60

Just as we instinctively protect our eyes when something dangerous comes close, I should protect myself just as carefully from ever falling under the control of disturbing emotions, even in difficult situations. ^7-61

Just as someone throws themselves into a game they love, fully absorbed and wanting more, I should approach my own tasks with that same kind of genuine enthusiasm, never feeling like I've had enough. ^7-62

We work so hard hoping for happiness, though there's never any guarantee we'll find it. But if the work itself is truly the cause of real happiness, why wouldn't doing it bring joy in itself? ^7-63

If people are never satisfied chasing after pleasures that are really like honey smeared on the edge of a blade, why would I ever grow tired of doing good, whose result is nothing but real, peaceful happiness? ^7-64

So, in order to see any task through to the end, I should throw myself into it fully — like an elephant, worn out by the midday heat, joyfully diving into a cool lake. ^7-65

But if my strength runs low partway through, it's fine to pause, planning to return to it later. And once something is genuinely finished, I should set it aside and move on to what matters next. ^7-66

Like a seasoned warrior meeting an old enemy on the battlefield, I should skillfully dodge the "weapons" of disturbing emotions, while staying ready to strike back and overcome them myself. ^7-67

Just as a warrior who drops his sword in battle rushes to grab it back out of fear, I should just as quickly recover my mindfulness if it slips, by remembering the real dangers of losing my way. ^7-68

Just as poison spreads through the whole body once it enters the bloodstream, wrongdoing can spread through the whole mind the moment it finds even a small opening. ^7-69

Just as someone carrying a brimming bowl of oil, with an armed guard right behind threatening to kill them if they spill a drop, would stay intensely focused — a serious practitioner should hold that same kind of careful attention. ^7-70

So, just as I'd jump up immediately if a snake landed in my lap, I should push away sleepiness and laziness with that same urgency, the instant I notice them. ^7-71

Whenever I make a mistake, I should take time to really reflect on it, and firmly resolve: "I'm going to make sure this doesn't happen again." ^7-72

Thinking, "How can I make mindfulness a real, lasting habit in every situation?" I should actively seek out the right circumstances, and the right company, to help make that happen. ^7-73

Before taking on any task, I should remind myself of everything I've learned about being careful and mindful, and get myself properly prepared and energized first. ^7-74

Just as a wisp of cotton drifts wherever the wind carries it, I should let myself be carried along by real enthusiasm and joy. That's exactly how things actually get accomplished. ^7-75

*Thus ends Chapter Seven: "Diligence."* ^7-a

## Chapter 8: Meditative Concentration

Having built up real effort like this, I should now settle my mind into deep concentration. Someone whose mind is scattered lives constantly caught between the jaws of disturbing emotions. ^8-1

Through physical and mental solitude, distraction just doesn't come up. So I should step back from the busyness of ordinary life, and let go of unnecessary, scattered thinking. ^8-2

The reason I don't step back from busy, ordinary life is attachment — craving for gain and the like. So, to truly let go of that, a wise person should reflect carefully, like this: ^8-3

Knowing that deep insight, combined with a calm, settled mind, is what truly overcomes disturbing emotions, I should first work on developing that calm, settled mind — and that itself comes from genuinely letting go of worldly attachment. ^8-4

Why would anyone who is impermanent hold onto real attachment for someone else who is just as impermanent? Because of that attachment, you might not even see them again for a thousand lifetimes. ^8-5

Without seeing that person, there's no joy, and the mind can't settle down. And even when you do see them, you're never really satisfied — the craving just keeps burning, exactly like before. ^8-6

Being attached to other people completely clouds our ability to see things clearly. It kills any real sense of urgency about this cycle of life, and in the end, it just leads to suffering and grief. ^8-7

Simply obsessing over that one person can cause this precious life to slip away, wasted. And because of that fleeting friendship, even our lasting connection to the true teaching gets damaged. ^8-8

Following the same path as people caught up in short-sighted, worldly concerns will surely lead somewhere bad. And if they're pulling you in a completely different direction from your own, what's really the point of relying on them? ^8-9

In one moment they're a friend, and in the next, suddenly an enemy; they get angry even about things that should make them happy. Ordinary people, caught up like this, are genuinely hard to please. ^8-10

Try to say something genuinely helpful, and they might get angry at you for it — even try to talk you out of doing what's actually good. And if you don't go along with them, they get angry, and that anger can lead them straight into trouble. ^8-11

Jealous of those doing better, competitive with equals, arrogant toward those doing worse when praised, and furious when criticized — when has real good ever come from associating with someone caught in this cycle? ^8-12

Associating with people caught up like this inevitably leads to self-praise, putting others down, and endless chatter about worldly pleasures — some kind of unwholesome habit tends to follow. ^8-13

In this way, this kind of relationship really just ends up harming both people involved. It doesn't help me, and I don't end up helping them either. ^8-14

So I should keep a healthy distance from people caught up in this kind of pattern. When we do meet, I can be warm and friendly, but without becoming too close — just staying pleasant, at arm's length. ^8-15

Like a bee taking only the nectar it needs from a flower, I should take only what's genuinely useful, and stay unattached to the rest — as if meeting these situations for the very first time, without getting entangled. ^8-16

"I've got plenty of wealth and honor, and lots of people who like me" — holding onto this kind of pride is exactly what sets someone up for a terrifying shock after death. ^8-17

So, whatever a confused mind becomes attached to, it obsesses over endlessly — and along with that obsession, suffering just keeps coming back, again and again. ^8-18

That's why a wise person shouldn't get attached in the first place — because fear itself grows out of attachment. Understanding that everything naturally slips away eventually, I should stay steady, and really see this clearly. ^8-19

Even if I gain plenty of wealth, fame, and popularity, there's no telling where — if anywhere — I'll be able to carry it all when I die. ^8-20

If someone out there is criticizing me anyway, what real good does anyone else's praise do me? And if someone else is praising me anyway, what real harm does someone else's criticism do? ^8-21

Since even the Buddha himself can't please every single being, with their endlessly different preferences, what hope does someone as ordinary as me have? So I should just let go of worrying about what everyone thinks. ^8-22

People criticize those without wealth, and they speak badly even of those who have it. Given how naturally difficult it is to please anyone this way, how could real, lasting happiness ever come from worrying about it? ^8-23

Since people caught up in short-sighted self-interest can't really find joy except by serving themselves, the Buddha taught that such a person can never really be counted on as a true friend. ^8-24


[Sanskrit variant:] When pleasure arises only through self-interest, that pleasure is self-interest itself; as when property is lost, distress follows the loss of happiness. ^8-24x1
The deer, birds, and trees in the forest never speak an unkind word. When will I get the chance to simply live in that kind of peaceful company? ^8-25

Staying in a cave, an empty temple, or under a tree, when will I finally reach the point where I never look back, completely free of attachment? ^8-26

When will I finally live freely in vast, open spaces that belong to no one in particular, moving about as I please, without needing to hold onto any one place? ^8-27

Carrying just a simple bowl and a few basic things, wearing clothes no one else would even want, when will I finally live fearlessly, without needing to hide or protect this body at all? ^8-28

Visiting a charnel ground, when will I finally look at the scattered bones there and truly recognize that my own body is just as subject to decay? ^8-29

This very body of mine will eventually become something so unpleasant that even jackals won't want to come near it, put off by the smell. ^8-30

Even this one body, born all at once, ends up decaying, its own flesh and bones eventually falling apart from each other — so why even mention how much more easily other friendships fall apart? ^8-31

We're born alone, and we die alone too. Since no one else can actually share our suffering with us, what real good do clingy friendships even do? ^8-32

Just as travelers on a long journey take shelter wherever they happen to stop for the night, in the same way, beings traveling through the cycle of life take up temporary "lodging" in one life after another. ^8-33

As long as I haven't yet been carried away, mourned by others, weighed down in grief — that's exactly how much time I still have left. I should use it wisely, and head toward a life of real solitude. ^8-34

Free of both close attachments and resentments, living alone in solitude, and having already accepted, ahead of time, that I will die anyway — so that when death actually comes, there's no real grief left to feel. ^8-35

Since no one would be nearby to cause grief or distraction, nothing would pull me away from keeping the Buddha in mind and staying focused on genuine practice. ^8-36

So, in a forest full of natural beauty and calm, needing very little to sustain me there, bringing real peace and happiness, and settling every kind of distraction, I should choose to live there, on my own. ^8-37

Letting go of every other worry, and focusing only on training my own mind, I should work diligently to bring it to rest, and truly tame it. ^8-38

In this life and beyond, chasing after sensory pleasures brings nothing but trouble: in this life, it can lead to violence and imprisonment; in future lives, it leads to hell and worse. ^8-39

For the sake of these desires, people have gone to great lengths in the past — sending messages back and forth, willing to do wrong without hesitation, even risking their own reputation. ^8-40

I've thrown myself into danger, and used up everything I have, all chasing after these desires — and yet it's exactly these desires that people end up clinging to with the most joy, again and again. ^8-41

Since a body, when you really look at it, is nothing more than a skeleton — with no independence or true self at its core — why do I still cling to it so fully, refusing to move toward real, lasting peace? ^8-42

Someone who was once eagerly pursued and courted might, upon finally seeing the truth clearly, feel embarrassed enough to cover their face — whether they'd glimpsed it before or not. ^8-43

Imagine that same face, now picked clean by vultures, exactly as desire has already stripped it bare in truth. Seeing this clearly, why would I still run from facing it? ^8-44

If, even while others can see it plainly, someone still jealously guards a body as if it were theirs to keep, then why not guard it against the one who really consumes it in the end — death itself? ^8-45

Seeing that this body is really just a heap of flesh, eventually eaten by vultures and scavengers, why decorate it now with garlands and perfume, as if honoring a meal meant for someone else? ^8-46

If just seeing a still, lifeless skeleton frightens you, why aren't you even more frightened seeing that same skeleton walking around, alive, like something reanimated? ^8-47

Since both waste and saliva come from the very same food, why does one disgust you while the other doesn't? ^8-48

Someone caught up in desire, confused about what real impurity even means, won't enjoy a soft, cotton-filled pillow just because it doesn't smell nice — yet happily embraces a body that constantly does. ^8-49

If you're attached to a covered-up body, why wouldn't you also want to see it uncovered? And if there's really no need to see it that way, why cling to it so tightly even while it stays covered? ^8-50

If you're really not drawn to something impure, then why would you embrace another person at all — really just a cage of bones held together with sinew, covered in a layer of flesh and blood? ^8-51

You yourself are already made up of plenty of impurity, and you rely on that very body constantly — yet you still crave another "bag" of impurity too, driven by that same confused desire. ^8-52

If you say, "I enjoy this body, and want to touch and look at it," then how could you truly desire flesh that, by its very nature, has no mind or awareness of its own? ^8-53

The mind you're actually drawn to can never be touched or seen directly. And whatever you can touch or see has no awareness of its own. So really, what's the point of the whole embrace? ^8-54

It's not that surprising that people fail to see the impure nature of someone else's body — but it really is astonishing that they somehow fail to see that same nature in their own. ^8-55

Why, with a mind caught up in attachment to impurity, would someone ignore a fresh, dew-covered lotus opening in clear sunlight, and instead take delight in what is really just a "basket" of impurity? ^8-56

If you wouldn't want to touch ground that's been soiled, then why would you want to touch the very body that impurity actually comes from? ^8-57

If you're truly not drawn to impurity, then why would you embrace another body, which grew, quite literally, from an impure source? ^8-58

A small worm, born from impurity, doesn't appeal to you at all — yet you still desire a full human body, made up mostly of impurity, and born the very same way. ^8-59

Not only do you fail to see your own impurity clearly, you go further and crave someone else's "bag" of impurity too, driven by the very same craving. ^8-60

Even pleasant things, like fragrant camphor, rice, or vegetables, once chewed and spit back out, make even the ground beneath them seem dirty and unpleasant. ^8-61

If you still doubt that the body is really this impure, even after seeing it directly, go look at other bodies left behind in a charnel ground, and see for yourself. ^8-62

Knowing that simply peeling back the skin would reveal something deeply unsettling underneath, how is it that people still find themselves drawn back to that same body, again and again? ^8-63

Even the pleasant scent on a body is really just perfume, like sandalwood — it's not something the body itself produces. So why would anyone become attracted to a person just because of a scent that actually comes from somewhere else? ^8-64

If it's really better not to be attached to the body because of its natural odor, then why do people pointlessly burn incense on it, just trying to make it seem more appealing than it really is? ^8-65

And if that pleasant scent really is just sandalwood, what does it actually have to do with the body wearing it? So again, why be attracted to a person over a scent that isn't even theirs? ^8-66

If the body's natural, unadorned state — long hair and nails, yellowing teeth, its natural smell — is honestly something a little unsettling to look at plainly, then— ^8-67

why put so much effort into grooming and polishing it, almost like sharpening a weapon that will only harm you in the end? This kind of confused effort, spent obsessing over the body, has caused so much needless turmoil in the world. ^8-68

If seeing a bare, still skeleton in a charnel ground unsettles you, how could you take pleasure in a whole town full of these same "moving skeletons," walking around you every day? ^8-69

This kind of impure indulgence isn't even free — pursuing it brings real exhaustion in this life, and it can even lead to serious suffering in future ones. ^8-70

A child can't grow wealth on their own, so it would be nice to simply reach maturity and finally enjoy life. But if someone spends their entire life just accumulating wealth, what's even left to enjoy by the time they're old? ^8-71

Some people, driven relentlessly by desire, wear themselves out completely with a full day's work, and come home each night, collapsing into bed as if already dead. ^8-72

Some people, worn down by the hardships of traveling far from home for work, suffer through long separations — and even while longing to see their family, they sometimes never get the chance again. ^8-73

Confused into thinking it's for their own good, some people sell their very freedom for the sake of wealth — and even then, without actually gaining what they hoped for, they just end up pushed around by other people's demands. ^8-74

Some people sell their own labor and end up completely under someone else's control — and even when their wives give birth, it might happen with nowhere better to go than under a tree, out in the open. ^8-75

People fooled by desire, thinking, "I just need to make a living," willingly risk their lives in dangerous work, and even become someone else's servant, all chasing after profit. ^8-76

Some people caught up in these desires end up having their bodies mutilated; some face terrible violence; some are struck down; some are even burned — all driven, ultimately, by desire. ^8-77

Understand clearly that wealth brings endless trouble — through the effort of earning it, the anxiety of protecting it, and the grief of eventually losing it. People distracted by attachment to wealth like this never really find a way out of the suffering built into life. ^8-78

For people caught up chasing desires, there are so many drawbacks, and only the tiniest of rewards — like a cart-pulling animal that only occasionally gets a mouthful of grass along the way. ^8-79

And even that small reward isn't hard for a beast of burden to get — yet for the sake of that same tiny reward, people caught up in endless work end up wasting this rare and precious human life. ^8-80

Sense pleasures inevitably fall apart in the end, and can even lead to a bad rebirth. Since they're not even that valuable to begin with, chasing them brings constant, exhausting trouble for very little real return. ^8-81

If full enlightenment could actually be reached with just a fraction of that same effort, then someone caught up chasing desires suffers even more than someone practicing this path — yet without ever reaching enlightenment at all. ^8-82

Thinking carefully about the suffering that awaits, sense pleasures are actually worse than weapons, poison, fire, cliffs, or even enemies — nothing really compares to how much damage they can cause. ^8-83

So, having grown genuinely tired of chasing sense pleasures, I should cultivate real joy in solitude instead — in peaceful, quiet places, free of conflict and disturbing emotions. ^8-84

In a spacious, simple dwelling, cooled by moonlight and the scent of sandalwood, walking through the quiet, gently stirring forest breeze, thinking only about how best to help others. ^8-85

Living freely in an empty house, under a tree, or in a cave, for as long as I like, letting go of the exhausting burden of owning and protecting things, I can finally live with real ease, worry-free. ^8-86

Living freely, without attachment, and without being tied down to anything — this kind of contented, easy life is genuinely hard to find, even for the wealthiest and most powerful people. ^8-87

Having reflected on the benefits of solitude in these and other ways, I should calm all my scattered thinking, and turn my attention to meditating on bodhichitta. ^8-88

First, I should work hard at truly seeing myself and others as equal. Since everyone equally wants happiness and wants to avoid suffering, I should protect every being just as carefully as I protect myself. ^8-89

Although the hand and other body parts are all different, they're still protected together as parts of one single body. In the same way, even though different people each have their own happiness and suffering, we're all equally united by that same simple wish to be happy. ^8-90

Even though my own suffering doesn't harm anyone else's body, it still feels completely unbearable to me — simply because I identify so strongly with myself. ^8-91

In the same way, even though someone else's suffering doesn't directly affect me, it should feel just as hard to bear, once I really learn to identify with them the same way I do with myself. ^8-92

I should work to relieve others' suffering, simply because it is suffering, exactly like my own. And I should work to help others, simply because they too are living beings, exactly like I am. ^8-93

Since both I and everyone else equally want to be happy, what real difference is there between us, that would justify me working only toward my own happiness? ^8-94

Since neither I nor anyone else wants to suffer, what real difference is there between us, that would justify me protecting only myself, and not everyone else too? ^8-95

If I don't bother protecting others from suffering simply because it doesn't harm me directly, then why do I protect myself from suffering that hasn't even happened yet — which, right now, also doesn't harm me at all? ^8-96

The idea that "I am the one who will experience that future suffering" doesn't really hold up — because in a real sense, the person who dies and the person reborn afterward aren't exactly the same continuous person. ^8-97

If the idea is that only the person actually experiencing suffering should be the one protected from it, then, using that same logic, since the foot's pain doesn't belong to the hand, why does the hand still reach out to help the foot? ^8-98

Someone might object, "True, that reasoning isn't perfectly logical either, but we protect our own foot because we feel like one unified self." But then, whatever kind of thinking isn't fully logical — for self or for others — should be let go of, wherever we reasonably can. ^8-99

What we call "a person" or "a self" over time is really just a convenient label — like calling a string of beads "a rosary," or a group of soldiers "an army" — not some solid, unchanging thing. Since there's no single, fixed owner of suffering, whose suffering, exactly, is it supposed to be? ^8-100

Since suffering doesn't really belong exclusively to any one fixed "owner," there's no fundamental difference between my suffering and someone else's. Suffering should simply be relieved because it's suffering — there's no real point insisting on rigid boundaries here. ^8-101

Why shouldn't everyone's suffering be addressed equally? There's really no good reason to argue otherwise. If suffering deserves to be relieved at all, it deserves to be relieved for everyone — and if not, well, that includes me too, since I'm just one being among countless others. ^8-102

Someone might ask, "Why deliberately cultivate compassion, when it brings its own kind of suffering?" But when you really consider the vast suffering of all beings, how could the discomfort of compassion possibly compare? ^8-103

If accepting a little suffering myself could genuinely reduce the greater suffering of many others, then someone with real compassion should be willing to take that on, for everyone's sake. ^8-104

That's exactly why the bodhisattva Chandraprabha, even knowing about the danger a cruel king posed, didn't avoid his own suffering — because he understood that accepting it would end the suffering of many others. ^8-105

In this way, once I've truly grown used to feeling joy at relieving others' suffering, I would even be willing to enter the worst suffering myself, the way a swan happily glides into a beautiful lotus pond. ^8-106

Wouldn't the immense joy of helping beings become free from suffering be more than enough reward on its own? What more could someone who genuinely wants real freedom possibly need? ^8-107

So, even while working hard to help others, there's no need for pride or self-congratulation. Taking joy purely in the act of helping, there's no need to hope for some personal payoff later. ^8-108

Just as I naturally protect myself even from something as small as an unkind remark, I should cultivate that same protective, caring attitude toward everyone else too. ^8-109

Through pure habit, we've come to feel a sense of "me" even in relation to something as basic as the biological material that formed this body — something with no real, solid self in it to begin with. ^8-110

In that same way, why couldn't I train myself to feel that same sense of connection toward other people's bodies too? It's really no harder than how I already came to feel connected to this body of mine. ^8-111

Recognizing that I myself have plenty of flaws, while others have so many good qualities, I should practice letting go of self-centeredness, and instead work on genuinely embracing others. ^8-112

Just as we naturally accept that the hands and feet are simply parts of one body, why not, in that same way, come to see all beings as parts of one greater whole? ^8-113

Just as, through sheer habit, a sense of "me" developed around this body — which actually has no independent self at its core — why couldn't that same sense of connection develop, through habit, toward other beings too? ^8-114

In this way, even while helping others, there's really no reason to feel proud or self-congratulatory — just as I don't expect a reward simply for feeding myself when I'm hungry. ^8-115

So, just as I naturally protect myself even from something small like an unkind word, I should train myself, through practice, to hold that same caring, protective attitude toward every being. ^8-116

This is exactly why the compassionate Avalokiteshvara, out of a wish to remove fear from beings, even blessed his own name with the power to bring comfort. ^8-117

I shouldn't shy away from this challenging practice, because through the power of habit, even something whose very name once seemed frightening can eventually become something we genuinely miss if it's gone. ^8-118

Anyone who wants to quickly protect both themselves and others should practice this profound, secret method: exchanging places between self and other. ^8-119

Because of my attachment to this body, even small threats bring on real fear. So really, who wouldn't feel some resentment toward a body that causes this much anxiety, almost like it's an enemy? ^8-120

Wanting simply to satisfy the body's hunger and thirst, some people end up killing birds, fish, and deer, and lying in wait along their trails. ^8-121

For the sake of profit and status, some people go as far as harming even their own parents, or taking what rightfully belongs to sacred, communal purposes — and for that, they end up facing terrible consequences. ^8-122

What truly wise person would desire, protect, and honor the body the way we usually do, without any clear-eyed perspective? Who wouldn't instead relate to it with a balanced, healthy attitude, without extreme attachment? ^8-123

"If I give this away, what will I have left for myself?" — thinking this way, focused only on self-interest, is the mindset of a truly stingy, hungry-ghost-like mind. "If I use this for myself, what will I have left to give others?" — thinking this way, focused on others' benefit, is the mindset of the truly generous. ^8-124

If I harm others for my own sake, I'll end up suffering the consequences later. But if I'm willing to accept some hardship myself for the sake of others, I open the door to every kind of real excellence. ^8-125

Wanting to put myself above everyone else just leads to a bad rebirth, confusion, and misery. But redirecting that same energy toward wanting others to succeed instead leads to a good rebirth and genuine respect. ^8-126

If I make others serve my own interests, I'll eventually find myself in a position of servitude too. But if I put myself in service of others, I'll end up experiencing real leadership and genuine respect instead. ^8-127

All the happiness that exists in this world comes from genuinely wanting others to be happy. And all the suffering that exists in this world comes from wanting only one's own happiness. ^8-128

Why say more? Just look at the clear difference between someone caught up in pure self-interest and the Buddha, who worked entirely for the benefit of others. ^8-129

If I don't genuinely learn to trade my own comfort for taking on others' burdens, I'll never truly reach enlightenment — and I won't find real, lasting happiness in this life either. ^8-130

Setting aside future lives for a moment — even just in this life, someone who won't put in real effort, and an employer who won't pay people fairly, never really succeed at their own goals either. ^8-131

By giving up the real happiness that comes from benefiting both this life and future ones, and instead causing suffering to others, a confused person just ends up bringing terrible suffering down on themselves. ^8-132

All the violence, fear, and suffering in this world really comes down to clinging to a sense of "self." So what good is this great "demon" of self-clinging really doing for me? ^8-133

Without truly letting go of self-centeredness, there's no way to actually escape suffering — just like there's no way to avoid being burned while you're still holding onto the fire. ^8-134

So, in order to end the harm I bring on myself, and to ease the suffering of others, I should give myself fully to others, and hold them just as dear as I hold myself. ^8-135

"I now belong to others' care" — mind, you need to really accept this. From now on, don't think about anything except the benefit of every being. ^8-136

It's not right to use my eyes and other abilities only for my own self-interest, when really they exist to serve everyone. And it's certainly not right to use those same abilities to harm others. ^8-137

So, I should put others first, and whatever I find of value in myself, I should offer it up and put it to real use for their benefit. ^8-138

Treating someone in a humbler position as if they were "me," and treating myself as if I were "someone else," I should try, as a training exercise, seeing what it's like to feel envy, rivalry, and pride directed at "myself" this way. ^8-139

"That person over there gets honored — not me. I don't have what they have. They get praised, while I get criticized. They're happy, while I struggle." ^8-140

"I'm the one doing all the hard work, while that person just relaxes. They're seen as important, while people say I have nothing to offer at all." ^8-141

"What's the point of saying I have no good qualities? I have plenty! Sure, some people surpass me, but I surpass others too." ^8-142

"My decline in discipline or wisdom isn't really my fault — it's just the power of disturbing emotions at work. I should do whatever I can to help myself, and even accept some harm along the way if that's what it takes." ^8-143

"But if I'm not even worth helping, then why is that other person looking down on me? What good do their fancy qualities even do for me? Fine, they're the one with all the qualities, apparently." ^8-144

"This person, teetering right on the edge of a terrible rebirth themselves, has no real compassion for suffering beings at all — yet they're the one showing off their supposed good qualities, hoping to impress people who actually know better." ^8-145

"Comparing themselves to me as if we were equals, just to make themselves look better, they'll go out of their way — even starting arguments — to make sure they end up with more wealth and recognition than I have." ^8-146

"How can I make sure everyone knows about my own good qualities, while making sure no one ever hears a word about this other person's?" ^8-147

"My own faults should stay hidden, and I should be the one receiving praise and offerings, not that person. I should be the one who gets ahead, while they get nothing." ^8-148

"For a long time, I'll actually enjoy watching this person go through some misfortune, becoming a target of ridicule and criticism from everyone around." ^8-149

"And this troublesome person even claims to rival me? Do they really match up to me in knowledge, intelligence, looks, family background, or wealth?" ^8-150

"Hearing my own good qualities announced and celebrated by everyone like this fills me with real joy, and I thoroughly enjoy this happy feeling." ^8-151

"Even if this person has some wealth, if they're really supposed to be working for me, I should just give them the bare minimum to survive on, and take the rest for myself by force." ^8-152

"I should make sure this person never gets to be truly happy, and stays constantly exposed to harm from me instead — after all, this is the very person who has caused me trouble in hundreds of ways, throughout this endless cycle of life." ^8-153

So, mind, even though countless lifetimes have already passed with you working only for your own benefit, all that tremendous effort has produced nothing but ongoing suffering. ^8-154

So now, truly commit yourself to working for the benefit of others instead. Since the Buddha never taught anything false, you'll come to see for yourself, later on, exactly how valuable this really is. ^8-155

If you had actually taken up this practice sooner, you wouldn't be stuck now in this state, still lacking the complete happiness of full enlightenment. ^8-156

So, just as you already learned to feel "this is me" about a body that originally came from someone else's biological material, you should learn, in that same way, to feel that same closeness toward other people too. ^8-157

Having truly become devoted to others' interests, take whatever you find of value in your own life and body, and put it directly to use helping them. ^8-158

"I'm happy, while that other person isn't. I'm respected, while they're overlooked. I'm the helpful one, while they contribute nothing." Given all that, mind, why wouldn't you feel a bit of jealousy toward your own self-centered habits? ^8-159

I should be willing to give up some of my own comfort, and take on some of others' suffering instead, constantly asking myself, "What is this self-centeredness actually doing for me?" and really look honestly at my own faults. ^8-160

I should be quick to take responsibility, even for others' mistakes, treating them almost as my own, while openly admitting even the smallest of my own faults to others. ^8-161

I should let go of chasing my own reputation, and instead speak well of others, putting myself humbly to work, serving whatever others actually need. ^8-162

This self-centered mind of mine is naturally full of faults, so I shouldn't go around praising it for some occasional good quality. In fact, I should be perfectly fine if no one ever finds out about whatever good it does. ^8-163

In short, whatever harm this self-centered mind has caused others in the past, purely for my own benefit — now let that harm come back onto me instead, for the sake of all beings. ^8-164

Don't let this mind grow forceful and unruly. Instead, keep it gently restrained, the way a shy newcomer might hold back, with real humility and caution. ^8-165

"Do this. Stay this way. Don't do that." This is exactly how I should keep this mind of mine in check — and if it steps out of line, I should firmly bring it back. ^8-166

But if, even after all this guidance, you, mind, still refuse to cooperate — since you're really the root cause of every fault — you deserve to be firmly reined in. ^8-167

"That earlier time when you managed to get away with harming me is over now. I see you clearly now — there's nowhere left for you to hide. I'm going to break down all of your arrogance." ^8-168

"Give up, once and for all, this idea that you still have some separate interest of your own to protect. I've already committed you to serving others — so stop complaining, and just get on with it." ^8-169

"If, out of carelessness, you fail to genuinely dedicate yourself to beings, I will hand you over instead to the guardians of hell." ^8-170

"You've caused me so much long-lasting suffering in the past, through this exact same self-centered pattern. Remembering all of that now, I'm going to break down this self-centeredness of yours, once and for all." ^8-171

If I genuinely want happiness, I shouldn't chase it through self-centeredness. And if I genuinely want to protect myself, I should instead focus on always protecting others. ^8-172

The more I pamper and indulge this body, the more delicate and overly sensitive it becomes, falling into the very same trap, again and again. ^8-173

And once caught in that pattern, even everything on this whole earth wouldn't be enough to satisfy its endless wants. So really, who could ever hope to fulfill that kind of craving? ^8-174

Impossible desires just breed more disturbing emotions and a growing sense of discouragement. But for someone who isn't dependent on getting anything in particular, their sense of real contentment never runs dry. ^8-175

So I shouldn't give this body's constant desires any extra room to expand. Someone who doesn't cling to appearances and pleasant sensations is really living the good life. ^8-176

In the end, this body just becomes ashes — completely still, moved only by outside forces. So why would I cling to this uncomfortable, impure form as if it were really "me"? ^8-177

Whether alive or already as good as dead, what real use is this "machine" of a body to me? What real difference is there between it and a simple clump of earth? Why can't I just let go of this misplaced pride? ^8-178

By constantly pampering this body, I've only piled up pointless suffering for myself — through attachment and frustration over something that's really no different from a block of wood. What's the actual point? ^8-179

Whether I pamper this body carefully, or it ends up eaten by vultures someday, the body itself feels no attachment or resentment either way. So why am I still so attached to it? ^8-180

If this body itself has no idea whether it's being criticized or praised, then really, who have I been working so hard to please and protect, all this time? ^8-181

Someone might say, "Whoever finds my body appealing is truly my friend." But since literally everyone naturally cares about their own body, why wouldn't that same logic make me feel warmly toward everyone, equally? ^8-182

So, without clinging to it, I should offer this body up entirely for the benefit of others. Even though it has plenty of flaws, I can still put it to good, meaningful use, like any other tool. ^8-183

That's more than enough of behaving like an ordinary, self-centered person. I should follow the example of the truly wise instead, remembering everything about staying careful and alert, and pushing back against sleepiness and mental fog. ^8-184

So, in order to clear away these mental obstacles, I should turn my mind away from unhelpful patterns, and constantly settle it, through meditation, on what's genuinely true and worthwhile. ^8-185

*Thus ends Chapter Eight: "Meditative Concentration."* ^8-a

## Chapter 9: Wisdom

*Note: This chapter contains the most philosophically dense material in the whole text. It examines deep questions about what's ultimately real. The ideas themselves are naturally challenging — but the goal here is simply to walk through the reasoning in plain, everyday language, one step at a time.*

The Buddha taught all these practices — patience, diligence, and the rest — for one real purpose: wisdom. So, since I want to be free from suffering, I need to develop real wisdom. ^9-1

There are said to be two ways of understanding truth: the everyday, conventional way, and the ultimate way. The ultimate truth is something ordinary thinking can't fully grasp — everyday thinking itself is what we call the conventional truth. ^9-2

Within this everyday world, there are two kinds of people: those who have real insight, and ordinary people. And the understanding of ordinary people gets challenged and corrected by those with deeper insight. ^9-3

And even among those with insight, people with a deeper level of understanding correct those at an earlier stage. Both sides often use the same kinds of examples, without digging into their full implications. ^9-4

Ordinary people see things and assume they're solidly, truly real. Someone with real insight sees the same things, but recognizes they're more like an illusion. This is really the heart of the disagreement between ordinary and awakened understanding. ^9-5

Even something that seems totally obvious, like the shape of an object, is really just something we've all agreed to see a certain way — not something proven by honest examination. It's a bit like how something unclean might get casually called "clean," just by habit, even though it's not accurate. ^9-6

Someone might object: "But the Buddha himself described things directly, in ordinary terms, to help people understand. Surely he wasn't saying these things don't even exist for a single moment — wouldn't that even go against what's normally accepted, in everyday terms?" ^9-7

There's actually no problem with how someone with real insight understands everyday reality — they simply see it clearly, exactly as it truly functions on that level. The problem would be following ordinary assumptions blindly — for instance, mistakenly thinking a human body is truly "clean" — which contradicts what careful examination actually shows. ^9-8

Someone might ask: "Just as good merit comes from honoring even an illusion-like Buddha, exactly as it would a real one, then if beings themselves are also like illusions, how could anyone actually be reborn after death?" ^9-9

For as long as the right conditions keep coming together, an illusion-like appearance can keep showing up, again and again. Just because something lasts a long time doesn't actually mean it's solidly, permanently real. ^9-10

There's no real wrongdoing involved in "killing" a mere illusion, since an illusion has no mind. But for a being who does have a mind — even if that mind, in the deepest sense, is also illusion-like — real consequences, both good and bad, can still genuinely arise. ^9-11

Since a magician's spell can't actually create a real mind, no illusion can ever really have a mind of its own. But illusions that arise from various different causes can still appear in all sorts of different forms. ^9-12

There's no single cause anywhere that alone has the power to produce absolutely everything. Someone might object: "If nirvana is truly, ultimately real, while samsara is only conventionally real—" ^9-13

"—then even a Buddha would technically still be wandering in samsara on the conventional level, so what's even the point of practicing this whole path? And since causes and conditions never really stop flowing, the illusion-like appearance of things should never actually end either." ^9-14

But once the flow of causes and conditions truly comes to an end, nothing further arises, even on the conventional level. And once confusion itself has completely ended, there's no one left to perceive any illusion in the first place. ^9-15

So when no illusion at all is appearing to you anymore, what exactly is there left to see? And if you say some other "form" is still there at that moment, well — that so-called form is really nothing more than the mind itself. ^9-16

But if the mind itself is also illusion-like, in that same way, then who is there left to perceive anything at all? The Buddha himself taught exactly this: "The mind cannot directly see itself." ^9-17

Just as a sword's blade can't cut itself, the mind can't perceive itself in that same direct way either. But someone might object: "Doesn't a lamp illuminate its own light directly, though?" ^9-18

But actually, a lamp doesn't quite "illuminate itself" in a meaningful sense — it was never covered in darkness to begin with. Saying it "illuminates itself" is a bit like saying the color blue is "blue by depending on nothing at all" — the concept doesn't really add anything. ^9-19

In that same way, we notice that some things depend on other things, and some don't. But the color blue was never "non-blue" first, only to somehow turn itself blue afterward. ^9-20

If we can only say, "the lamp illuminates," because some awareness recognized that fact, then by what other awareness could we possibly know and declare that "the mind is inherently self-aware and clear"? ^9-21

Since nobody can actually observe this "self-awareness" directly, debating whether it's clear or unclear is pointless — a bit like arguing over how gracefully the daughter of a woman who's never had children might dance. There's simply nothing real there to discuss. ^9-22

Someone might object: "But if there's no such thing as the mind being aware of itself, how could we ever remember a past experience? So in what state would it need to exist at the very moment of transformation? And what exactly would that transformation even need to produce, to count as something "truly existing"? ^9-146

If it's still non-existent at that particular moment, then clearly it hasn't become "existent" yet. So exactly when would it ever properly, fully become something truly, solidly existing? Since it hasn't genuinely, fully arisen yet, that earlier state of "non-existence" can't really be said to have properly ended either. ^9-147

And as long as that earlier state of non-existence hasn't properly, fully ended, there's really no room for genuine existence to actually occur either. And it's not accurate either to say that "non-existence" just stops being non-existence on its own, without truly transforming into something else — since that would absurdly mean the same thing has two completely contradictory natures simultaneously. ^9-148

So, in this way, there's really no such thing as a genuinely solid "ending," and there's no such thing as a genuinely solid, independently existing "thing" in the first place either. Because of this, absolutely everything in this world, without exception, is always, in its deepest nature, never truly born and never truly ending. ^9-149

All beings, and this whole world of experience, are really just like a dream. When you look carefully, they're also just like the layers of a banana tree trunk — nothing solid found at the very center. In this deepest sense, there's really no fundamental difference at all between someone who has reached true, lasting peace, and someone who hasn't yet. ^9-150

So, given that everything is, in this deep sense, fundamentally empty of solid existence, what real "gain" is there to actually have, and what real "loss" is there to actually suffer? Who is really there to receive honor, and who is really there to be disrespected? ^9-151

In this deepest sense, where does real happiness actually come from, and where does real suffering come from? What is there to genuinely feel bad about, and what is there to genuinely feel good about? And if you look closely, who is truly the one doing the craving, and what exactly is being craved? ^9-152

If you examine this living world of ours closely, who exactly is it that will truly die here? What will genuinely come into being, and what has genuinely already come to be? And who, in this deepest sense, is truly a relative, and who is truly a friend? ^9-153

May all of us, sharing this same nature, come to truly understand that everything is, in this deep sense, just like open space. And yet, people who chase only their own selfish happiness, driven by the countless causes behind conflict and excitement— ^9-154

—end up swinging between agitation and excitement, going through grief, exhausting struggles, arguments, and even real violence against each other — surviving, ultimately, through wrongdoing, and facing tremendous, exhausting hardship along the way. ^9-155

Even after being reborn again and again into fortunate circumstances, and enjoying plenty of happiness there, people still end up, after death, falling into long, unbearable suffering in a bad rebirth. ^9-156

Within this cycle of existence, there are countless dangerous pitfalls waiting. And the true nature of things there never quite matches how it actually appears to us. Since different traditions and philosophies even contradict each other about what that "true nature" even is, there's really no single, solid, consistent version of reality to be found within samsara at all. ^9-157

And this same cycle of existence contains a boundless, unbearable ocean of suffering, beyond any real comparison. And within it, our own strength is so limited, and even our own lifespan is painfully short. ^9-158

And even while chasing after a long, healthy life within samsara, our time gets eaten up by hunger, exhausting labor, sleep, being harassed by others, and getting entangled in pointless relationships with immature, self-centered people. ^9-159

Life slips away pointlessly, so quickly, and real insight and wisdom remain extremely hard to develop. And in the middle of all this, what real way is there to overcome our deeply ingrained habit of constant distraction? ^9-160

And within samsara, negative influences work relentlessly to push us toward a truly severe, difficult rebirth. And within it, there are so many misleading paths, and it's genuinely hard to work through all the doubt and confusion. ^9-161

And it's genuinely difficult, once lost, to find this kind of rare, fortunate opportunity again. It's extremely rare for a Buddha to even appear in the world at all. And it's genuinely hard to overcome the powerful current of disturbing emotions. It's heartbreaking, really, how relentlessly this suffering just keeps repeating, over and over. ^9-162

In this way, even while going through such intense suffering, beings often don't even recognize it clearly for what it is. For these beings, caught helplessly in this endless river of suffering, we should really feel deep, genuine compassion. ^9-163

It's a bit like someone who repeatedly bathes themselves, only to immediately jump into fire afterward, over and over again. In that same way, even while living through truly intense suffering, some people still, strangely, imagine themselves to be perfectly happy and fine. ^9-164

In this way, living as if old age and death will simply never come for them, people carry on recklessly — until, quite suddenly, they're struck down, and fall straight into an unbearable, difficult rebirth. ^9-165

When, then, will I finally be able to bring real relief to these beings, burning in the fire of such intense suffering — offering them, like a gentle, soothing rain from clouds of genuine merit, a real share of my own happiness and goodness? ^9-166

When will I finally be able to build up a great store of goodness, with real sincerity and care, in a way that's completely free from grasping onto things as solid and fixed — and then, for the sake of those genuinely harmed by that very habit of grasping, finally help them discover the truth of emptiness for themselves? ^9-167

*Thus ends Chapter Nine: "Wisdom."* ^9-a

## Chapter 10: Dedication

Through whatever goodness I've gained from writing this guide to the bodhisattva's way of life, may every being come to enter this same path. ^10-1

May everyone suffering in body or mind, everywhere, find, through this goodness, an ocean of real joy and happiness. ^10-2

May they never lose that happiness for as long as they remain in this cycle of life. May the world find the same unbroken happiness that bodhisattvas experience. ^10-3

For every hell realm that exists throughout the universe, may the beings suffering there instead find the joy and happiness of a pure land. ^10-4

May those suffering from bitter cold find warmth. And may boundless, cooling rivers, flowing like rain from the great compassion of the bodhisattvas, soothe those suffering from unbearable heat. ^10-5

May the forest of blade-like leaves in hell transform into a lush forest of fragrant sandalwood. May the terrible thorny trees there become wish-fulfilling trees instead. ^10-6

May the regions of hell become beautiful instead, filled with lotus-scented lakes, and made lovely by the sweet calls of geese, swans, and ducks. ^10-7

May the heaps of burning coals become heaps of jewels instead. May the scorching ground become smooth crystal. May the crushing mountains of hell become beautiful palaces, filled with Buddhas receiving offerings. ^10-8

May the rain of burning stones and weapons become, from this moment on, a gentle rain of flowers instead. And may all the fighting there, striking one another with weapons, turn into a playful game of tossing flowers. ^10-9

May those drowning in that terrible river, their flesh gone and their bones white as jasmine flowers, gain, through this goodness, a beautiful new body, and rest peacefully by the riverbank, surrounded by joyful company. ^10-10

"Why do the terrifying servants of death — the crows and vultures — no longer frighten anyone here? What wonderful power has cleared away all this darkness and brought such joy?" — and then, looking up, may they see a radiant, protective figure in the sky, and, filled with overwhelming joy, become free of wrongdoing and join him. ^10-11

Seeing the burning coals of hell suddenly put out by a shower of flowers mixed with fragrant water, and wondering, "What is this sudden happiness filling me so completely?" — may the beings there see the compassionate bodhisattva appear right in front of them. ^10-12

"Friends, let go of your fear, and come this way, quickly! Through whose power have we suddenly become free of suffering, filled instead with this wave of joy?" — a young, radiant protector has arrived, bringing fearlessness and safety to everyone. ^10-13

"Look — hundreds of gods bow their crowned heads to honor him; a shower of flowers falls gently from above; and in a beautiful palace, a thousand joyful voices sing his praise." Seeing such a gentle, radiant protector, may all the beings in hell now cry out with joy instead of pain. ^10-14

In this way, through the roots of my own goodness, may the beings suffering in hell see clouds — like great, compassionate bodhisattvas — bringing happiness, coolness, and a wonderful fragrance, and may the gentle rain falling from these clouds bring them real, deep joy. ^10-15

May animals be freed from the fear of being hunted and eaten by each other. And may hungry ghosts find the same ease and happiness enjoyed in the most fortunate of human lands. ^10-16

May the compassionate hands of Avalokiteshvara pour forth a stream of nourishing milk, satisfying every hungry ghost, and giving them a cooling, soothing bath, always. ^10-17

May the blind be able to see, and may the deaf always be able to hear. And may pregnant women give birth safely and without pain, just as smoothly as in the old stories of miraculous births. ^10-18

May those without clothes find something warm to wear, and may the hungry find nourishing food. May the thirsty find clean water, and refreshing drinks to enjoy. ^10-19

May the poor find real wealth. May those weighed down by grief find genuine joy. And may those who feel hopeless find fresh encouragement, and a true, lasting sense of stability. ^10-20

May every sick being be quickly healed of their illness. And may no kind of sickness ever afflict any being again. ^10-21

May the frightened become fearless. May the imprisoned be set free. May the powerless gain real strength, and may everyone's hearts grow gentle and kind toward one another. ^10-22

May all travelers, everywhere, find their journeys safe and pleasant. And may whatever they set out to do be accomplished easily, without struggle. ^10-23

May those traveling by boat or ship have their wishes fulfilled, and, arriving safely on shore, may they be joyfully reunited with their families. ^10-24

May those lost on a dangerous, empty road encounter kind fellow travelers, and continue their journey without exhaustion, safe from thieves and dangerous animals, along an easy and peaceful path. ^10-25

May the gods watch over and protect children, the elderly, and anyone without protection who finds themselves lost in deserts or other dangerous, pathless places — and also those who are asleep, unconscious, or intoxicated, and unable to protect themselves. ^10-26

May all beings be freed from every kind of hardship, blessed with faith, wisdom, and compassion, provided with good food and a good way of living, and may they always be able to remember their past lives clearly. ^10-27

May everyone have access to endless resources, as vast and inexhaustible as the sky itself, and may they enjoy them freely, in peace, without any conflict or harm toward one another. ^10-28

May beings who feel small or overlooked gain real presence and influence instead. And may those whose bodies have been worn down by hardship become healthy and strong instead. ^10-29

May every woman in the world be born as a man. May those of humble birth rise to high standing. And may pride be overcome everywhere. *(This verse reflects assumptions from its historical context about gender and social status that don't match how we understand equality today. In its own time, it expressed a wish for beings facing social disadvantage to gain more freedom and standing — and it asks that those of humble standing rise up, while everyone's arrogance and pride is overcome.)* ^10-30

Through this goodness of mine, may every single being, without exception, give up all wrongdoing, and always act with real goodness instead. ^10-31

May they never lose touch with bodhichitta, and may they stay devoted to this way of life. May the Buddhas watch over and care for them fully, and may they give up anything that works against this path. ^10-32

May every being enjoy an immeasurably long life. May they always live happily, and may even the very sound of the word "death" never need to be heard. ^10-33

May every direction be filled with beautiful, wish-fulfilling groves, echoing everywhere with the sweet sound of the Buddha's teachings. ^10-34

May the ground everywhere be free of gravel and rough patches, and may it become as smooth and level as the palm of a hand, gentle and beautiful like polished stone. ^10-35

In gatherings as vast as can be imagined, may many bodhisattvas remain present, adorning the whole earth simply through their own wonderful qualities. ^10-36

May every being constantly hear the sound of the true teaching, coming ceaselessly from birds, from trees, from beams of light, and even from the open sky itself. ^10-37

May they always have the chance to encounter the Buddhas and their spiritual heirs. And may they honor this Guide of the World with endless, generous offerings. ^10-38

May the rains come at just the right times, so that crops grow abundantly everywhere. May those in power govern fairly and with integrity, and may the whole world flourish and thrive. ^10-39

May medicines truly heal, and may prayers and mantras carry real power when recited. May all spirits, even fierce ones, be filled with genuine compassion. ^10-40

May no being ever suffer, may no being ever feel afraid or disrespected, and may no being's heart ever feel unhappy. ^10-41

May monasteries be filled with study and recitation, and remain well-kept and beautiful. May monastic communities always live in harmony, and may their genuine goals be fulfilled. ^10-42

May monks who want to train find real solitude to support their practice. And, letting go of all distractions, may their minds become genuinely calm and ready for deep meditation. ^10-43

May nuns be well provided for, and free from conflict and harm. And may everyone who has taken up a spiritual life stay true to their discipline, without ever losing their way. ^10-44

May those who have broken their discipline feel genuine remorse, and work continually to make things right. May they attain a fortunate rebirth, and there too, keep their commitments strong, without slipping. ^10-45

May the wise be honored, and may they always have what they need to sustain themselves. May their conduct remain genuinely pure, and may their good reputation spread far and wide. ^10-46

Without ever needing to experience the suffering of a bad rebirth, and without unnecessary hardship, may they quickly attain full enlightenment, in a body more excellent even than that of the gods. ^10-47

May every being, again and again, make heartfelt offerings to the Buddhas in countless ways, and may they always experience the boundless happiness that comes with full enlightenment. ^10-48

May the bodhisattvas' heartfelt wish to help all beings be genuinely fulfilled. And may whatever the great Guardians intend for the good of beings actually come to pass. ^10-49

And in that same way, may those following other paths within the Buddhist tradition also find true and lasting happiness. ^10-50

Through the kindness of Manjushri, may I too, all the way until I reach a deep level of realization, always remember my past lives clearly, and always have the chance to live a genuinely meaningful, spiritual life. ^10-51

May I be content sustaining myself with whatever simple food is available, and always stay healthy and capable. And in every lifetime, may I find the right conditions for genuine, peaceful solitude. ^10-52

And whenever I wish to see him, or ask even a small question, may I always be able to see the compassionate Manjushri directly, without any obstacle in the way. ^10-53

In order to accomplish real good for every being everywhere, throughout the whole universe, may my own way of life come to match the example set by Manjushri. ^10-54

For as long as space itself exists, and for as long as any being remains, may I too continue to exist, working to relieve the suffering of the world. ^10-55

Whatever suffering exists for any being, may it come and ripen on me instead. And through the compassionate community of bodhisattvas, may all beings come to know real happiness. ^10-56

May this teaching — the one true remedy for the suffering of beings, and the source of all real happiness — remain in the world for a very long time, genuinely valued and supported. ^10-57

I bow to Manjushri, through whose kindness this wholesome intention arose in me in the first place. And I bow, too, to my spiritual teacher, through whose kindness I have been able to grow and flourish on this path. ^10-58

*Thus ends Chapter Ten: "Dedication."* ^10-a
