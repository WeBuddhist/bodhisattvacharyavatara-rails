## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 20 and 21 from the third chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. Together we reflect on that deep prayer of becoming, ourselves, a support for all beings, like earth, water, fire, wind and space.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a passage of scripture that is easy to understand and encouraging.

This is a sutra passage quoted in Khenpo Minyak Kunzang Sonam's commentary, by way of the _Shikshasamuchaya_. It shows that we should turn this body of ours into a support for the life of all beings. The four great elements are such a support.

> I shall give this body over to whatever work all beings have. For example, these four great elements, the earth element, the water element, the fire element and the wind element, are used and fully used in many ways, in many kinds, for many purposes, as many articles, and in many activities. In the same way, with this body made of the four great elements, let me think: in many ways, in many kinds, for many purposes, as many articles and in many uses, may I widely sustain the life of all beings.

So says the _Akshayamati Sutra_.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when I help someone, I will do it like the earth, without expecting anything in return.

**Explanation:** *(Generosity Practice)* This verse says that we should benefit beings as the earth and space do. The earth is a support for everyone alike. In the same way, if we help others without hoping for a return, happiness comes to us and to them both.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

In brief: the four great elements, earth and the rest, together with space, are simply not the sort of thing any particular being can claim as its own. They are like common property — something all beings put to use, something all beings enjoy.

And they are not that for a while only. For as long as space itself is not exhausted, they remain what all beings share and use in common. So the prayer runs: may I too be like the elements — earth, water, fire, wind and space — and for as long as beings are not exhausted, may I become for them something they can all draw on and use in common.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^3-20]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

These four — earth and the rest — are called "elements" because everything that arises, outer and inner alike, arises from them; and they are called "great" because so much depends upon them and their reach is so wide.

Earth supports, water binds together, fire ripens, wind keeps things from rotting, and so on; and space, by pervading everything and opening up room for it, sustains all beings. Just so —

may I myself also, always, become for beings beyond counting the cause or the ground of every kind of sustenance there is: supporting, binding, ripening, moving, opening up room, and providing food, clothing, wealth and all the rest they enjoy. This is a prayer of very wide reach.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-20]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Just as the four great elements, earth and the rest, do the work of beings, and just as space does, so may I too always become, in many kinds of ways, a cause of the sustenance of measureless beings.

This accords with what is said in the *Heap of Jewels*: that a bodhisattva works for the welfare of beings in the manner of the five elements.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^3-20]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

> [!note] Grouped span
> In Ngulchu Thogme's commentary all the prayer verses from 3-17 to 3-20 are explained together as a single passage. Since 3-20 is the last verse of that passage, his concluding words for the whole set are also given below.

Just as the four great elements, earth and the rest, together with space, are always the ground of the sustenance of all beings, so may I too become the ground of the sustenance of measureless beings, in many kinds of ways.

He takes this set of verses — the protector of the protectorless, the wish-fulfilling jewel, the great elements and all the prayers that go with them — as the generating of the mind that prays to be victorious over the god of virtue and over enemies alike.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The great elements, earth and the rest, sustain beings in this way: earth supports, water binds together, fire ripens, wind keeps things from rotting, and by this medicines, trees and crops and the like are made to flourish. And space sustains them by pervading everywhere and opening up room for every activity. Just so —

may this body of mine, which is itself a gathering of the great elements, always become the ground of every kind of sustenance there is for beings measureless and without end: supporting them, binding together, ripening, moving, opening out wide room for them, and all the rest.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Of the four great elements, earth and the rest: this great earth acts as the support of beings and of what beings enjoy — crops, medicines, flowers, grass, trees and so on. Water binds them together, fire ripens them, wind keeps them from rotting; and in these ways all of it is made to increase and flourish, and so beings are sustained. Space too, by pervading every direction and opening up room for whatever activity is done, acts as a condition for the sustenance of beings. Just so —

may I myself also, always, become the ground or the cause of every contributing condition for the sustenance of beings measureless and without end, through many kinds of activity: supporting them as earth does, and all the rest.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-20]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

There is the ground of earth, which becomes the basis on which beings dwell; water, which is moist and wet and relieves the affliction of thirst; fire, which ripens things further and further and frees them; and wind, which stirs and moves — these four great elements. And there is what arises from them: form, smell, taste and things to touch. May I, by way of all of these, accomplish whatever is of benefit to beings.

And just as empty space itself never changes into anything other than what it is, so may I too, always and without a break, become the ground of the sustenance of beings countless and measureless — in whatever many kinds of ways they need: whatever they require, food and clothing, bedding and shelter, land and the rest, may I become just that for them. So one prays.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Prayer to Become, Like the Four Great Elements and Space, the Ground of the Sustenance of Measureless Beings for All Time

All the commentaries agree in the end that this verse is a prayer to become, like earth, water, fire, wind and space, the ground of the sustenance of beings.

###### 2. What "Great Element" Means — So Much Depends Upon Them and Their Reach Is So Wide

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that the four great elements, earth and the rest, are called "great" because they are the great place from which everything that arises, outer and inner alike, arises.

###### 3. The Commentaries Differ Over the Particular Work of Each of the Four Elements ⚑

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam all three explain that earth supports, water binds together, fire ripens and wind keeps things from rotting. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains it differently: water relieves thirst and makes things moist and wet, and wind stirs and moves.

###### 4. Space Sustains All Beings by Pervading Everything and Opening Up Room for Every Activity

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk — all four — explain this quality of space in the same way.

###### 5. This Prayer Accords With the *Heap of Jewels*, Where It Is Said That a Bodhisattva Works for the Welfare of Beings in the Manner of the Five Elements

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains the verse by linking it to that passage from the *Heap of Jewels* sutra.

###### 6. Since the Four Great Elements and Space Are the Common Property of All Beings, Claimed by None, May I Too Become Something Beings Share and Use in Common for as Long as They Last

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that the five elements are common property, not the sort of thing any particular being can claim as its own, and makes the prayer on that basis.

###### 7. In Ngulchu Thogme's Commentary Verses 3-17 to 3-20 Are Explained Together as the Generating of the Mind That Prays to Be Victorious Over the God of Virtue and Over Enemies

Ngulchu Thogme takes the whole set of prayer verses together — the protector of the protectorless, the wish-fulfilling jewel, the great elements and the rest — and explains them as one.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In this verse the prayer is made that, like the four great elements — earth, water, fire and wind — and like space, I too may always become the ground of the sustenance of measureless beings, in many kinds of ways. All the commentaries agree in the end that this is a dedication of very wide reach, resting on the four elements that are the support of everything that arises, outer and inner alike.

**Key points.**

- Like earth, water, fire, wind and space, may I too always become the ground of the sustenance of measureless beings.
- To call them "great elements" means that so much depends upon them and that their reach is wide.
- ⚑ The commentaries differ over how the work of each element is identified: Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam say that water binds together and wind keeps things from rotting, while Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk says that water relieves thirst and wind stirs and moves.
- Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains that this prayer accords with what is said in the *Heap of Jewels* — that a bodhisattva works for the welfare of beings in the manner of the five elements.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains the five elements as what all beings use in common, claimed as their own by none; and so the prayer is that I too, for as long as beings last, become something they share and use in common.
- Ngulchu Thogme takes all the prayer verses from 3-17 to 3-20 together and explains them as the generating of the mind that prays to be victorious over the god of virtue and over enemies alike.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

These words carry great power, he says. For all the realms of beings reaching to the limits of space, through limitless doors and in every possible way, until every one of them has passed into nirvana: whatever it is that beings need for their sustenance, may I become that very thing for them. Dedicating oneself in this way, he explains, gives rise to real strength of mind.

He makes the point with the example of an insect. We have obtained a human body, and we take on many hard undertakings; but if, when the time comes to actually carry them out, we turn deceitful and evasive, then we are in a worse state even than that insect. So this prayer, he says, must be made and carried out with everything we have.

Up to and including this verse, he says, the seven-branch preparatory ritual for taking up the mind set on supreme awakening has now been taught in full.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^3-21]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For as long as space itself has not ceased to be, the realms of beings extend everywhere and are never exhausted. For those realms of beings, at all times and in every way, until all of those beings have awakened — until not even one is left out — may I be the cause of their sustenance, providing every kind of enjoyment, whatever each one needs. This, he explains, is a prayer that endures for a very long time.

This training of the mind, he says, is both a preparation that comes before generating bodhicitta and a training to be practised afterwards. So one should open the mind out wide and then make the prayer.

At present, the extent of the enlightened activity of the buddhas and bodhisattvas appears to correspond to how wide or narrow their aspiration and their prayers were. For that reason, he explains, it is extremely important to let one's aspiration and one's prayers be vast.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-21]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Just as one has become the ground of their sustenance, so too: for the realms of beings that fill the whole field of space to its very limits, at all times and in every way, until every one of them is free from the suffering of samsara and has attained nirvana, may I be the cause of the particular sustenance each one needs and of every kind of enjoyment. So he explains it.

At the close of this verse he marks a section heading, "(Second) the main part," and explains that from here the preliminary prayer comes to an end and what follows enters the main part, the actual taking up of bodhicitta.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-21]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Just as before: for the realms of beings that fill the whole reach of the element of space to its limits — realms that are vast and that last a long time — in every possible way, until all beings are free from the suffering of samsara and have attained the state of the nirvana that abides in neither extreme, may I be the ground or the cause of an inexhaustible supply of the sustenance and enjoyments that each of those beings needs. One should think this over again and again, he says, and so train the mind.

On these preliminary verses about giving away one's body, possessions and the rest, he cites Butön's words: earlier scholars did not agree about whether these verses belong to the branch of dedication, whether they are the mind of aspiration, whether they are counted as part of the ritual of the main part, and so on. And he cites Butön's own view that, following the system of Shantipa, it is reasonable to explain these separately: the cause is bringing to mind the taking of beings into one's care out of compassion, while the conditions are prostration, confession, rejoicing, dedication and prayer.

Taking refuge and confessing one's misdeeds clear away what is unfavourable; making offerings, rejoicing and the rest gather what is favourable; and giving away the body and making the prayer — all of this, he explains, is indispensable as the preliminary Dharma that precedes generating bodhicitta. For bodhicitta is hard to give rise to in someone who has done harmful deeds and has not gathered merit, while in someone who has the special virtuous intention of working for the welfare of others it arises effortlessly. This, he says, is stated in the *Gaṇḍavyūha*, and he cites it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-21]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Just as was explained above: for those realms of beings that extend as far as space itself reaches, at all times and in every way, until the day comes when all beings have attained the state of nirvana that abides in neither of the two extremes, existence or peace, may I become, in every possible way, the cause of the sustenance of each and every one of those beings. So one prays, he explains.

Up to and including this verse, he says, the Dharma that goes before as preparation has now been taught; and from the next verse begins the main part, the pledge, which shows how bodhicitta is actually taken up.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-21]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. This Verse Is a Prayer That Endures for a Very Long Time — That Until All Beings Have Passed into Nirvana, I Myself May Be the Cause of the Sustenance of Every One of Them

All the commentaries hold to essentially this same meaning; the wording differs a little, but they agree in what they say.

###### 2. "Nirvana" Here Means the Nirvana That Abides in Neither of the Two Extremes, Existence or Peace

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk states this explicitly, and Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary and Sabzang Mati Panchen likewise speak of "the nirvana that does not abide."

###### 3. This Verse Brings the Preliminary Prayer and the Seven-Branch Ritual to a Close, and What Follows Begins the Main Part — the Pledge in Which Bodhicitta Is Actually Taken Up

Sabzang Mati Panchen marks this with the section heading "(Second) the main part," and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama explain it in the same way.

###### 4. This Training of the Mind Is Both a Preparation Before Generating Bodhicitta and a Training to Be Practised Afterwards; and Since the Scope of One's Aspiration and Prayers Shapes the Scope of Enlightened Activity, One Should Open the Mind Out Wide Before Praying

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that this is extremely important. The extent of the enlightened activity of the buddhas and bodhisattvas appears to correspond to how wide or narrow their aspiration and prayers were.

###### 5. The Reason Behind This Prayer to Become a Cause of Sustenance Matches the Example of the Four Great Elements, Which Serve All Beings Without Doing Them Harm or Good in Return

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains this by citing the *Akshayamati Sutra* and the *Vimalakīrtinirdeśa*.

###### 6. The Whole Set of Preliminaries — Taking Refuge, Confessing Misdeeds, Making Offerings, Giving Away the Body, Praying and the Rest — Is Indispensable as the Dharma That Precedes Generating Bodhicitta

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, citing the *Gaṇḍavyūha*'s example of the precious vajra gem, that bodhicitta is hard to give rise to in someone who has not gathered merit, while in someone who has the intention of working for the welfare of others it arises effortlessly.

###### 7. These Words Have the Power to Give Rise to Great Strength of Mind, and Such a Prayer Must Be Carried Out With Everything One Has — More Straightforwardly Even Than an Insect

His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes the point with the example of the insect.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** With this verse one makes a great prayer that endures for a very long time: until all beings, reaching to the limits of space, have attained the nirvana that abides in neither extreme, may I myself be the cause of their sustenance. According to many of the commentaries, this verse brings the seven-branch preparatory ritual to a close, and what follows enters the main part, the actual taking up of bodhicitta.

**Key points.**

- Until all beings have passed into nirvana, may I myself be the cause of the sustenance of every one of them: this is a prayer that endures for a very long time.
- "Nirvana" here means the nirvana that abides in neither of the two extremes, existence or peace.
- This verse closes the preliminary prayer, and what follows begins the main part, the pledge.
- Since the extent of enlightened activity appears to correspond to how wide or narrow one's aspiration and prayers were, one should open the mind out wide and then pray.
- This prayer to become a cause of sustenance matches the example of the four great elements, which serve beings without doing them harm or good in return; and many scriptural passages make clear that without the preliminaries — taking refuge, confessing misdeeds, giving away the body and the rest — bodhicitta is hard to give rise to.
- These words give rise to great strength of mind; and His Holiness the Dalai Lama says that such a prayer must be carried out with everything one has, more straightforwardly even than an insect.
