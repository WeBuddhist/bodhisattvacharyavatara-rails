## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 57 through 59 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. In everyday life we do not think about death. We pass the time hoping we still have many years ahead of us. But no one knows when death will come, and the drop into the lower realms is very deep. So these verses call on us to start today. They ask us to give up the laziness of not paying attention, to confess our wrongdoing, and to take up doing good without delay.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses quote scripture that is valuable, easy to understand, and encouraging.

The *Letter to a Friend* says that our life is more fleeting and more fragile than a bubble on water stirred by a fierce wind. We fall asleep at night. That we wake again the next morning is shown to be a great wonder.

Likewise the *Letter to Kanika* says that the Lord of Death has no affection for anyone, and that there is no certainty at all about when he will come. So we should not put things off by saying "I can do it tomorrow" or "I can confess later." From this very day we should make an effort in the Dharma, confess our wrongdoing, and quickly take up doing good. The letter teaches this as a point of the greatest importance.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Tonight, as I am falling asleep, I will remember death and confess with regret whatever wrong I did during the day.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* The time of death is uncertain, and life is as fragile as a bubble on water. So we should not grow lazy and say "I can confess tomorrow." As the verses say, we should make a quick effort today, before we die, to clear away what we have done wrong. That is the best way there is to take away the fear that comes at the moment of death.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

If we were to fall from a drop of one or two storeys, we would be alarmed, thinking, "I am going to injure my leg," or "I am going to break a rib." Then, when we have come to the very edge of falling into a cliff many thousands of leagues deep — the boundless cliff of the lower realms, the bad migrations — there is plainly no need to say that we must rely on carefulness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-57]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Here is the reason one must think of the cliff of the lower realms — the result of wrongdoing — and confess. Even at a small, ordinary drop such as the top of a staircase, where a fall would at most injure a limb, one has to stay careful as one comes and goes and goes about one's business. Then, as for "falling a thousand leagues" — that is, twenty thousand leagues and more — one falls beneath, into the Reviving hell and the rest, and there undergoes fierce suffering for a very long time, for an intermediate aeon and more. What need is there, then, to speak of being careful for fear of falling into that cliff?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-57]]

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Even at a small, ordinary cliff on a mountain or the like, where a fall would harm no more than a limb, one has to stay careful for fear of falling. What need is there, then, to speak of being careful about the cliff into which one falls thirty-two thousand leagues and more — which is what "many thousands of leagues" stands for — and where one has to remain for a very long time? So one should make an effort to rely on the antidotes to the afflictions.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Even at a small, ordinary cliff one must stay careful so as not to fall in. "A thousand leagues" indicates something vast: what need is there, then, to speak of being careful about the cliff where one falls into great suffering and is not free of it for a very long time?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-57]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Even at a small, ordinary cliff or ravine, where a fall would harm no more than a limb, one must keep to careful conduct in coming and going and in everything else. *(The text here carries a note that this line of the root verse needs checking.)* What need is there, then, even to mention being careful, for fear of falling, about the cliff where one falls twenty thousand leagues and more, and in Avīci and the other such places undergoes fierce suffering for a very long time — a lifespan of an intermediate aeon and more?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-57]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One might think: it is enough simply to receive the teaching; why must one strain so hard at actually putting it into practice and relying on the antidote? Consider this. Even at a merely small, ordinary cliff — a mountain, a rock face and the like — where a fall would show no more than a broken limb and bring no more than the suffering of injury or death, one must at all times, in coming and going and in every situation, be careful in one's conduct, or rather take great care, wary of falling in. Then, as for "falling a thousand leagues": it is stated by the measure of a thousand leagues, and its sense is to indicate an exceedingly great abyss. Or else it means "many thousands of leagues" — one falls beneath, to a depth of some twenty thousand leagues, into the exceedingly vast abyss of Avīci and the rest, where for a very long time, something like a whole intermediate aeon, one must undergo unbearable suffering. What need is there even to mention that one must take the very greatest care, wary of falling into so terrifying an abyss? Therefore, giving up carelessness and becoming extremely careful, one should follow the word of the victorious one and strive quickly to rely on the antidotes to the afflictions.

On this, the *Treasury of Abhidharma* says that Avīci lies twenty thousand leagues below this world; that beneath the surface of the earth, at thirty-two thousand leagues from here, lies the Reviving hell, and that from there, at intervals of four thousand leagues each, lie the other seven; and it says that the first two of the hot and cold hells lie directly one below the other, and that the lower two lie directly below as well.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-57]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Even at a small, ordinary cliff" in this world means a small place of danger — fire, water, a mountain, a rock face and the like — where the harm might be no more than a broken or sprained arm or leg, or a wound; and where, if the drop is a little larger, there is even the risk of death. Even at such small dangers one must remain careful, taking care again and again. Then consider the result of unvirtuous actions such as killing, motivated throughout by the afflictions of attachment, aversion and delusion: in the next life one sinks into the hells beneath the earth, twenty thousand leagues down and more — the hot hells, Avīci and the rest — and there, for a very long time, for half an intermediate aeon or a whole one, one must undergo the fierce suffering of heat and cold. What need is there even to mention that, wary of falling into that very cliff, one must strive again and again at the work of accomplishing virtue and giving up wrongdoing, and must remain careful?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-57]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. If One Must Be Careful Even at a Small Cliff, What Need Is There to Speak of the Great Cliff of the Lower Realms?

All the commentaries agree in the end: if a fall from a small, ordinary cliff of mountain, rock, fire or water would risk no more than the loss of a limb, and even that calls for care, then what need is there to speak of having to be careful about the cliff of the lower realms, where one falls many thousands of leagues and must undergo fierce suffering for a very long time?

###### 2. The Image Is Given as an Instruction to Strive Quickly at the Antidotes to the Afflictions

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both state plainly that the image of the cliff is meant to show why one must hurry to apply the antidotes to the afflictions.

###### 3. "A Thousand Leagues" Can Be Read in Two Ways

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains the number both as a figure of speech indicating an exceedingly great abyss, and as a phrase referring directly to the place of the Avīci hell, which lies some twenty thousand leagues down.

###### 4. It Is Hard to Get Free of the Cliff of the Lower Realms

He cites the *Śiṣyalekha*, which shows that even a birth among barbarians whose conduct is like the hell-guardians', or among animals that devour one another, is a state that goes on falling into the abyss.

###### 5. The Danger of Falling Over an Ordinary Cliff Is Present in Every Activity — Coming, Going and the Rest

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that with the cliffs of fire, water, mountain and rock one has to take care again and again.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse applies the image of a cliff to show that one must be careful in relying on the antidotes to the afflictions. If even a fall from a small, ordinary cliff, which would cost no more than a limb, calls for care, then what need is there to speak of the cliff of the lower realms, many thousands of leagues deep, where one must undergo fierce suffering for a very long time? All the commentaries explain it in the same way.

**Key points.**

- If one must be careful even at a small cliff where a fall would cost no more than a limb, what need is there to speak of falling into the hells of the lower realms and having to undergo suffering there for something like a whole intermediate aeon?
- The phrase "a thousand leagues" can be read either as a figure of speech for an immense abyss or as a reference to the actual measure of Avīci.
- The image of the cliff is given as an instruction to hurry in applying the antidotes to the afflictions.
- The *Śiṣyalekha* states plainly how hard it is to get free of the cliff of the lower realms, showing that even one born among animals that devour one another goes on falling into the abyss again and again.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

To think, "Nothing will go wrong today; I am not going to die; nothing at all is going to happen" — this is not backed by any sound reason. If it is a suffering that is bound to come in the future, the only question being whether sooner or later, then it can never be right to settle down comfortably and say, "It will not come today." In just the same way, it is not reasonable to sit at ease saying, "Nothing will go wrong today."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Hence the instruction to strive quickly at the antidote, at virtue. To let the mind settle, thinking, "For today at least I shall not die," and to go on sitting like this, is neither reasonable nor sound. For the time when I shall die and cease to be is certain to come, beyond all doubt; and there is no guarantee that it will not come this very evening. As it says in the *Letter to a Friend* too: "Life has many harms, and is more fleeting than a bubble on water struck by a torrent. That we breathe out, breathe in, and fall asleep — and still find the chance to wake — is a great wonder."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

In Zhenga's annotation commentary the underlying gloss is set out from the side of the annotation commentary itself, without any difference as to whether a full explanatory commentary is appended. It sets out in brief, in the underlying gloss, the manner of it: to think, "I shall not die just for today," and so to sit at ease without making any effort at the means of purifying wrongdoing, is not reasonable, because the time when this body of mine will cease to be is certain to come.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One might grant that effort at the antidote is needed and think, "I shall make the effort next month, or next year." No — it is right to make the effort this very day. To remain in the comfort of laziness, not striving at the antidote because "I shall not die just today," is not reasonable; for there is nothing to rely on in the thought that one will not die today, and the time when I shall die and cease to be may well arrive tomorrow, beyond all doubt. So, without laziness, make the effort this very day.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

One might think, "Wrongdoing is indeed a grave fault, but it will be enough to confess it later on." It is not reasonable to sit at ease saying, "I shall not die just today." For the time when I shall cease to be — the time of death — is certain to come. *(Part of a passage in which verses 58 and 59 are gathered together and explained as one.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

To let the mind settle and to sit on comfortably at one's leisure, thinking, "For today at least I shall not die," is not reasonable. For the time when I shall die and cease to be is certain to come, beyond all doubt, and there is no certainty as to when it will come. As it says in the *Letter to a Friend* too: "This life has many harms, and is more fleeting than a bubble on water struck by the wind. That we breathe out, breathe in, and fall asleep — and still find the chance to wake — is such a wonder."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One might say: "Wrongdoing is a grave fault, but since I am not about to die just now, it will be enough to confess it at leisure." It will not be enough. Why? Because to take "for today at least I shall not die" as one's reason, and so, instead of striving at the antidote, to stretch out in laziness, relaxed and comfortable in mind, is in every way unreasonable. There is nothing to rely on even in the thought that one will not die today; the tomorrow on which I myself shall die and cease to be is certain to come, beyond all doubt, and there is no certainty as to when it will come. Thus the *Letter to Kaniṣka* says: "The Lord of Death is friend to no one, and falls upon us suddenly; so do not put things off, saying, 'I shall do it tomorrow' — turn instead to the true Dharma, and act." And: "'This tomorrow, that the next' — such planning does a person no good; for the tomorrow on which you will no longer be is certain to come." So, since there is no certainty about when one will die, one should not take one's ease in laziness, but strive from this very day at the antidotes to the afflictions and to wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This is the instruction that one must quickly take up the means of purifying wrongdoing. To sit or remain at ease, comfortable and relaxed in mind, not striving at the means of purifying wrongdoing on the grounds that "just for today, I am not going to die," is in every way unreasonable and unsound. The reason is this: the time when this very life of mine, while I am alive, runs out — when I die and cease to be — is certain to come; it will most definitely come to pass.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-58]]

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. It Is Not Reasonable to Relax, Thinking, "I Shall Not Die at Least Today"

All the commentaries agree in the end that to let the mind settle in the thought that one will not die just for today, and so to sit at ease without striving at the means of purifying wrongdoing, is not reasonable.

###### 2. The Time of Death Is Certain to Come, and There Is No Certainty When — So Putting It Off Is Unreasonable

The time when this body of mine will cease to be is certain to arrive, and there is no guarantee that it will not come this very evening; so it is not reasonable to postpone matters with the thought that it will be enough to confess later on.

###### 3. Life Is More Fragile Than a Bubble

The *Letter to a Friend* shows that life is more fleeting than a bubble on water struck by the wind, and that it is a great wonder that we fall asleep each night and still find the chance to wake.

###### 4. Do Not Put Off the Work of Purifying Wrongdoing Until Tomorrow

The *Letter to Kaniṣka* advises that, since the tomorrow on which you will no longer be is certain to come, one should turn to the true Dharma this very day.

###### 5. This Is an Instruction to Take Up the Means of Purifying Wrongdoing Quickly

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that this line is given precisely as an instruction that one must quickly enter upon the means of purifying wrongdoing.

Who has given me the sublime gift of fearlessness — the promise, "You will not die"? Since all who live end in death, how could I be sure of escaping this death? And if it is beyond doubt that I shall cease to be, how can it be right for my mind to sit at ease?

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Who has given me freedom from fear?" — no one has given it, and there is no one at all who gives such fearlessness. So how could one be sure of escaping those fears? Sitting back at one's ease is no way to get free of them. And as for "Since it is certain that I will not be here, how can my mind be at ease?" — if it is certain that beyond doubt I shall cease to be, then for what reason would it be right to sit in carelessness, in the kind of laziness that clings to worthless activity? It is not right to sit like that.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-59]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

So then: who has given me the assurance that I shall not die even for today, that I need not fear death or be frightened of it? Neither the Buddha, the Blessed One, nor anyone else has given it. And if no one has given it, how could I be sure of escaping this fear of death? I could not. As the *Sutra of the Auspicious Night* says: "Who knows whether one may die as soon as tomorrow? Make your effort this very day. That great host, the lord of death — do you not know him yourself?" And if it is certain that beyond all doubt I shall die and cease to be, how could I let my mind sink into comfort and pass the time at leisure in eating and drinking, games and distractions? It is not right to sit like that. As it says in the *Dispelling Sorrow* too: "Below the earth, or in the higher realms — someone born there who has not died: have you seen even one such? Or heard of one? Or even doubted the matter?"

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-59]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

In Zhenga's annotation commentary the underlying gloss is set out from the side of the annotation commentary itself, without any difference as to whether a full explanatory commentary is appended. It sets out in brief, in the underlying gloss, the manner of it: no one has given me the sublime gift of fearlessness, the promise "You will not die"; since all who live end in death, there is no getting free of this death; and if it is beyond doubt that I shall cease to be, it is not right for my mind to sit at ease.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One might say: "Even though we die, being afraid does no good, so there is no need to be afraid." But we see and hear that everyone else dies, and the frightening ground of falling into the lower realms after death is there all the same. So who — what holy being — has given me the fearlessness that says, "You need not be afraid of death or of wrongdoing"? There is no giver of it at all. Therefore, if I do not strive at the antidote, how could I be sure of escaping this wrongdoing and this death? I shall not escape. And if, having died, it is beyond doubt that I shall cease to be, then for what reason would it be right for me, under the power of laziness, to sit at ease without striving at the antidote to wrongdoing? It is not right. So one should make an effort to meditate on the path.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Who has given the fearlessness that says, "I shall not die"? No one has given it; and how could one be sure of escaping this death? One could not. <!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Furthermore, what person has given me freedom from the fear of death? No one has given it. And how could I be sure of escaping this fear of death? I could not. And if it is certain that beyond all doubt I shall die and cease to be, for what reason would I sit on at my leisure and ease? It is not right to sit like that. As the *Dispelling Sorrow* says too: "Below the earth, or in the higher realms — someone born there who has not died: have you seen even one such? Or heard of one? Or even doubted the matter?"

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-59]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Even so, one might say: what good does it do to fear death and be frightened of it? Fear is not appropriate. — But consider: "Everyone else dies, and those whose actions are unwholesome fall into the lower realms; you, however, will not die; and even if you do die, you will not go to the lower realms, so you need not be afraid." Who, what person, has given me such fearlessness? There is no one able to give it. And more than that: if there were some method of escaping these things, it would be right to sit unafraid; but as things are — with the fear of death upon one, and with wrongdoing and downfalls still on one, and with the fear of falling into the lower realms after death — whatever method one might try, how could one be sure of escaping? There is no means of escape whatever. So it is certain that beyond doubt I shall die and cease to be, and certain too that through unwholesome actions I shall go to the lower realms. That being so, for what reason do I sit at ease, relaxed and taking my leisure, without striving at the antidote to wrongdoing? It is not right to sit like that; from this very moment one must strive to rely on the antidote. Some editions read the line as, "How, then, can my mind sit at ease?"

Thus a sutra says: "All who live end in death." And the *Dispelling Sorrow* says: "On earth, or else in the higher realms — someone born there who has not died: have you seen even one such? Or heard of one? Or even doubted the matter?" And: "Thus, before every one who is born, death stands waiting; since all alike stand ready to pass on, it is fitting to grieve." And the *Letter to Kaniṣka* says: "The Lord of Death, who has no tenderness, cuts down a person's prime to no purpose; and while that killing is coming into plain view, what wise person would sit and take his ease? So, before that great and merciless warrior looses the unbearable arrow that never misses — for just so long as it is not yet shot, for that long, call yourself to your own true good." By this it is shown that there is no one at all who can reassure us, saying, "You need not fear or be frightened" — since no one born gets past death in the end, and those who do wrong do not get past going to the lower realms. And it is shown too that it is not right to stretch out at one's leisure and sit at ease.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-59]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Who has given me freedom from fear?" means: who has given me a lifespan I need not fear — an assurance such as "you will not die at least today," or "you will not die before the end of this month or this year," or "you will not die at all"? Who, what holy being that the mind could rely upon, has given such a thing? No one is able to give it, and no one has given it before. Therefore it is fitting to strive quickly at the means of purifying wrongdoing. As for "How can I be sure I will escape it? Since it is certain that I will not be here, how can my mind be at ease?" — this means: how could one be sure of escaping this life, impermanent and liable to sudden death? In no way at all will one escape it; beyond all doubt this very life, while I live, will run out and I shall come to be no more. That is its very nature. That being so, for what reason would it be right for my mind to sit at ease, not striving at the means of accomplishing virtue and giving up wrongdoing, not genuinely practising the true Dharma? In no way is it right.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. There Is No One Who Can Give the Fearlessness That Would Free Us From Death

All the commentaries agree in the end that neither the buddhas nor anyone else has given the assurance, "You will not die," and that there is no giver of such a thing at all.

###### 2. Since All Who Live End in Death, There Is No Escaping It

No one who has been born gets past death; so there is no sure escape from this death, and it is beyond doubt that one will cease to be.

###### 3. That Being So, the Consequence Follows: On What Grounds Could My Mind Be at Ease?

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and others state plainly that to sit at ease under the power of laziness, knowing full well that death is certain and that there is no way out, is not reasonable.

###### 4. Three Things That "Who Has Given Me Freedom From Fear?" Rules Out

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that no one is able to give a mind-worthy assurance of any of the three: "you will not die at least today," "you will not die before the end of this month or this year," and "you will not die at all."

###### 5. Many Scriptures State Plainly That Death Is Certain and That There Is No Escape

The *Sutra of the Auspicious Night*, the *Dispelling Sorrow* and others are cited: death stands before everyone who has been born, and no one has ever seen or heard of anyone, born below the earth or in the higher realms, who did not die.

###### 6. The *Letter to Kaniṣka* Uses the Image of the Lord of Death's Unbearable Arrow, Which May Be Loosed at Any Moment, to Urge That Carelessness Be Given Up

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites it to show that, for as long as that merciless arrow has not yet been shot, one must work at one's own true good.
