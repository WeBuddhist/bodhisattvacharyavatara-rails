## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the seventy-third and seventy-fourth verses of the fifth chapter. Today we think about walking silently like a cat. We also think about bowing our head with respect and becoming a student of everyone.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable example, which is easy to understand and encouraging. Just as a heron kills fish, a cat kills mice, and a thief steals goods, going silently and getting what we want is an example of getting things done through gentle conduct. Bodhisattvas always practice silent and calm conduct. The purpose of this is to give up the thorns of deep meditative concentration, and to become skilled in the ways of accomplishing the benefit of others. We must learn the kind of conduct that makes beings happy as soon as they see us. His Holiness the Dalai Lama taught that followers of the Buddha must learn the way of being a friend to everyone.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when someone gives me helpful teaching, I will listen with respect and without pride.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* If we listen with respect to the helpful teaching of others, our pride becomes calm. If we are a student of everyone, we gain new good qualities. This sets our mind on doing good.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

If one asks, "Why must one act in this way?" — For example, just as herons, cats, and thieves, moving stealthily and without a sound, accomplish the aims they have in mind, so too the able ones, that is, those who keep a disciplined practice, should always act in just that way, giving up the thorns of deep meditative concentration, and abide.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In the world, unpeaceful ones — herons, cats, and thieves — moving with a very gentle bearing, stealthily and without the sound of hands, feet, and so on, accomplish each their own desired aims, such as food; and if they did not act in this way, they would not succeed. If so, then as for the able one, or the bodhisattva, in accomplishing the aims of self and others there is no need even to mention that he must always act likewise, with a peaceful, tamed, and careful (བག་ཡོད) bearing.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

The example of aims being accomplished through gentle conduct is this: just as herons, cats, and thieves, moving stealthily and without a sound, accomplish each their own desired aims, so the able one — that is, the bodhisattva who keeps a disciplined practice — should always act with that same conduct.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

For example, just as herons, cats, and thieves, moving stealthily and without a sound, accomplish the aims they themselves desire, so too the bodhisattva, the able one who keeps a disciplined practice, should always act in the same way, with peaceful conduct of body and speech.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-117]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

In the world, herons, cats, thieves, and other unpeaceful ones, for the time being, move stealthily, without being noticed, in complete silence, and accomplish each their own desired aims; just so, those who practice austerities, who are restrained and diligent and keep a disciplined practice, should also always act likewise — giving up harsh and unruly manners (དྲག་བརྡལ) and acting with peace and gentleness. As the *Moon Lamp* says:

> "Smiling like the full moon, gentle, / to elders and to newcomers / always speak courteously, / and, being sincere, be free of pride."

— just as this says.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-73]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For example, the heron, the bird that kills fish, and cats and those who commit robbery, crouching low, making no great noise, going slowly and stealthily so that others do not notice, are each able to accomplish their own desired aims; if they did not act in that way, they could not accomplish them. Likewise, the able ones — bodhisattvas who have taken up the austere, disciplined practice of training the three doors — should also always act in the same way, through carefulness, practicing peaceful and gentle conduct, and thereby become skilled in the means of accomplishing others' aims just as they themselves desire. This is merely the joining of the example and its meaning for accomplishing desired aims by way of gentle conduct. These are the way of putting body and speech to Dharma-consistent conduct, by abandoning, on all occasions of conduct — chiefly the body's activities — the Dharma-contrary conduct that gives rise to faults; and that is itself the means of taking the essence of body and speech. Thus, from the *King of Meditative Concentration*:

> "Smiling like the full moon, gentle, / to elders and to newcomers / always speak courteously, / and, being sincere, be free of pride."

And in the *Jewel Garland* as well:

> "When you see others, / with calmness speak courteously, / give up irritability and shyness, / and always remain smiling."

As these say; and in short, in all conduct one must train in the kind of behavior that makes all other beings, the moment they see the bodhisattva himself, glad and eager. In the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*: "Making beings eager is itself the bodhisattva's task," as in the *Perfect Compilation of the Dharma*:

> The bodhisattva Mthong-dgas asked: "Blessed One, in whatever way a bodhisattva becomes a source of gladness the very moment beings see him, I shall practice in just that way. Why so? Blessed One, a bodhisattva has no other task than to bring beings to full maturity. Blessed One, this is the bodhisattva's true exposition of the Dharma."

As this says, one should train.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-73]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

For example: / the heron, the fish-eating water bird, / the cat, / and robbers, / constantly moving without a sound, stealthily creeping along, / each accomplish the aims they themselves desire — that is, such aims as the heron's being able to kill fish, the cat's being able to kill mice, and the thief's being able to steal goods and property — and in just the same way, / the able ones, the bodhisattvas, should always conduct themselves silently and unobtrusively, with a peaceful and tamed nature.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Herons and Others Accomplish Their Own Aims by Moving Silently and Stealthily: This Is the Example

The able one — the bodhisattva who keeps a disciplined practice — should always act in the same way.

###### 2. Give Up Harsh, Unruly Manners

Giving up harsh and unruly manners (དྲག་རྡལ), one acts with carefulness in peaceful and gentle conduct.

###### 3. The Purpose of Gentle Conduct

The purpose is to give up the thorns of deep meditative concentration, to become skilled in the means of accomplishing others' aims, and to train in the conduct that makes beings glad the moment they see one.

###### 4. Learn From the Deeds of the Able One

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that followers of the Buddha must learn the manner of abiding as a friend to all.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The commentators agree that, just as herons, cats, and thieves accomplish their own desired aims by moving stealthily and silently, so bodhisattvas too must always give up harsh and unruly manners and, through peaceful and tamed conduct of body and speech, accomplish the aims of self and others.

**Key points.**

- Just as the heron kills fish, the cat kills mice, and the thief steals goods and property by moving silently and stealthily and so accomplishes desired aims, so a bodhisattva too must accomplish desired aims through gentle conduct.
- The purpose of practicing peaceful, tamed conduct is to give up the thorns of deep meditative concentration, and to become skilled in the means of accomplishing others' aims, which make beings glad and eager the moment they see one.
- The followers of the Buddha must give up harsh and unruly manners and learn the peaceful, tamed deeds of the able one, who abides as a friend to all.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

One should make oneself skilled at encouraging others — that is, at giving Dharma-consistent advice by which others correct their faults, dispel remorse, and are urged to engage in virtue; and if, whether one has made the request "Please advise me" or has not asked, friends and others, out of a wish to benefit, give one guidance and follow-up instruction that benefits one in this life and the next, one should not rebut them by belittling them but, regarding them as one's lama, with respect, accept it on the crown of the head, saying "Excellent!"; and always, without pride, act in the manner of becoming like a student who does as all of them say. This is the means of abandoning disrespect and pride.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

What "encouraging others" means: toward any other person — male or female, old or young — urging, out of a wish to benefit, with words such as "Study!", such as "Have a good motivation!", such as "Do others' good well!"; and being skilled in every way in such urging; and, without oneself having asked others, "Please teach me and help me," accepting, gladly and respectfully on the crown of the head, all words by which others in turn speak beneficial advice; and always becoming a student of all those who, with the good intention of speaking beneficial words or giving instruction, offer them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-74]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Be Skilled in Encouraging Others

One must be skilled in encouraging others — advising them to correct their faults and to engage in virtue.

###### 2. Accept Unasked-For Beneficial Advice Respectfully, Without Belittling

When someone gives beneficial advice even though one has not asked for it, one should accept it respectfully on the crown of the head, without belittling.

###### 3. Without Pride, Always Become Everyone's Student

Without pride, one should always become the student of everyone.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that one must become skilled in encouraging others to correct their faults and to engage in virtue, and that when someone offers beneficial advice even though one has not asked for it, one must accept it respectfully on the crown of the head, without pride or belittling, and always become everyone's student.

**Key points.**

- **Skilled in encouraging others.** Moved by a wish to benefit others, one must be skilled in every way at correcting faults and at giving Dharma-consistent counsel — giving up sin, accomplishing virtue, and so on.
- **Respectfully accepting beneficial words, even unasked.** Even though one has not specially asked, if others speak words of counsel and follow-up instruction that benefit one in this life and the next and in the short and long term, one must accept them respectfully on the crown of the head, without belittling or rebutting them.
- **Becoming everyone's student without pride.** As a means of abandoning disrespect and pride, one should always become like a student who does as all those who counsel one well and speak beneficially say.
