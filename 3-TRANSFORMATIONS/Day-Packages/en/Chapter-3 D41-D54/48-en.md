## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 17 to 19 from the third chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. For the sake of all beings we wish to become a bridge, a lamp, and even a servant to everyone. What sort of stirring does that prayer set off in our minds?

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an explanation and a passage of scripture, easy to understand and encouraging. When His Holiness the Dalai Lama explained verse nineteen, he quoted this from the _Ratnavali_ of the Protector Nagarjuna:

> Like earth and water and fire and wind,
> like medicine and the trees of the wilderness.

Those six things are earth, water, fire, wind, medicine and the trees of the wilderness. By their very nature they benefit every being, holding none near and none far. In the same way a bodhisattva prays to become like that for the sake of all beings.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will listen to whatever an elderly or sick person asks of me, without pride, and help them.

**Explanation:** *(Generosity Practice)* This verse says: may I become a servant to others. So today we should give the strength of our body and speech for the benefit of others. If we serve those who need help, the sick and the elderly especially, without any pride, then our own pride settles. And a great store of merit is completed.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the fourth part. May I become the true protector and refuge of those beings who are weak and powerless and who are worn down by having no one to protect them. In the same way, may I become the chief guide of merchants and others who have set out on a journey. And for those who wish to cross to the far shore, may I become a good boat on water of middling size, a great ship on great water, and a bridge on the small waters. So the prayer is explained.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-17]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

Within the annotation commentary itself the different sizes of water are clearly distinguished: a boat for great water, a ship for middling water, and a bridge for small water. Each of the three sizes of water is identified separately in this way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^3-17]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

May I become the protector of those who have no protector, the guide of those who have set out on the road, and, for those who wish to cross water, a boat and a ship and a bridge. He explains this in a general way, without separating out the different sizes of water.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^3-17]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

May I act as the true protector and refuge of embodied beings who have no protector. In the same way, may I become the chief guide of merchants and others who have set out on a journey. And for those who wish to cross to the far shore, may I become the good boat that carries them across a river, the great ship that carries them across the ocean, and the bridge that carries them across a small stream. So it is explained.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-17]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If there is any being at all who has no protector, no refuge and no ally, may I myself become the protector and the refuge of such beings. If any being or person has set out on a long road they do not know, and has lost the way or taken the wrong one, may I become the very guide who leads them along the road and shows them the way. And likewise, for those beings or persons who wish to cross to the far side of a great river or a great ocean, may I become their boat, their ship, and their sturdy bridge. So it is explained. Here the three — boat, ship and bridge — are not assigned separately to different sizes of water, but are explained in a general way, as the means of crossing to the far side of a great river or a great ocean.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-17]]

<!-- div:divergences -->
##### ⚑ Divergences (where the commentaries differ)

- **Khenpo Kunzang Pelden:** he explains a good boat for water of middling size and a great ship for great water. ⚑
- **Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary):** he explains a boat for great water and a ship for middling water — the reverse of Khenpo Kunzang Pelden's assignment of the sizes of water. ⚑
- **Sabzang Mati Panchen:** he does not aim at the size of the water at all. He explains the boat as what carries beings across a river and the great ship as what carries them across the ocean, so that what he has in view is the kind of water rather than its size. ⚑
- **Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk / Ngulchu Thogme:** they do not assign the three — boat, ship and bridge — separately to different sizes of water, but explain them in a general way as the means of crossing to the far side of a great river or a great ocean. ⚑

On the strength of the words "may I become a ship and a bridge," it is explained that when a bodhisattva acts for others' benefit with a pure intention and pure conduct, that action turns out exactly as the other person wishes; and the story of the wings of the nāga king Ela is given as the illustration of this. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Bodhisattva's Prayer Is to Become, According to Beings' Needs, Their Protector, Their Guide, and Their Means of Crossing Water

All the commentaries agree in the end that the verse is a prayer to become, as beings' needs require, the protector of those who have no protector, the guide of those who have set out on the road, and the boat, ship and bridge of those who wish to cross water.

###### 2. "Protector" Is Meant for Beings Who Are Weak and Powerless and Who Have No Protector and No Refuge

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden speaks of beings who are weak and powerless and worn down by having no one to protect them; and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk states it plainly as beings who have no protector, no refuge and no ally.

###### 3. "Guide" Means the One Who Shows the Way to Beings Who Do Not Know a Long Road and Who Have Lost the Way or Taken the Wrong One

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains the guide as the one who leads along the road and shows the way to those who have set out on a long road they do not know and who have lost the way or taken the wrong one; and Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains it as the chief guide of merchants and others who have set out on a journey.

###### 4. The Three — Boat, Ship and Bridge — Are All for Beings Who Wish to Cross Water, but the Commentaries Differ Over How They Are Matched to the Sizes of Water ⚑

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains a boat for middling water and a ship for great water, whereas Zhenphen Jampé Gocha explains the reverse — a boat for great water and a ship for middling water. Sabzang Mati Panchen has the kind of water in view instead, explaining the boat as what carries beings across a river and the ship as what carries them across the ocean. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and Ngulchu Thogme both explain the three in a general way, without separating the sizes of water.

###### 5. On the Strength of This Verse, the Story of the Wings of the Nāga King Ela Shows That a Bodhisattva Acting for Others With a Pure Intention Turns Out Exactly as Others Wish

Kunkhyen Tsonawa takes the words "may I become a ship and a bridge" and uses the story to illustrate this point.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows, in the form of a bodhisattva's prayer, how one prays to become — as beings' own needs require — the protector of those who have no protector, the guide of those who have set out on the road, and the boat, the ship and the bridge of those who wish to cross water. All the commentaries agree on this general meaning; they differ only on the side point of which of boat, ship and bridge is matched with which size of water.

**Key points.**

- The prayer is to become the protector and refuge of beings who are weak and powerless and who have no protector and no refuge.
- The prayer is to become the guide who shows the way to beings who have lost the way or taken the wrong one on a long road they do not know.
- The prayer is to become a boat, a ship and a bridge for beings who wish to cross water — though the commentaries differ over which is matched with which size of water. ⚑
- Some explain the three — boat, ship and bridge — in a general way, without separating the sizes of water, as the means of crossing to the far side of a great river or a great ocean.
- On the words "a ship and a bridge" in this verse, the story of the wings of the nāga king Ela shows that when a bodhisattva acts for others' benefit with a pure intention and pure conduct, it turns out exactly as others wish.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For those looking for an island, may I be an island; for beings wandering in the dark who want a lamp, may I become the light of a lamp; for those who want bedding, bedding; and whatever embodied being wants a servant, may I become the servant of them all. In other words, this is a prayer: whatever anyone needs — equipment, supplies, whatever it may be — may I become that very thing for them.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For those who have travelled a long time in the middle of the ocean's waters and grown weary of it, and who are now looking for an island, the verse says: may I be an island — dry land, with fruit trees and flowers. For those who want to read a text at night, or to do anything else where one has to see what to take up and what to leave aside, but whose work is blocked by the dark so that they want a lamp, it says: may I be a lamp. For those who want a place to sit and a bed to lie down in, it says: may I be a good place and a good bed. And for embodied beings who are sick or old and who need someone to serve them, it says: may I become the one who is there for them all, doing whatever they ask.

"For those looking for an island in the water, may I be an island; for those who want a lamp, a lamp" — this line, he says, is found in some editions of the text. He holds that these prayers are the generating of the aspiring mind of awakening, in the manner of Gelha and Dralé Namgyal.

For beings who are stuck in pitch darkness and want a lamp or a light, he explains: may I become the lamp itself. For those who are worn out by a long journey, with no house and nowhere to sleep, and who want bedding, he explains: may I myself become the house and the soft bed. And if there are beings who want a servant, a manservant or a maidservant, someone to do their work for them, he explains that one prays: may I become the servant of all of them too.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. In the Form of a Prayer, May I Become Whatever Each Being Needs

His Holiness the Dalai Lama and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that this verse shows how to pray that I myself turn into whatever it is that beings need, one thing after another.

###### 2. "Island" Means Becoming, for Beings Worn Out by a Long Voyage on the Ocean, an Island With Water, Trees and Flowers

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that for beings looking for an island one becomes the island itself — dry land with fruit trees and flowers where there was only water.

###### 3. "Lamp" Means Becoming, for Beings Threatened by the Dark, the Very Light of a Lamp

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama all agree in explaining it this way.

###### 4. "Bedding" Means Becoming, for Beings Worn Out by a Long Journey or With Nowhere to Sleep, a Good House and a Good Bed

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain it so.

###### 5. "And I, for Embodied Beings" Means Becoming the Servant Who Does Whatever Is Asked, for Every Being Who Needs Someone to Serve Them — the Sick, the Old and the Rest

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains it with the sick and the old particularly in mind. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains it more broadly, as covering anyone at all who wants a manservant or maidservant to do their work.

###### 6. There Is a Question About the Textual Warrant for the Phrase "for Those Looking for an Island" ⚑

Sabzang Mati Panchen says the phrase is not found in the Indian manuscript or in the commentary, and that *dīpa* ("lamp") may have been misread as *dvīpa* ("island"); and Ngulchu Thogme likewise identifies the phrase as something found only in some editions.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse is the bodhisattva's prayer that whatever any being needs, he himself become that very thing: an island for beings in danger on the ocean, a lamp for beings in the dark, bedding for beings with nowhere to rest, and a servant for every being who needs one. All the commentaries agree in explaining it this way.

**Key points.**

- "Island" means becoming, for beings worn out by a long voyage on the ocean, a place with water, trees and flowers.
- "Lamp" means becoming, for beings threatened by the dark, the very light that shines for them.
- "Bedding" means becoming, for beings with nowhere to rest, a good house and a good bed.
- For every being who wants a servant, the prayer is to become the servant who does whatever is asked.
- ⚑ Sabzang Mati Panchen and Ngulchu Thogme both note a question about the textual warrant for the phrase "for those looking for an island."

May I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, king among precious things, which has the power to make whatever one wants fall as rain upon the four continents and the rest; and an excellent vase, a great treasure from which everything anyone could want comes forth; and an accomplished spell — for those who have accomplished the spells, by scattering the substance of the spell over another, whoever is touched by it becomes a holder of awareness equal in fortune to the gods they wished for, with a lifespan longer than the sun and moon, a complexion brighter than a lotus, strength greater than an elephant's, and other such qualities; so an accomplished spell is one that brings about attainments by mere recitation, and with it one works for the good of beings. One should train the mind by praying again and again with this thought: may I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, which clears away all poverty and from which whatever one wants comes forth; and an excellent vase, from which, when one puts a hand inside with the intention of taking the very thing one wants and draws it out, everything one wants comes forth in abundance; and an accomplished spell, by which every attainment one could want — pacifying, increasing and the rest — is accomplished by merely reciting the spell; and a great medicine, an elixir that pacifies the aches of sickness and all harm; and the wish-granting trees, from which all the necessities the mind imagines and thinks of — food, clothing and so on — come forth just as one wishes; and also a wish-granting cow, from which everything embodied beings want comes when it is milked.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

May the precious wish-fulfilling jewel, from which all the fruits of what one has in mind come just as one wishes, and from which every need and want arises, appear before those beings who are destitute and poor. And likewise, may I become an excellent vase — like the treasure vase beneath the earth, or the vase accomplished by the holders of spells — an excellent vase from which, merely by putting one's own hand inside it, every object one desires is obtained in the hand out of the vase. And likewise, may I become what is called an accomplished spell, by which anything at all — flying in the sky, passing through the earth, walking on water without sinking, and so on — is accomplished the moment it is undertaken. And may I become a great medicine that has the power to cure all those bad illnesses of wind, bile, phlegm, their combinations and the rest. And there are the wish-granting trees in Unpleasant Sound, the northern continent: when those beings of Unpleasant Sound are born there by spontaneous birth, a tree is established for each one at the moment of their birth, and for that whole lifetime every needed and wanted thing comes from that tree — may I become such an excellent tree as that. And "may I become a wish-granting cow for embodied beings," that is, for sentient beings: in Bountiful Cow, the western continent, there is a cow that yields every desirable thing anyone might need — may I too become of that very kind. So he prays.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. This Verse Is Part of a Bodhisattva's Prayer: May I Myself Become Six Things — the Wish-Fulfilling Jewel and the Rest — for the Sake of All Embodied Beings

All the commentaries agree in explaining this as a prayer: may I become the six — wish-fulfilling jewel, excellent vase, accomplished spell, great medicine, wish-granting tree and wish-granting cow — as objects that give beings whatever they want.

###### 2. The Wish-Fulfilling Jewel and the Excellent Vase Bring About the Thing Itself Merely by Reaching Out a Hand With the Wish for It

Both the annotation commentary and Sabzang Mati Panchen explain that whatever one wants comes forth merely by putting a hand inside; and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains it as being like the treasure vase beneath the earth and the vase accomplished by the holders of spells.

###### 3. The Accomplished Spell Yields All Sorts of Attainments With Which to Work for the Good of Beings

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that attainments come by mere recitation, and that qualities arise such as a lifespan longer than the sun and moon and a complexion brighter than a lotus. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that anything at all — flying in the sky, passing through the earth, walking on water without sinking, and so on — is accomplished the moment it is undertaken.

###### 4. The Great Medicine Pacifies Sickness, Harmful Influences and Harm

The annotation commentary explains it as the single cause that pacifies all harm; and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that it has the power to cure all bad illnesses of wind, bile, phlegm, their combinations and the rest.

###### 5. The Wish-Granting Tree and the Wish-Granting Cow Are Things That Give All Embodied Beings Everything They Need in Common

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk identifies the wish-granting tree as the tree established for each person born in the northern continent of Unpleasant Sound, from which every need and want comes forth for that whole lifetime; and the wish-granting cow as the cow in Bountiful Cow, the western continent, which yields every desirable thing anyone might need.

###### 6. The Wish-Granting Tree Is a Tree From Which Every Need and Want the Mind Imagines and Thinks of Comes Forth Just as One Wishes, and the Wish-Granting Cow Yields Whatever Embodied Beings Want When It Is Milked

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Sabzang Mati Panchen agree in the end in explaining it this way.

###### 7. The Prayer Is That These Six Things Appear Before Beings Who Are Destitute and Poor

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains it in the manner of a prayer: may the wish-fulfilling jewel and the rest appear before those beings who are destitute and poor.

###### 8. The Meaning of This Prayer Matches the *Ratnavali*'s Example of Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Medicine and the Forest Trees

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that these have the same meaning as the *Ratnavali*'s words, "Like earth and water, fire and wind, like medicine and the forest trees."

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows a part of a bodhisattva's prayer: that for the sake of all embodied, sentient beings I myself may become six things — a wish-fulfilling jewel, an excellent vase, an accomplished spell, a great medicine, a wish-granting tree and a wish-granting cow. All the commentaries agree in explaining that each one of these six becomes, merely by putting in a hand, or reciting, or milking, an object through which everything embodied beings want is accomplished.

**Key points.**

- The wish-fulfilling jewel and the excellent vase are things that bring about the thing itself merely by reaching a hand out to them with the wish for it.
- With the accomplished spell, all sorts of attainments for working the good of beings — and indeed whatever is wanted — are accomplished the moment they are undertaken.
- The great medicine pacifies all sickness, harmful influences and harm.
- The wish-granting tree and the wish-granting cow are things that give all embodied beings everything they need in common; Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk identifies them as the tree of the northern continent of Unpleasant Sound and the cow of Bountiful Cow, the western continent.
- The prayer is that these six things appear before beings who are destitute and poor.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that the meaning of this prayer matches the *Ratnavali*'s example of earth, water, fire, wind, medicine and the forest trees.
