## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the twenty-ninth and thirtieth verses of the fifth chapter. Here, mindfulness is shown clearly as a gatekeeper standing at the door of our own mind's house, guarding against the thieves of the afflictions.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Our own mind is like a house. Mindfulness is like a gatekeeper stationed at the door of that house. This gatekeeper must always guard the door, so the thieves of the afflictions do not enter the house of the mind. So we must never send this gatekeeper of mindfulness away from the door.

If mindfulness weakens and the gatekeeper is gone, the thieves of the afflictions will destroy us. So the *Letter to a Friend* says: mindfulness is the greatest strength of the body. The Buddha showed it as the one path. I will guard it with all my effort, because when mindfulness is lost, thieves bring ruin.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will not let mindfulness leave the door of my mind. If it does wander, I will remember the harm of the lower realms and bring it back at once.

**Explanation:** *(Diligence Practice)* This practice is the best method for guarding our own mind stream. It keeps wrongdoing and mistakes from arising through a distracted mind. If we rely on mindfulness at the door of the mind, and if it wanders, we remember the suffering of the lower realms and bring mindfulness back at once, our mind can stay calm and remain firmly turned toward doing good. This brings great benefit.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Therefore, mindfulness must never be sent away from the gate of the mind's citadel — that is, it must never be allowed to go elsewhere. Even if, at some point, it should go elsewhere, one must then recall the harm of the lower realms and bring it close once again.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-29]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For this reason, since mindfulness must always keep hold of what is and is not fitting to be done, mindfulness must never be sent elsewhere. For us as beginners, even if mindfulness lapses and the mind wanders off for a moment, we must at once recall the harm of the lower realms and restore mindfulness. For this to happen, one must constantly know what is and is not fitting, what is and is not appropriate. It should not be like knowing something while reading a text and then forgetting it the moment the book is closed; rather, the meaning of the text must be settled firmly and clearly in the mind, and that settled understanding must be one that can be recalled again and again, in whatever posture or activity — walking, sitting, sleeping. For example, if the advice that anger is a most unfortunate thing, and similar such counsel, can be kept constantly in mind without being forgotten, then, when one day we meet someone who, out of habit, starts to prepare a lie, mindfulness must at once catch the thought: there is no need to speak this lie.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For this reason, this mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and what is to be discarded should be posted at the door of one's own mind's house — that is, at the door through which the mind enters the domain of mistaken objects — for the purpose of guarding against the thieves of the afflictions, and it must never, at any time, be sent outside or away from that post. In other words, one must rely on mindfulness so that the mind is guarded from ever wandering, at any time, into the objects of the six consciousnesses. And should one forget to rely on mindfulness, so that the mind wanders onto its objects, then, recalling that having thereby fallen under the power of the afflictions one is bound to experience the harm or suffering of the lower realms, and holding these faults in mind, one must at once bring mindfulness back and rely on it again.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Since lacking awareness carries many faults, therefore, in order to guard awareness, one must install mindfulness — which does not forget the object of virtue — within the house of the mind, and never send it away through that door. Even should mindfulness decline and depart, one must, by recalling the harm of the lower realms, at once bring it back within the house of the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Since there are many faults if mindfulness is not guarded, therefore, as the Great Commentary states, mindfulness must never be sent away through the door by which it enters the house of the mind. Even should it go away, one should, by recalling the resulting suffering of the lower realms in its entirety, bring it back close once again.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Since its decline carries grave fault, therefore this mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded should be posted at the door of the mind's house for the purpose of guarding against the thieves of the afflictions, and must be held so as never, at any time, to be sent outside or away from there. Should it, through the power of carelessness, go off onto some other object, one should recall the fault of thereby coming to experience the harm of the lower realms such as the hells, and should draw it back once again and fix it one-pointedly close at hand.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Since, when awareness declines, mindfulness likewise declines, and since the decline of mindfulness is an exceedingly grave fault, therefore this mindfulness that does not forget the objects of what is to be adopted and discarded should be installed within the house of the mind, posted as the inner doorkeeper guarding against the thieves of the afflictions, and one must always keep it firmly in place, never sending it away or elsewhere at any time. Even should mindfulness, through the power of carelessness, decline and wander off onto some other object, one should at once recall that the harm of the lower realms is bound to arise from that very decline, and again and again draw mindfulness back and settle it, as before, close within the house of the mind. ⚑ Some commentators — Gelha and others — explain that here "the mind" refers to bodhicitta itself: since bodhicitta is the very means for attaining awakening, it is itself spoken of as a "door," and mindfulness is the doorkeeper posted always at that outer door, which guards against the thief that is the lack of awareness and is never to be sent away from it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

For this reason, this mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded — like a doorkeeper — must never be sent away or aside from its post at the door of the house that is one's own mind. And even should mindfulness, on some occasion, go away and be forgotten, then, through the fault of lacking mindfulness, attachment and the other afflictions arise within one's own mind-stream and one comes to engage in the paths of non-virtuous action; as a result, in a future life one is born into a lower realm such as the hells, where fierce suffering and harm arise. Reflecting properly on this, he teaches, one must bring mindfulness back to its post at the door of one's own mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

**Mindfulness as the Doorkeeper of the Mind's House.** Continuing the image of the thief from verses 5.27–5.28, a new image is here added: one's own mind is set forth as a house, and mindfulness is posted at its door as a doorkeeper who guards against the entry of the thieves of the afflictions. All the commentators consistently present the point that this doorkeeper must never be sent away from that door.

#### Scriptural Quotations

> "Mindfulness by which no affliction whatsoever arises; mindfulness by which no occasion opens for any work of Māra; mindfulness by which one never strays onto a wrong or erring path; and mindfulness which, being like a doorkeeper, gives no occasion to any unvirtuous state of mind or mental factor whatsoever — this is correct mindfulness."

<!-- sub:divergences -->
#### Divergences Among Commentators

- ⚑ **Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing Gelha and other commentators:** Most commentators read "the door of the mind" as the door of the ordinary mind's house, guarded by mindfulness against the thieves of the afflictions. Some commentators, however — Gelha among them — explain "the mind" here as bodhicitta itself: since bodhicitta is the very means for attaining awakening, it is itself spoken of as a "door," and mindfulness is the doorkeeper permanently posted at that outer door, guarding against the thief that is the lack of awareness, never to be sent away from it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Mindfulness Must Be Stationed as a Doorkeeper at All Times, and Swiftly Restored If Forgotten

All the commentators agree that mindfulness must be permanently stationed as a doorkeeper at the door of the mind's house, guarding against the thieves of the afflictions; and should it ever be forgotten, its post must swiftly be restored by calling to mind the harm of the lower realms.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites Both the Jewel-Crest Sūtra's Four Marks of Correct Mindfulness and the Letter to a Friend's Verse on Guarding It

Minyak Kunzang Sonam draws on two scriptural sources to make this point clear: the *Ratnacūḍa-sūtra*'s four defining marks of correct mindfulness, and Nāgārjuna's *Letter to a Friend*, which teaches that when mindfulness declines, thieves bring ruin.

###### 3. ⚑ Some Commentators Read "The Mind" as Bodhicitta Itself, with Mindfulness as Its Outer Doorkeeper

Some commentators, such as Gelha, explain that "the mind" in this verse refers to bodhicitta itself — the very means for attaining awakening, and hence itself a "door" — with mindfulness serving as the doorkeeper posted at that outer door, guarding against the loss of awareness; this is a distinctive interpretive departure from the reading found among the other commentators.

###### 4. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Illustrates the Practice with Everyday Examples Such as Anger and the Impulse to Lie

His Holiness the Dalai Lama shows how to put this into practice: by settling the meaning of the teachings firmly in the mind so that it can be recalled again and again in any posture or activity — for example, remembering that anger is deeply unfortunate, so that when one is on the verge of telling a lie out of habit, mindfulness catches the thought at once and there is no need to speak it.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In this verse, Acharya Shantideva instructs that, in order to guard the mind against the thieves of the afflictions, mindfulness must be relied upon without ever wavering, like a doorkeeper stationed at the door of the mind's house. Even should mindfulness, through carelessness, slip away from the door of the mind, one must recall the suffering and harm of the lower realms, immediately fix mindfulness anew at its post, and thereby return again to the correct path.

**Key points.**

- **Mindfulness relied upon as a doorkeeper.** As Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites from the *Ratnacūḍa-sūtra* (the *Sūtra Requested by the Jewel-Crest*), mindfulness is said to be like a doorkeeper because it prevents the arising of the afflictions, denies any opportunity to the work of Māra, keeps one from straying onto a wrong path, and denies any opening to unvirtuous states of mind and their mental factors. Likewise, as Master Nāgārjuna teaches in the *Letter to a Friend*, since all things are destroyed once mindfulness declines, mindfulness must be guarded with great effort — mindfulness is thus the guardian posted at the gate of the mind.
- **How mindfulness is applied in practice.** As His Holiness explains, when one meets, in the course of daily activity, conditions for non-virtue such as telling a lie or the arising of anger, recalling the relevant instructions and thereby holding one's own mind in check at once is the actual practice of mindfulness. Should mindfulness slip away from the door of the mind, the harm of the lower realms must be recalled and mindfulness gathered back at once through that same door.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-29]] [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-29]]

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-30`):*

> Mindfulness arises easily for the fortunate who live near a teacher, follow instruction, and act with respect born of conscientious fear.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

For beginner practitioners who cannot yet sustain mindfulness on their own, the method — or cause — by which mindfulness can arise is to associate with and rely upon a qualified teacher who possesses mindfulness and awareness; hence it is taught, in every vehicle's system, that one must rely upon a teacher without interruption. The teacher or preceptor, for his part, must give instruction and guidance to the student in accordance with what is to be adopted and discarded; and when the student, following that instruction, practices what is to be adopted and discarded exactly as taught, and is fearful of the teacher's reproach and of the faults that follow from transgressing the training — a fortunate person who thus holds the training in reverence — mindfulness arises easily in such a one, that is, without difficulty.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-30]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For mindfulness to arise, one needs a teacher who can teach both the general points of what is to be adopted and discarded and the manner of relying on mindfulness. Such a teacher must be one who is able, not merely by virtue of sitting on a high throne, to explain the nature of the Dharma correctly and who has personal experience of it. What is called the "qualifications of a teacher" has nothing to do with the grandeur of one's residence; it is essential to rely on a teacher who possesses the complete qualifications of learning, discipline, and good character. ⚑ Just as the teacher, for his own part, must guide students onto a path that has sound reasoning behind it, so too, even should a teacher teach something without sound reasoning, there is no need simply to set it aside and follow along. For example, there was once, in a place in America called Jonestown, someone who set himself up as a kind of teacher. Although we should not rely on the person but on the Dharma, there were those who, without examining or reflecting on whatever the teacher said, simply took a personal liking to him and accepted whatever he said as correct; and when, one day, matters went wrong, the teacher told them to take their own lives, and some accepted this and did so. In the end, some eight or nine hundred people took their own lives and died. It is clear that these events happened because of failing to examine carefully whatever was being done.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For beginner practitioners who cannot yet sustain mindfulness on their own, the method — or cause — by which mindfulness can arise is to associate with and rely upon a qualified teacher who possesses mindfulness and awareness; hence it is taught, in every vehicle's system, that one must rely upon a teacher without interruption. The teacher or preceptor, for his part, must give instruction and guidance to the student in accordance with what is to be adopted and discarded; and when the student, following that instruction, practices what is to be adopted and discarded exactly as taught, and is fearful of the teacher's reproach and of the faults that follow from transgressing the training — a fortunate person who thus holds the training in reverence — mindfulness arises easily in such a one, that is, without difficulty.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

The method for guarding mindfulness is this: through associating with one's own preceptor, teacher, and fellow practitioners of pure conduct, one receives from the preceptor instructions and guidance, along with a sense of conscience; and through fear of being reproached by others, mindfulness that does not forget the object of virtue arises easily in the fortunate person who thereby holds the training in reverence. So whichever of hearing, reflecting, or meditating one undertakes, one should become skilled in sustaining mindfulness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

It is from associating with a teacher and with spiritual friends of true Dharma — from whom, along with one's preceptor and others, one receives guidance in what is to be engaged in and what is to be avoided — and, should one transgress this, from the fear of being shamed by them, that mindfulness arises easily in the fortunate person who thereby holds the training in reverence. ⚑ As the *Jātakamālā* says: "Even if wrongdoing is done where none can see, how could one possibly be at ease, as though having eaten poison? For it is not possible that gods and yogins, with their pure eyes, should fail to see it."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

This very mindfulness arises from associating together with genuine teachers; and since the preceptor gives instructions and guidance, and, should one transgress them, fear arises, the fortunate ones of good conduct come to hold the training in the deepest reverence, and so mindfulness arises easily for them, with little effort.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Mindfulness arises from closely associating with spiritual teachers who are genuine masters and with fellow practitioners of pure conduct. The manner of this is as follows: the preceptor and teacher, among others, give instruction and guidance on how to engage in and turn away from what is to be adopted and discarded. Then, fearing that one might transgress or contradict what has thus been taught, one develops, for one's own part, a sense of conscience that shrinks from wrongdoing; and, fearing and dreading being inwardly reproached by one's preceptor, teacher, and others, one comes to be a fortunate person who engages unmistakenly in what is to be adopted and discarded, exactly as taught, and who holds the training in reverence. For such a person, without needing any other great effort, mindfulness arises easily, with little difficulty.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The cause of mindfulness, or the method for sustaining it, is this: mindfulness arises easily through long association with one's own spiritual teacher — that is, it arises through being together with the teacher, and likewise, at the time of novice or full ordination, through training oneself properly in accordance with the preceptor's instruction and guidance. In brief, should one come to transgress the vows of training, then, out of fear or dread of the fault of being reproached or met with displeasure by one's teacher or preceptor, the fortunate person — in brief, one who properly holds the vows of training within his own continuum in reverence — is one in whom mindfulness that does not forget what is to be adopted and discarded arises easily.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-30]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR_bo -->
##### The Story of King Pu-yira

The noble Kātyāyana, dwelling in the Jetavana Grove, taught King Pu-yira the Dharma of impermanence; moved by faith, the king went forth as a monk. While he was guarding his mind and meditating in a hermitage, another king's queen came to request teachings from him, and that king grew jealous; he had Pu-yira beaten with thorned whips until blood flowed from nine openings of his body, and his mind became greatly disturbed. Pu-yira went to Kātyāyana, offered back his monastic vows, and wished to raise an army and go to war. The preceptor told him: "Rest tonight, and offer back your vows tomorrow." That night, through the preceptor's blessing, he dreamed that he raised his own army, was defeated in battle, and himself fell into enemy hands. Waking in terror, he went for refuge to the preceptor and related his dream. The preceptor then taught him the Dharma, saying, among other things, "there is victory even in being outnumbered," whereupon Pu-yira withdrew his offer to return his vows and settled into one-pointed meditation, declaring that this was owed entirely to the kindness of his preceptor. The preceptor further taught him three verses of Dharma, through which Pu-yira gained faith and was led to make four pledges — to give up killing living beings, to release prisoners, to give up eating meat, and to give the four gifts among holy persons — and was afterward restored to his royal throne. This is taught as an illustration of the benefit of relying upon a teacher.

#### Metaphors and Examples

**The Teacher — the External Condition for Mindfulness to Arise.** This verse sets forth two chief external conditions for mindfulness to arise: (1) associating with a qualified teacher or preceptor who possesses mindfulness and awareness, and receiving instruction from them; and (2) the sense of conscience that fears being reproached by the teacher, or shamed by others, should one transgress that instruction. All the commentators consistently present these two conditions as together giving rise to mindfulness easily, with little effort, in the fortunate person.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Commentators Agree: Mindfulness Arises Easily Through a Qualified Teacher and the Fear of Reproach

All the commentators consistently present the point that mindfulness arises easily through two conditions together: associating with a qualified teacher or preceptor who possesses mindfulness and awareness and receiving instruction from them, and the sense of conscience that fears being reproached should one transgress that instruction.

###### 2. ⚑ Ngulchu Thogme Cites the Jātakamālā: Even Hidden Wrongdoing Is Seen by Gods and Yogins

Citing the *Jātakamālā*, Ngulchu Thogme makes clear that fear of wrongdoing extends even to what is unseen by others: though a misdeed may be committed where no one appears to see, it can never truly be concealed, since gods and yogins perceive it with their pure eyes — so one can be no more at ease than someone who has eaten poison.

###### 3. ⚑ His Holiness the Dalai Lama Stresses Careful Examination in Relying on a Teacher, Citing the Tragedy of Jonestown

His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes the point that examining a teacher's qualifications carefully is of great importance. Drawing on the actual history of Jonestown in America, he shows the great danger of accepting whatever a teacher says without any examination, and stresses the central teaching that one should rely on the Dharma, not on the person.

###### 4. The Story of King Pu-yira Illustrates the Benefit of Relying on a Teacher

As the story makes clear, it was through the preceptor's blessing and skillful means that King Pu-yira was turned back from offering up his vows and going to war, and was instead brought to one-pointed meditation — a vivid, direct illustration of the benefit of relying on a qualified teacher.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** In this thirtieth verse of the fifth chapter of the *Way of the Bodhisattva*, two chief conditions are set out clearly for the fortunate person to give rise easily to mindfulness and awareness: relying upon a qualified spiritual friend and listening well to their instruction, and cultivating reverence by holding a conscientious sense of fear that shrinks from wrongdoing. The background to this verse includes the story of how the noble Kātyāyana took King Pu-yira under his care and, through skillful means, turned back his anger and established him in one-pointed meditation; the related commentaries and modern teachings further enrich the verse with profound and far-reaching points on the meaning of fear and on how to rely upon a spiritual friend.

**Key points.**

- **Relying on a teacher, and the background of the story.** In essence, when one receives instruction from a teacher or preceptor and holds a sense of fear and reverence that shrinks from wrongdoing, mindfulness arises naturally. Although King Pu-yira had gone forth as a monk under the noble Kātyāyana, another, jealous king had him beaten with whips, and, filled with anger, he resolved to return to lay life and wage war. Yet the noble one, through the skillful means of a dream, caused him to see his own army defeated and himself fall into enemy hands; turning his mind back from war and settling his mindfulness one-pointedly, this shows the benefit of relying upon a spiritual friend.
- **⚑ First turning point: dread of hidden wrongdoing.** Ngulchu Thogme cites a verse from the jātaka tales, teaching that even wrongdoing done where none can see is nonetheless witnessed by those with divine sight and by yogins, so that one can never truly be at ease, as if one had eaten poison. Thus, he explains, "fear" here is not merely fear of being seen by others; rather, having realized that nothing whatsoever is ever truly hidden, one must give rise to a mindfulness that shrinks even from concealed wrongdoing.
- **⚑ Second turning point: a modern point on examination.** His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes an important point connected to modern needs, on how one should rely on a teacher. Citing the harm done at Jonestown, he teaches that following a teacher with blind faith and no examination can lead to great ruin; therefore, in keeping with the teaching that one should rely on the Dharma and not on the person, he stresses that when relying on a teacher, one must repeatedly apply intelligent examination — mere blind faith is not sufficient.
