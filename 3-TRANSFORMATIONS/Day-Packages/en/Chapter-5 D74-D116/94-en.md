## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the fifty-second, fifty-third, and fifty-fourth verses of the fifth chapter. As soon as an affliction arises in the mind, we practice together how, like a hero, to hold the mind steady with the antidote.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an explanation that is easy to understand and encouraging.

First, we must always rely on mindfulness. By relying on mindfulness continually, we can give up pointless actions. By giving up pointless actions, we can give up harmful actions. Giving up harmful actions is the true meaning of guarding the conduct of the body. In the same way, today's verses teach us to stay still like wood. This is also the best method when a pointless or harmful thought arises. With mindfulness, we hold the thought before it slips into action.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when I want to pursue only my own benefit and neglect the benefit of others, I will stay still like a piece of wood.

**Explanation:** *(Doing good)* When the wish to pursue only our own benefit arises, we can do nothing and keep body and mind steady. If we do, no harm reaches others. This makes our bad thought of cherishing ourselves smaller. If we stay still like wood in this way, great benefit comes to both ourselves and others.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Remain Like Wood Even When Neglecting Others' Welfare and Pursuing Only One's Own

This verse sets out that even when the thought arises of casting others' welfare aside into neglect and wishing to pursue only one's own benefit, one must remain like wood; the commentaries are in agreement on this.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Intolerance; the thought of laziness in the form of idleness (སྙོམ་ལས); and what is called fear — this fear, which appears because one does not wish to accomplish virtue, is explained as a fear of the opposing forces that are to be abandoned. On the basis of a connection with sources and scripture, the confidence of thinking "I can do it" is valid. But thinking "I can manage it!" in a way unsuited to how things actually are is pride. Thinking, with no source, scripture, or reason, that "it will all come about by itself" is called unrestrained bravado (སྤྱི་བརྟོལ). Senseless chatter (མུ་ཅོར) is saying whatever comes up, whatever happens to come out, with no consistency with reasoning or grounds. On occasions when a thought arises such as attachment to one's own side and hostility toward the other side, too, one should not act and should not speak, but must remain like wood.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-53]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Intolerance — the opponent of patience, being unable to bear hardship; laziness, the opponent of diligence; on hearing of difficult conduct and of emptiness, being afraid or terrified of them; likewise unrestrained bravado (སྤྱི་བརྟོལ), that is, acts of bluster; senseless chatter (མུ་ཅོར), that is, speaking of various kinds of unexamined talk and disordered words, whether fitting or not; and, if a mind of clinging and attachment arises toward friends on one's own side whom one likes, thinking "whatever they do, in Dharma or worldly matters, is good" — at that time one should remain like wood. And regarding this, he said that, since most of the outward enthusiasm shown toward the lamas and the like on one's own side is partisan attachment, faith and partisan attachment must be told apart without confusing them.

Being unable to endure the hardships of others' welfare; laziness, which is taking no joy in virtue; fear and timidity toward magnificent conduct; likewise unrestrained bravado — launching into things through forced boldness though one is unable; senseless chatter that speaks wildly without examination; and if a mind arises accompanied by exceptional, overt attachment to relatives, friends, and the like who belong to one's own side, that person should remain like wood, unmoved, relying on the antidote.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whenever, in accomplishing virtue and others' welfare through the three doors, one is unable to bear hardship and suffering, and so is intolerant; or, from the mind not delighting in the side of virtue, there is laziness — either the laziness of idleness (སྙོམ་ལས), in which body and mind are unfit for virtuous action; or fear — flinching in advance — before undertaking great deeds for one's own or others' sake; likewise unrestrained bravado (སྤྱི་བརྟོལ), which means entering, through mere great boldness, upon difficult tasks one has no ability to accomplish, or being very bold through lack of restraint on every side; and speaking senseless chatter (མུ་ཅོར), that is, saying whatever comes off the tongue in unrelated talk, without examination; and if a mind of partiality (ཕྱོགས་ལྷུང) arises, of exceptional attachment to relatives, friends, close attendants, disciples, and the like who belong to one's own side — then too, at that time, by being able to stand firm with the individual antidote for each, one should remain steady, unmoving, like wood.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Twenty-second: the thought of being unable to bear harm, which is the opponent of patience. Twenty-third: the thought of laziness arising, the opponent of diligence. Twenty-fourth: a thought such as being afraid and terrified of the meaning of difficult conduct, of selflessness or emptiness, which is the opponent of wisdom. Twenty-fifth: "unrestrained bravado" (སྤྱི་བརྟོལ) — through great bluster or audacity, proclaiming as though one had all those qualities and deeds that one does not have, or that one has the ability for them, which is called "swagger" (འུད་ཤོབ). Twenty-sixth: "senseless chatter" (མུ་ཅོར) — such as speaking of many kinds of disordered, reasonable and unreasonable, even shameful talk. Twenty-seventh: if a thought or mental factor arises of great clinging and attachment, in particular, toward one's own relatives and others on one's own side, then too, at that time, one should not act with the body, should not speak words with the voice, and should keep the mind unmoving, firm like wood: such is the meaning.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Remain Like Wood Even in Intolerance, Laziness, Fear, Unrestrained Bravado, Senseless Chatter, and Partisan Attachment

This verse sets out that even on occasions of intolerance, laziness, fear, unrestrained bravado, senseless chatter, and attachment to one's own side — the last of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct — one must remain like wood; the commentaries are in agreement on this.

###### 2. Fear Is Explained as Fright at Emptiness (Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk) and as Fear of Accomplishing Virtue (His Holiness the Dalai Lama)

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains the meaning of "fear" not as an opponent of patience but counts it as an opponent of wisdom, in the sense of being frightened of the meaning of emptiness; and His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains it in harmony with this, as the appearance of fear that comes from not wishing to accomplish virtue.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Adds a Separate Caution: Tell Faith Apart From Partisan Attachment

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden sets down separately the fault of attachment to one's own side: because there is great scope for outward enthusiasm and attachment toward the lamas and companions on one's own side, he gives the advice that faith and partisan attachment must be distinguished without confusing them.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Divides Laziness into Two: Flinching Before Great Undertakings and the Laziness of Idleness

Minyak Kunzang Sonam makes clear that laziness is of two kinds: the laziness of flinching in advance from great undertakings, and the laziness of idleness.

**Key points.**

- When intolerance, laziness, fear, unrestrained bravado, senseless chatter, and attachment to one's own side arise, one must remain like wood; this concludes the varieties of the "twenty-seven gateways."
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains "fear" as being afraid and shrinking back from the meaning of emptiness and selflessness, while His Holiness the Dalai Lama, the Fourteenth, explains it as shrinking back from undertaking virtuous conduct.
- Khenpo Kunzang Pelden says that, because partisan attachment toward the lamas on one's own side is so common, pure faith and partisan attachment must be distinguished without confusing the two.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam divides laziness into two kinds: the laziness of shrinking back in advance from vast undertakings, and the laziness of idleness.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, when these faulty aspects appear in the mind, at a time when there is danger of bodily or verbal expression emerging — pointless bodily expression such as digging the ground — the hero endowed with strength, who has the courage for practicing the Dharma and who strives to vanquish the opposing forces, must immediately have the antidote of mindfulness and introspection take hold, and so hold the mind firmly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-54]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

As taught before, one carefully examines and identifies whether there has arisen a mind that strives at the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct — thoroughly afflicted states such as attachment and aversion — or at pointless things such as digging the ground, cutting grass, and drawing diagrams on the ground; and, when one's own mind is striving at affliction and the pointless, at that time the hero, that is, the bodhisattva, relying on the antidote to each in his own continuum, should hold that mind firmly, so that it does not scatter or become distracted toward the objects of affliction and of the pointless; and neither body nor speech may be allowed to follow after bad thoughts.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-54]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Having thus examined whether there is a thoroughly afflicted mind and a mind striving at pointless things, at that time the hero, with an antidote likened to one possessing strength, should hold that mind firmly so that it does not enter evil deeds.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-54]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Thus, having examined whether there has or has not arisen a thoroughly afflicted mind that hoards aims (དོན་གསོག་པ) and a mind striving at the pointless, if it has arisen, then at that time the hero, the bodhisattva, with the antidote should hold that mind firmly, so that it does not stir, on one's own side.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-101]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

As taught before, having carefully examined the mind that sets about thoroughly afflicted deeds such as desire and attachment, and pointless deeds such as digging the ground and cutting grass, at the time one sees it entering those deeds, the hero who vanquishes the opposing forces, with the antidote accompanied by introspection, should hold that very mind of his own firmly, blocking its entry into improper deeds and entering into fitting and proper deeds.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-54]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Thus, from attachment and anger and so on up to attachment to one's own side — the mind that is thoroughly afflicted by way of inverted aims, and the mind that sets out upon or strives at pointless deeds such as digging the ground and cutting grass — having carefully examined such motivations, and having recognized each one immediately, as "this is that, this is that," at that time the hero, the bodhisattva who triumphs over the enemy of opposing forces such as the afflictions, with the specific, powerful antidotes — the general antidote having introspection as its chief part, and the particular antidotes such as meditating on the unattractive and on love as antidotes to attachment and the like — should hold his own mind firmly at all times, and, blocking and subduing all entry into improper, evil deeds, should train in engaging with joy in proper and fitting deeds. And as for how, when a thoroughly afflicted mind and an inclination toward fruitless deeds arise, one relies on the antidote of mindfulness and introspection — this is taught extensively in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* (Compendium of Trainings). By these [teachings] it is shown how, when the three doors incline toward entering evil deeds prompted by the afflictions, one overpowers them with the antidote to weaken their strength and abandon them; in short, they are the ways of turning back from things that belong to the side to be abandoned and that one should not do.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-54]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

As explained above, there are those twenty-seven sources of thoroughly afflicted misconduct, and besides them the pointless digging of the ground, cutting of grass, drawing of diagrams on the ground, and the like. Whether a mind or mentality that strives at these has arisen or not, one examines and investigates again and again by way of introspection; and if a mind of affliction has arisen, then at that time that very hero, the bodhisattva, relying in his own continuum on the antidote to each, should hold that very mind firmly and steadfastly, so that it is not scattered toward objects and is not let go under the power of the afflictions.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Examine Whether the Mind Is Afflicted or Striving at the Pointless, Then Hold It Firmly With the Antidote

This verse brings the instruction on the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct to a close: having examined whether the mind is thoroughly afflicted and whether it is striving at pointless things, the bodhisattva hero must hold the mind firmly with the antidote; the commentaries are in agreement on this.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam, Quoting the Śikṣāsamuccaya, Shows at Length How Mindfulness and Introspection Are Relied Upon

Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, explains in detail how the antidote of mindfulness and introspection is relied upon: guarding the body is abandoning actions that turn to harm; this is accomplished through mindfulness; and mindfulness, the scripture shows, arises from respect for the training.

###### 3. Reverence Arises From Seeing the Benefits of Calm Abiding, and Calm Abiding Rests on Pure Ethical Discipline

This reverence itself arises from the faith that sees the benefits of calm abiding, and calm abiding is accomplished in reliance on pure ethical discipline, as indicated by the quotation from the *Candrapradīpa Sūtra*; it shows that ethical discipline, meditative absorption, and wisdom are linked to one another as cause and effect.

###### 4. Sabzang Mati Panchen Marks the Turn From Restraining the Mind to Engaging Joyfully in Others' Welfare

Sabzang Mati Panchen sets down as a side point that after this verse a new phase begins, of engaging with joy in the welfare of others, showing that the earlier phase, of properly applying the conduct of the three doors, is being brought to a close.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse completes the teaching on the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct: the bodhisattva hero examines whether his own mind is afflicted or striving at the pointless, and must hold it firmly and unmoving with the antidote.

**Key points.**

- The commentaries agree that, having examined whether the mind has entered afflicted or pointless activity, one must hold it firmly with the antidote.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, shows that guarding the body comes from abandoning what is harmful; that this comes from abandoning pointless action and constantly relying on mindfulness; and that mindfulness comes from reverence for the training.
- That reverence in turn arises from seeing the benefits of calm abiding, and calm abiding is accomplished in reliance on pure ethical discipline, as the quotations from the *Dharmasaṃgīti Sūtra* and the *Candrapradīpa Sūtra* (also called the *King of Meditative Absorptions Sūtra*, ཏིང་ངེ་འཛིན་རྒྱལ་པོའི་མདོ) show.
- Sabzang Mati Panchen says that this verse completes the conduct of making the three doors fully pure, and that from here the text turns toward engaging with great joy in the welfare of others.
