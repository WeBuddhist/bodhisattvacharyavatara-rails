### Opening

Today's practice is based on the eighty-fifth and eighty-sixth verses of the fifth chapter. Today, we think about the extra food and clothes we have, and to whom we can or cannot give them.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable point, which is easy to understand and encouraging. The food we get from alms and so on must be divided into four parts. One part must be given to those who have fallen into wrong ways, one to the helpless, and one to those who keep strict discipline. We ourselves must eat the last part, knowing the right amount of food, not too much and not too little. Also, we must not be attached to extra clothes beyond the three Dharma robes. We must give them to others.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will eat just enough, and give away my extra food and clothes to the helpless.

**Explanation:** *(Generosity Practice)* The root verse says that after eating just enough, we must give our extra food and clothes to the helpless and to those who hold vows. This gives up our own desire and helps others. By being moderate with food and clothes, the mind finds peace.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

One should fully care for one's own body with medicine, clothing, and so on, because it is needed for the sake of others. For that purpose, when going about among the objects of one's conduct, one should go keeping in mind the training as it has been taught.

Then, from the alms one obtains, one divides it into four portions. One should share it with those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as animals, and with the helpless, such as beggars, and with those who live the pure life together with oneself and who keep strict discipline. When one eats oneself, one should know it as taught in detail in the *Compendium of Training*, and eat neither too much nor too little, but just enough.

Of clothing too, one should give everything except the three Dharma robes that protect the body. "Śāriputra, if a bodhisattva were to give up the three Dharma robes completely and put the beggar first, then he would not be practicing having few desires." Since this is forbidden, these are not to be given away.

Furthermore: Śāriputra, a bodhisattva householder who fills buddha-fields as many as the sands of the Ganges with the seven precious substances and offers them to the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, creates much greater merit when, Śāriputra, a bodhisattva who has gone forth teaches a single four-line verse, than that does. Śāriputra, the Tathāgata has not permitted one who has gone forth to give material gifts. Śāriputra, if the gain that has come into one's almsbowl, gain that has entered the almsbowl, is in accord with the Dharma and has been obtained through the Dharma, then one should eat it in common together with those who live the pure life — and so on, he taught.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This teaches how to partake of food and drink. First, beings who have fallen into wrong ways, such as hungry ghosts. Second, the helpless, beings made wretched by illness. Third, those who keep strict discipline, who abide in the strict discipline of the pure life. Of our food and drink, we divide it into four portions, give and offer three of the portions to each of these in turn, and eat one portion ourselves, just enough. This is what is taught.

It is not that the one meal of each day is divided into four in that way. Rather, from whatever we have, such as our property for use, we ordinarily make something like four portions. One is given to beggars. Upward, an offering is made to the Jewels. And the remaining portion, about the measure of one part, is what is taught to be used for one's own livelihood.

If one is one who has gone forth, one takes that as the basis, and it is taught that one should not give those three Dharma robes to others, but that a monk must hold them inseparably, like his own skin. It is also taught that whatever surplus clothing there is, apart from the three Dharma robes, is to be given to others. Those who have surplus clothing that is not among the thirteen requisites of livelihood, and who can consecrate it as a needed requisite or an additional requisite and so on, do very well. As for taking something as one's own, in one's mind thinking "this is mine," there is a definite limit, so that afterward no one will force one by compulsion to divide and share it.

Abiding in the four noble lineages: being content with mere poor-quality Dharma robes, lodging, and alms, and delighting in abandoning and in meditation. As the *Treasury* says, "Of those noble lineages without attachment..." — just as that was taught, it is extremely powerful.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The second: when one is staying like a hermit in a mountain retreat, whatever alms one obtains should be shared with those who have fallen into wrong ways, that is, animals such as birds and dogs; with the helpless, the beggars; and with the retreatants who keep strict discipline, outer and inner. From one's own share as well, one first sets aside an offering to the Jewels. Then, without attachment or aversion, one eats just enough to satisfy one's hunger, or a fitting measure of fullness, for the sake of nourishing the body, and the remainder one dedicates and gives to the ogresses. This is because if one eats too much one becomes heavy and dull, and if too little one becomes weak and cannot accomplish virtue.

As for the clothing that one holds oneself — the Dharma robe, the lower robe, and the outer robe — apart from the three kinds of Dharma robes, whatever else one has one should give to beggars. The three Dharma robes are not to be given away, because the *Bodhisattva Prātimokṣa* says: "If he would have fewer than three Dharma robes, then relying on what would he abide in the pure life? He should not give them up completely."

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

When partaking of food, if there are those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as animals and hungry ghosts, or the helpless, such as the sick, or those who keep strict discipline, who live the pure life together with oneself, one should share a portion with each of them as well.

"One should also know the right measure oneself in everything." As this says, without the faults that depend on food, one should eat just enough, acting as taught in the *Letter to a Friend* on motivation and so on.

A bodhisattva monk, if he has other requisites apart from the three Dharma robes — the outer robe, the upper robe, and the lower robe — should give them away. Even of the three Dharma robes, if he has surplus requisites he should give them; but if he has only one set, he should not give it, because that would become an obstacle to the pure life.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

This means: from whatever alms one obtains, if there are those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as animals and hungry ghosts, or the helpless, such as beggars and the like, or those who live the pure life and keep strict discipline, one should share a portion with each of them. As for the fourth portion, as the *Letter to a Friend* says, "Like food, like medicine, with reasoning, / rely on it without desire or hatred," one should eat just enough, with that thought, so that the body does not become very weak or heavy. Otherwise, if one makes use of it through attachment and so on and does not strive in virtue, then, as the *Moon Lamp Sutra* says, "Having obtained delicious, excellent food, / yogis who do not strive eat and drink it; / for them that food turns into poison, / like a calf that has not washed the lotus root," it becomes a fault.

This means: if one has requisites apart from those three Dharma robes — the outer robe, the upper robe, and the lower robe that one holds oneself — one gives them away. In the *Bodhisattva Prātimokṣa* it says: "If someone comes and asks for an almsbowl or Dharma robes, then if one has more than the three Dharma robes the Buddha allowed, one should give them up and give them away as appropriate. If one would have fewer than three Dharma robes, one should not give up that relying on which one abides in the pure life. Why is that? Because the Tathāgata has said that the three Dharma robes are not to be given up." This is what was said, so he said.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

When partaking of food in order to protect the body for the sake of others as well, one should divide it into four portions. One portion is for those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as ogresses and hungry ghosts. One is for the helpless, such as the sick, beggars, and the like. One is for those who keep ascetic practice and strict discipline, such as those who live the pure life. And one should eat the last portion oneself, neither too much nor too little, but just enough, measured to the right amount, without attachment or clinging. This is because if one eats too much one becomes heavy and dull, and if too little one becomes weak and cannot accomplish virtue.

In protecting the body with clothing as well, in order to give rise to a sense of shame and decorum, and to guard against harm from flies, mosquitoes, and the like, one should rely on whatever clothes are suitable in common. In particular, one who has gone forth should rely on the Dharma robes, the upper robe and lower robe, and monks should rely on the three, including the outer robe. What is left over beyond these should be given away. The three Dharma robes are not to be given away, because the *Bodhisattva Prātimokṣa* says:

> "If he would have fewer than three Dharma robes, then relying on what would he abide in the pure life? He should not give them up completely."

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Bodhisattvas who have gone forth, who are close to partaking of food, the "medicine" to be practiced constantly, go on the alms round in the village that is their sphere, for the sake of food. Keeping in mind, without contradicting them, the trainings of one who goes for alms as they have been taught, they go. Then, whatever alms they have obtained, they divide into four. One portion is for those who have fallen into wrong ways: hungry ghosts and animals, birds, dogs and the like, both the visible and the invisible. One portion is for the helpless, those who suffer: the sick, beggars, and so on. And if there are any nearby, one portion is for companions who keep strict discipline, who live the pure life and practice austerity. They should share it among these in that way.

One portion they eat themselves, in order to nourish their own body for the sake of others. Even so, in order to abandon the faults that depend on food, they should eat just enough, neither much nor little. If it is too little, hunger and bodily weakness make them unable to bear applying themselves to virtue; so, until tomorrow's mealtime arrives, they should eat an amount at which hunger does no harm. If it is too much, the body feels heavy as if carrying a load, breathing in and out is difficult, and they are overcome by drowsiness and unfit for work, so that they lack the power to rely on the antidotes to the afflictions and it becomes an obstacle to purification. So one must measure it as though one filled one third of the stomach with food and one third with drink, and left one third empty as the place for the movement of the winds. As it is put in the *Compendium of Training*: *(Editorial note: the rail does not reproduce the quotation here.)*

In regard to that, taking the case of the constantly relied-upon medicine, the noble *Cloud of Jewels* says: *(Editorial note: the rail does not reproduce the quotation here.)*

and then the trainings for the time of going for alms and so on, as taught in the *Heap of Jewels Sutra*, are shown there extensively and should be learned from it. Again, from the same source: "Know the right measure in everything." As this says, one must measure the amount of food, neither too much nor too little. Besides neither too much nor too little but just enough, it is taught that knowing the measure of food has four qualities: the food should be digestible, it should agree with one, and it should be non-afflictive and of a suitable amount. Being digestible and agreeable means abandoning the old painful sensations produced by food and not producing new ones. Non-afflictive food of a suitable amount means that it does not produce faults and one remains in comfortable contact.

By knowing the measure in this way, one is endowed with the abandoning of eight faults: excessive attachment to food, attachment to the body, blocking of the life-force, hunger and weakness, bodily heaviness, giving rise to illness, impure livelihood, and distraction through many tasks. "Therefore eat what is beneficial and sufficient; / fix the time of meals." This teaches that measure is needed, because lack of measure produces faults.

As for the thoughts to hold at mealtimes: the motivation is that one must eat thinking, "I will benefit the donor," and "now I gather the worms of the body with material things, and in the future I will gather them with the Dharma," and "I will accomplish the benefit of all beings." As for perceptions: in general, according to the Vinaya, one must hold the perception of oneself as a patient tormented by hunger and thirst, and of food as the medicine that removes it. In particular it is taught that ten perceptions must be relied on. (1) The perception of arising from the impure: thinking that food comes from impure things such as dung. (2) The perception of likeness: rice looks like the worm-like young of ants; yogurt like human brain; flour like flour of human bones; meat like the flesh of a child; and so on. (3) The perception of becoming: thinking that what is eaten turns into waste, phlegm, mucus, and so on. (4) The perception of opposition: even father and son, mother and daughter quarrel for the sake of food. (5) The perception of corresponding cause: from eating food there arise one's impure self, feces, urine, and so on, which are utterly disgusting. (6) The perception of dependent arising: eating food draws in afflictions such as attachment. (7) The perception of difficulty of finding: one has to seek food with great hardship. (8) The perception of reliance: one has to depend on other helps, such as trickery (གཡོ་སྐོལ). (9) The perception of being accompanied by illness: not digesting, or digesting wrongly, produces various illnesses. (10) The perception of going separate ways: of what is eaten, one part is burned by the body's fire, one part fattens the body, one part becomes worms, and one part becomes feces, urine, and so on, each going its own way. As for the way of giving or not giving clothing: when someone asks a bodhisattva who has gone forth for clothing or other requisites, then of the Dharma robes that one wears oneself — the upper robe, the Dharma robe wa-chim (ཝ་ཕྱིམ), and the lower garment — and in particular, if one is like a monk, the Dharma robe that is the outer robe, apart from those three Dharma robes, whatever requisites one has should be given. Surplus clothing should be given, even if it is among the three Dharma robes and so on. But the one set of the three saffron-colored Dharma robes that one keeps close to oneself should not be given, even if someone asks. This is because they set one's marks apart from householders, non-Buddhists, and the like; because they revive the mindfulness of a renunciant who lives the pure life; because they protect against the touch of heat and cold and against harm from flies, mosquitoes, and so on. In particular, the outer robe, even for those who have gone forth but are not fully ordained, such as novices, distinguishes their marks, gives rise to a special sense of shame and decorum, and is to be held inseparably, to the extent of becoming a stupa, an object of offering together with the gods; so it should not be given. — thus, in general, material giving is done by the bodhisattva householder, and the gift of the Dharma by the bodhisattva who has gone forth. As for how to make use of clothing: in the Vinaya, in general, one must use it holding the perception of the body as a wound and of clothing as a bandage for the wound. In particular, one such as a renunciant must wear saffron robes, as explained above, for the sake of reviving the mindfulness of a renunciant and giving rise to a sense of shame and decorum, so it is taught. — and so on was taught. These show that in general one must wear whatever clothing is fitting in common, and in particular, if one is a renunciant, one must wear the saffron robes with reverence, with the thought of keeping the marks of a renunciant from deteriorating and making them firm. If one forgets the perception of being directed toward others' benefit, being free of craving, or if one becomes attached for the sake of some other being, it becomes a non-afflictive downfall. If one has such an intention, there is no fault even in using requisites dedicated to beings without permission; and the ways in which it is not right to lose faith, thinking, "if it is not actually given to beings, what was given up in the mind appears to be false," are also taught in the *Compendium of Training*, and one should learn them from there.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

A bodhisattva who has gone forth — one who has the support of a novice or a fully ordained monk and the like — and who lives on alms divides into four portions the alms that fill his almsbowl each day.

One portion he gives to those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as animals, birds, and dogs. One portion he gives to the helpless: the hungry, beggars. One portion he shares with those who keep strict discipline: companions in the pure life, Dharma practitioners, the sick, and the like. As for the last portion, when he eats it himself he eats just enough, for if he eats too much the body becomes heavy, and if too little the body becomes weak and cannot engage in the Dharma. So he should eat neither too much nor too little but just enough. If it happens that he has more, he is permitted, incidentally, that much only: to give even a little *changbu* (ཆང་བུ) to the yakṣiṇī, the ogress and her children.

Of clothing, there are the Dharma robes that protect one's own body: the lower robe, the upper robe, and the outer robe. Those three themselves may not be given to others. If one obtains any surplus clothing apart from them, one should give it. In general, for a bodhisattva who has gone forth, material giving is not permitted, and it is taught that the gift of the Dharma must be the main thing. As for material giving, if one has trifles such as paper, ink, and writing reeds, the Buddha likewise taught that they may be given.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Dividing the Alms into Four Portions

From alms and other food obtained, dividing it into four portions: one given to those who have fallen into wrong ways, one to the helpless, and one to those who keep strict discipline, while one portion is eaten oneself, just enough.

###### 2. Knowing the Measure of Food

Knowing the measure of food and eating it neither too much nor too little, but just enough.

###### 3. Giving Away the Surplus Clothing Beyond the Three Dharma Robes

Giving to others, without attachment, the surplus clothing beyond the three Dharma robes.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** A bodhisattva must share the alms he obtains with others and, by measuring it himself, eat just enough. One who has gone forth should give to others whatever surplus he has beyond the three Dharma robes.

**Key points.**

- The foods obtained from alms should be shared with those who have fallen into wrong ways, such as animals and hungry ghosts; with the helpless, beggars and the sick; and with those who keep strict discipline in the pure life.
- One should eat one's own share, without attachment or aversion, just enough, neither too much nor too little.
- The three Dharma robes must be held inseparably, like one's skin, and not given away; the surplus clothing apart from them should be given.
- For one who has gone forth, material giving is not permitted, so the gift of the Dharma must be made the main thing.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

The Blessed One's followers, regarding how to rely on livelihood, are forbidden the extreme of being too fine and lavish, and that is not suitable; and it is also taught that the extreme of being too weary and worn out is not suitable either. This good body-support, which can practice the profound and vast sacred Dharma, is not to be given such trifling harms as, for example, other brahmins relying on the five fires, or the naked ascetics pulling out the hair of the body and so on; it is taught that one should not do such trifling harm to the body, as part of that kind of practice.

Then what should be done? "If one does so, the aspirations of beings / will be quickly fulfilled." Relying on this support, if one accomplishes the benefit of others with whatever power of the three doors one has, the accumulations will be easily completed; therefore one should do these.

*(Editorial note: the rail's block opens with "ཞེས་" ("thus") and quotes the lines "If one does so..." without naming their source; it is translated as given.)*

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Here too, at this time when one is a beginner bodhisattva, this body of one's own, which properly practices the sacred Dharma, should not be harmed — by cutting off a hand and the like — for the sake of some trifling benefit to others. Rather, with just enough food and clothing to fill the belly and protect, not falling into the two extremes, one protects one's own body for the sake of other beings. Having practiced the conduct of a bodhisattva, one must dedicate the virtue to omniscience for the sake of beings, make aspiration prayers, and work for the benefit of beings. This is because, if one does so, gradually all the aspirations of all beings for temporary and ultimate benefit and happiness will be quickly fulfilled.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One's own body has become the support for practicing the sacred Dharma, by guarding which the great aims of oneself and others are accomplished. It should not be harmed for the sake of some trifling benefit to others, but should be protected like a wound.

This is because, if one does so, relying on the freedoms and endowments, through completing the three trainings, the aspirations of beings will be quickly fulfilled. As the *Four Hundred* says: "Though one sees the body as an enemy, / even so one should protect it. / A person with ethics who lives long / creates from it great merit." It is as this says.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

The body that practices the sacred Dharma should not be harmed for the sake of some trifling benefit to others. If one protects in this way this body that practices the Dharma, then gradually the aspirations of beings will be quickly fulfilled. As the *Four Hundred* says: "Though one sees this body as an enemy, / even so one should protect it. / A person with ethics who lives long / creates from it great merit." So it was said.

One should not actually give up this body when the intention of compassion is impure, that is, when one has not reached the ground of pure superior intention. This is because it is not certain that it would benefit others, and because regret, turning back of the mind, and obstacles to virtue may arise. In the *Compendium of Training* too: "For example, a medicinal tree, a tree beautiful to see, / its roots and so on, and seeds that are fit to be prepared, / even if given, should be guarded from use at the wrong time; / the perfect buddha, the medicinal tree, is just so." So it was said.

Here, in some versions of the text, "With an equal intention one should give it up" appears, and Namnang Tsho (རྣམ་སྣང་འཚོ) did not comment on it. If one asks, "If one does not give up the body, is that not contrary to one's pledge?" — it is not. The *Detailed Exposition* says: "In whatever way, so that in this life and in others it may become the cause of accomplishing great benefit for others, one should give it up into the keeping (དྲན་དུ, as the rail has it) of others." Then, when the intention is pure, one should give it up as is fitting.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-144]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

This very body, which properly practices and puts into practice the sacred Dharma, should not be tormented by harm from heat, cold, and the like, by being without Dharma robes, for the sake of some trifling small need. For if one did so, one would fall away from the great aim. And if one protects the body for the sake of others in that way, the aspirations of beings who wish for benefit and happiness will be quickly fulfilled.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The sacred Dharma of the bodhisattva is both "sacred" and "Dharma" because it is what accomplishes the great benefit and happiness of self and others, worldly and beyond the world. The bodhisattva's body has become the support for properly practicing and putting into practice that sacred Dharma. For the time being, one should not torment it with the suffering of such harm as giving it away, for the sake of some small benefit to others or some trifling need, to every flesh-eater and blood-drinker. Nor, failing to act as taught above on the use of food, clothing, and so on, should one scorch and torment oneself with pointless harm — by not relying on food and drink and so applying oneself to hunger and thirst, or by not wearing clothes and so exposing oneself to the touch of heat and cold. If one does, one falls away from the great aims of self and others, so the body must be protected with cherishing care.

Thus, one should not harm the bodhisattva's body, the source of merit, for the sake of trifling needs, but should protect it like the sprout of a medicinal tree. Relying on it, if one trains properly in the practices of the bodhisattva heirs — the six perfections, the four ways of gathering disciples, and so on — one will be able to bring about the quick fulfillment of beings' aspirations for temporary and ultimate benefit and happiness, just as they wish.

Not giving the body for trifling needs is the way of not giving it up from the standpoint of need. Moreover, it is taught that one should not give the body even to one who asks for it in order to make one commit harmful misdeeds that injure oneself or others, such as killing. For this reason, even for one who has made self and others equal, as long as the compassionate intention of cherishing others more than oneself is not fully pure, one should not actually give up this body and life through application. This is because it is not certain that giving would benefit others, because the hardship of giving may bring disheartenment, and thereby a turning back from the conduct of the bodhisattva heirs, and because regret may also arise later.

Therefore, when one has parted from the obstacles to giving, such as miserliness, one's superior intention is fully pure and has grown, and, from the others' side, giving up the body will surely accomplish the benefit of many beings and become the cause of completing the vast accumulations of self and others, then in whatever way, one will certainly give up this body completely, as a cause for accomplishing the great benefit and happiness of self and others, in this life and in other lives. In the Indian edition it reads: "If the intention is equal, give it up; / thus there will be no complete decline." This says: when one can hold everything with an equal superior intention, or can cherish others and the Dharma, one should give up one's life. When one has gained such skill in means, there is no turning back, no regret, and so on, and so one does not fall completely from awakening.

To gather what the *Detailed Exposition* teaches on this point: "compassionate intention" and so on are mentioned. As for not having reached the ground of pure superior intention: one does not give up the body, either because, not being accustomed to giving, one cannot give; or, even if one can, because earlier, through the power of bad karma, illness and the like arise, so that one regrets and is dispirited; or because, not having attained the samādhi of going happily in all phenomena, pleasant feeling does not arise at that time and so the mind wishing to give does not arise; or because, the power of truth not being accomplished, there may be obstacles to life and to the pure life. But for one who has reached the ground of pure superior intention, those faults do not arise, so it is only then that it is the time for giving. So it is taught.

Gelha (དགེ་ལྷ) says: if ordinary beings should not even give up the three Dharma robes, what need is there to mention life? To show this, "compassionate intention" and so on were said. Since the body that practices the Dharma should not be harmed for the sake of anything else, one should not give up the body with an impure compassionate intention. It is explained that even some ordinary beings who practice through devotion, if they have the equal mind of self and others, and give up their life, do not decline or fall, because in this way they understand the difference. And Vibhūti (བི་བྷཱུ་ཏི) says that one who has an unequal compassionate intention toward kin and non-kin should not give, but when one is even-minded toward all one should give; at that time there is no regret and so on, and so one does not decline.

"That is the meaning of 'having attained the path of seeing,'" it was said. Thus, not giving up the body without pure compassionate intention is the way of not giving it up from the standpoint of time. Even though from the beginning a bodhisattva has given body and so on for the sake of beings, until the intention of great compassion has grown such that he would not be disheartened by actually giving, for example, flesh from his body, he should not give it even if asked. So in the *Compendium of Training*:

> "Because one who has undertaken effort so intensely in that way becomes disheartened, he does not constantly remain a bodhisattva in fact. Therefore, in the *Cloud of Jewels*: 'Here, when a bodhisattva undertakes effort in all modes of conduct, undertaking effort in such a way that the body does not become weary and the mind does not become weary, this is called the constant effort of a bodhisattva.' So it is taught. If one asks what sort of effort it is that leads to weariness: it is undertaking hard tasks that are heavier than one's weak strength, or that go on for a long time, or for which one's devotion has not fully matured, such as giving one's own flesh. By this, although one has given the body to beings, one only turns them back from using it at the wrong time. Otherwise, the bodhisattva would become weary toward those beings, and because the seed of the awakening mind would then be wasted, a great mass of fruit would be wasted."

Therefore conduct must be related to the right time, and effort at the wrong time not only fails to become a cause of benefit to self and others but can even become an obstacle to virtue. In the *Compendium of Training*, in the citation of the *Treasury of Space Sutra*: "To wish at the wrong time is the work of Māra." Since it is taught that it becomes the work of the harming Māra, one must properly know the time for giving, the purpose of giving, and so on, and then give.

For giving after having known the time in this way, there is no fault of contradicting one's earlier pledge, and there is also no room for miserliness, regret, and so on. Therefore, even at the stage of a beginner bodhisattva, whatever things one possesses — body, enjoyments, roots of virtue, and so on — although one has given them to beings wholeheartedly, one must still protect them, purify them, increase them inexhaustibly, and so on. In particular, the body that has been given must also be protected from being used at the wrong time, and the great benefit of protecting it is shown in the *Four Hundred*:

*(Editorial note: the rail ends the block here, at "the Four Hundred," without giving the quotation.)*

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

One who practices the sacred Dharma oneself — this very special body-support, complete with the eight freedoms and ten endowments for practicing the Dharma — should not be harmed for the sake of trifling needs, for instance by cutting flesh from one's own body in order to feed living beings such as worms and insects. This is the meaning.

As this was said: "Though one sees the body as an enemy, / even so one should protect it." Having sustained this body-support just enough with food and so on, one strives and accomplishes virtue oneself, and dedicates it for the benefit of others, and so on. If one does so, the aspirations of beings will also be quickly fulfilled.

As the noble Āryadeva (འཕགས་པ་ལྷ) said: "The basis for a person with ethics to live long: / from that great merit is generated."

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** If one protects this body, the support for practicing the sacred Dharma, and accomplishes the benefit of others, the aspirations and accumulations of beings will be quickly fulfilled. Therefore this body that practices the sacred Dharma should not be harmed for the sake of trifling needs.

**Key points.**

- As examples of trifling harm — cutting off a hand, cutting flesh to feed worms and insects, relying on the five fires, pulling out the hair — these should not be done.
- This body should be protected like a wound or like a medicinal tree, and nourished just enough to fill the belly, or with food and so on in suitable measure.
- The *Four Hundred* teaches that though one may see the body as an enemy, it should be protected; a person with ethics who lives long creates great merit from it.
- Since the bodhisattva's body is a source of merit, one does not give it up for trifles; yet there is no fault in giving it once one knows the right time.
- The two extremes — being too fine and lavish, and being too weary and worn out — are both unsuitable; if one is tormented by heat and cold for the sake of some small trifling purpose, one will fall away from the great purpose.
