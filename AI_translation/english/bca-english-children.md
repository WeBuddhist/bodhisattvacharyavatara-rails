# The Bodhisattva's Way ^0

## Opening Words ^I-0

In the language of India: Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra ^I-1

In the language of Tibet: The Bodhisattva's Way ^I-2

I bow down to the Buddhas and to all the Bodhisattvas. ^I-3

## 1. Chapter One: Explaining the Benefits of Bodhicitta ^1-0

I bow down with respect to the Sugatas, who hold the body of true teaching,
and to their children, and to everyone else worth bowing to.
Now, following the scriptures,
I will explain simply how to enter the way of the Sugatas' children. ^1-1

I have nothing new to say here that wasn't said before,
and I am not skilled at writing beautiful words.
So I am not writing this because I think it will help others —
I wrote it only to help my own mind grow used to these teachings. ^1-2

Writing this will help my own strength of faith grow for a while,
since it helps me practice goodness.
And if someone else, a bit like me,
happens to see these words, maybe it will help them too. ^1-3

This freedom and good luck we have now is very hard to find.
Since we have it, and it lets us do something truly meaningful,
if we do not make good use of it now,
how could we ever find such a chance again? ^1-4

Just as on a dark night, thick with clouds,
a flash of lightning lights everything up for just a moment,
in the same way, through the power of the Buddhas,
only once in a great while does a wise thought of goodness arise in the world. ^1-5

So notice: goodness is always weak,
while wrongdoing is powerful and terrible.
What goodness could ever overcome it,
except bodhicitta, the wish to wake up fully? ^1-6

Thinking deeply about this for countless ages,
the sages saw that this alone truly helps.
Through it, countless people
can reach true happiness, safely and easily. ^1-7

If you want to end the hundreds of sufferings of endless rebirth,
if you want to clear away the unhappiness of beings,
and if you want them to enjoy many kinds of happiness,
then never give up bodhicitta. ^1-8

The moment bodhicitta is born,
even someone suffering, bound in the prison of endless rebirth,
comes to be called a child of the Sugatas,
and is honored even by the gods and humans of the world. ^1-9

It is like the finest potion that turns things into gold:
it takes this unclean body and turns it into the body of a Buddha,
a priceless jewel beyond all price.
So hold onto this thing called bodhicitta very firmly. ^1-10

The only guides of beings, with their endless wisdom,
looked closely at this and saw how precious it is.
So all of you who want to be free from endless rebirth,
hold firmly to this precious bodhicitta. ^1-11

All other goodness is like a banana tree:
once it gives its fruit, it withers away.
But the tree of bodhicitta
always gives fruit, and never withers — it just keeps growing. ^1-12

Even someone who has done terrible wrongdoing
can escape it in an instant by relying on bodhicitta,
the way relying on a hero saves you from great danger.
So why would a careful person not rely on it? ^1-13

Like the fire that ends an entire age,
it burns away great wrongdoing in a single moment.
Its benefits are truly endless —
the wise and loving protector explained them well to Sudhana. ^1-14

To sum up briefly, this bodhicitta
should be understood as two kinds:
the wish to wake up fully,
and actually acting to get there. ^1-15

Just as we understand the difference
between wanting to go somewhere and actually going,
in the same way, the wise understand
the difference between these two, one after the other. ^1-16

From the wish to wake up fully,
great results come, even while still in the cycle of rebirth,
but goodness does not flow on without a break
the way it does with the mind that actually acts. ^1-17

From the moment someone, in order to free
every being without limit, completely,
takes up a mind that will never turn back —
that is when bodhicitta has been truly taken up. ^1-18

From that moment on, even while asleep,
or even careless and not paying attention,
a great and constant power of goodness
keeps rising up, as vast and constant as the sky. ^1-19

This is true, and makes good sense —
when Subāhu asked about it,
the Buddha himself explained it this way,
for the sake of beings still not sure of themselves. ^1-20

Just wishing to clear away
even a simple headache for beings,
if you have that kind of caring wish,
you already gain endless goodness. ^1-21

Then what need is there even to mention
wanting to clear away the endless unhappiness
of every single being,
and wanting each one to gain endless good qualities? ^1-22

Does even a father or a mother
have a wish to help like this?
Do even the gods or the wise sages have it?
Does even Brahmā have it? ^1-23

Those very beings, in the past,
never even dreamed of such a wish
for their own sake —
so how could it ever arise for the sake of others? ^1-24

This wish for the good of beings, which others
never even have for their own sake,
is a truly special jewel of the mind,
a wonder never seen before, now born. ^1-25

It is the cause of joy for every being,
the medicine for the suffering of beings —
how could the goodness of this precious mind
ever be measured? ^1-26

If just wishing to help beings
is already better than making offerings to the Buddhas,
then what need is there to even mention
actually working hard for the happiness of every single being? ^1-27

Even though beings want to escape suffering,
they run straight toward suffering itself.
Even though they want happiness,
out of confusion, they destroy their own happiness like an enemy. ^1-28

For those who lack happiness
and have so much suffering,
satisfying them with every happiness,
and cutting off all their suffering, ^1-29

and even clearing away their confusion —
what goodness could ever equal that?
What friend could ever be like that?
What goodness could ever be like that? ^1-30

Even someone who simply pays back
a kindness done to them deserves some praise.
So what need is there to even mention
the bodhisattva, who does good without even being asked? ^1-31

The world calls someone kind and good
just for once giving a little plain food to a few beings,
filling their stomachs for half a day, even with a bit of scorn —
people say that person has done something good. ^1-32

So what need is there to even mention
giving, for countless beings, for a very very long time,
the very best happiness of the Sugatas,
fulfilling every wish in their hearts, always? ^1-33

The Buddha himself has said
that if someone has an unkind thought
toward such a generous child of the Buddhas,
they will stay in hell for as many ages as the unkind thoughts they had. ^1-34

But if someone's heart fills with joy toward them instead,
the result grows even greater than that.
Even when great harm comes to the children of the Buddhas,
no wrongdoing arises; goodness simply keeps growing on its own. ^1-35

I bow down to the body of anyone
in whom this precious, wonderful mind has been born,
and I go for refuge to that source of happiness,
who brings happiness even to those who harm them. ^1-36

This is the end of the first chapter, called "Explaining the Benefits of Bodhicitta," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^1-a

## 2. Chapter Two: Confessing Wrongdoing ^2-0

In order to take hold of that precious mind, bodhicitta,
I make good offerings to the Tathāgatas, to the true teachings,
to the stainless Three Jewels, and to the children of the Buddhas,
and to all whose good qualities are as vast as an ocean. ^2-1

Every flower and fruit there is,
every kind of medicine there is,
every jewel there is in the world,
and every clear, lovely water there is — ^2-2

every jeweled mountain, and in the same way,
every quiet, delightful forest and place,
every tree decorated with flowers,
and every branch bending under good fruit — ^2-3

every scent among the gods and in the world,
incense, wish-granting trees, and jeweled trees,
every kind of crop that grows without being planted,
and every other beautiful thing worth offering — ^2-4

lakes and pools adorned with lotus flowers,
with swans whose calls are so sweet and lovely,
reaching to the very edges of endless space —
all of these, belonging to no one, ^2-5

I take up in my mind, and offer them well
to the Buddha, the finest of beings, and to his children.
Please, you fields of offering, so worthy, so full of compassion,
think of me with love, and accept these gifts of mine. ^2-6

I have no goodness, I am so very poor,
I have no other wealth to offer.
So please, protector who thinks only of others,
accept this, through your own power, for my own sake. ^2-7

To the Victor and to his children, I will
forever offer up my whole body.
Please, finest of beings, accept me completely —
with respect, I will come to serve you humbly. ^2-8

Since you have taken me in completely,
I will bring no fear to any being in this life,
I will go beyond my past wrongdoing,
and I will never do wrongdoing again from now on. ^2-9

In a bathing room, so sweetly scented,
with a floor of clear, shining crystal,
with beautiful pillars blazing with jewels,
hung with a canopy glowing with pearls, ^2-10

to the Tathāgatas and to their children,
I offer a bath, with many jeweled vases
filled well with lovely, scented water,
accompanied by songs and music. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with cloths
unmatched, clean, and scented with the finest perfume.
Then I offer them
beautiful, sweetly scented, wonderful robes. ^2-12

With soft, fine, many kinds of beautiful clothes,
and with hundreds of the finest ornaments,
I decorate noble Samantabhadra, Mañjughoṣa,
Avalokiteśvara, and all the others. ^2-13

With the finest perfumes, whose scent
spreads through all three thousand worlds,
I anoint the bodies of all the Buddhas,
shining bright like pure, polished gold. ^2-14

To the Buddha, the finest field of offering,
I offer lovely mandārava flowers, lotus flowers,
blue lotus flowers, and every sweetly scented flower,
arranged into beautiful, well-made garlands. ^2-15

I also offer clouds of incense,
so fine, their scent carries everywhere.
And I offer heavenly feasts,
with all kinds of food and drink. ^2-16

I offer golden lotus flowers
arranged in rows, and precious lamps too.
On the ground, anointed with perfume,
I scatter lovely flower petals. ^2-17

To those whose very nature is compassion,
I offer boundless palaces, filled with beautiful songs of praise,
hung with strings of pearls and jewels, radiant and lovely,
adorning the vastness of the sky itself. ^2-18

I always offer to the Buddha
beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles,
their rims so lovely,
lifted up high, beautiful to see. ^2-19

And beyond all this, may every other offering,
with sweet, lovely music,
form into clouds that satisfy the suffering of beings,
and remain there, each in its own place. ^2-20

On the true teachings, on the Three Jewels,
on stupas, and on sacred images,
may showers of precious flowers and more
fall down without ever stopping. ^2-21

Just as Mañjughoṣa and others
make offerings to the Buddhas,
in that same way, I too offer
to the Tathāgatas, the protectors, and their children. ^2-22

To those whose good qualities are as vast as an ocean,
I offer praise with an ocean of praises.
May clouds of sweet praise for them
rise up, without ever failing, everywhere. ^2-23

To all the Buddhas of the three times,
to the teachings, and to the finest community,
I bow down, with my body,
as many times as there are specks of dust in the world. ^2-24

I bow down to every ground where bodhicitta arose,
and to every stupa,
I bow down to my abbots, my teachers,
and to all who hold the finest discipline. ^2-25

Until I reach the heart of awakening,
I go for refuge to the Buddhas.
In the same way, I go for refuge
to the teachings, and to the community of bodhisattvas. ^2-26

To the fully awakened Buddhas dwelling
in every direction, and to bodhicitta,
and to those who hold great compassion,
I join my hands and pray: ^2-27

Since beginningless time, through endless rebirth,
in this life or in others,
I have, without knowing, done wrongdoing,
or made others do it, ^2-28

overpowered by confusion and delusion.
Whatever I rejoiced in that was wrong,
now that I see this fault clearly,
from the depths of my heart, I confess it to the protectors. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have done, driven by disturbing emotions,
with my body, my speech, or my mind,
toward the Three Jewels,
toward my parents, or toward other teachers, ^2-30

whatever terrible wrongdoing I, so full of faults,
stained by so many mistakes, have done —
all of it, I confess
to all the guides. ^2-31

Before I have cleared away this wrongdoing,
death may come for me first.
So please, quickly,
protect me, so I may be truly freed from it. ^2-32

This Lord of Death cannot be trusted;
he does not wait for things done or undone.
Whether sick or well, none of us
can trust that this fleeting life will last. ^2-33

Since I must leave everything behind and go,
and did not understand this,
I have done all kinds of wrongdoing,
for the sake of those I love and those I do not. ^2-34

Those I dislike will one day be gone,
those I love will also be gone,
and I myself will also be gone —
in the same way, everything will be gone. ^2-35

Just as in a dream we experience things,
whatever we once made use of,
becomes only a memory —
we can never see anything that has already passed. ^2-36

Even in this one life,
so many I loved and did not love have already passed away,
yet the terrible wrongdoing I did for their sake
still stands right in front of me. ^2-37

So, not having understood
that I myself am so fleeting,
out of confusion, attachment, and anger,
I have done so many kinds of wrongdoing. ^2-38

Never resting, day or night,
this life is always slipping away,
and nothing extra ever comes to replace it —
so how could someone like me not die? ^2-39

Even while I lie in my bed,
surrounded by all my family and friends,
the feeling of my life force being cut
I will have to experience completely alone. ^2-40

When caught by the messengers of death,
what use are relatives, what use are friends?
At that time, only goodness can protect me,
and that is exactly what I have not relied on. ^2-41

O protectors, I, so careless,
not knowing this kind of danger was coming,
for the sake of this fleeting life,
have built up so much wrongdoing. ^2-42

Even someone being led today to a place
where their limbs will be cut off grows pale with fear —
their mouth goes dry, their eyes grow dull and strange,
their whole face changes from before. ^2-43

So what need is there to even mention
how utterly overwhelmed I will be,
caught by the terrifying, fearsome messengers of death,
struck down by a great and frightening sickness? ^2-44

"Who can truly protect me
from this great fear?" I will think,
and with wide, terrified eyes,
I will search in every direction for refuge. ^2-45

Seeing no refuge anywhere,
I will fall into complete despair.
And if there is truly no refuge to be found there,
what will I do then? ^2-46

So, from this very day,
I go for refuge to the Victors, protectors of the world,
who work hard to protect all beings,
whose great power clears away every fear. ^2-47

And in the same way, I truly go for refuge
to the teaching they have realized in their hearts,
which clears away the fear of endless rebirth,
and to the community of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Trembling with fear,
I offer myself to Samantabhadra.
And to Mañjughoṣa also,
I offer up my own body. ^2-49

To that protector Avalokiteśvara too,
whose compassionate actions never fail,
I cry out in anguish, in a voice full of pain:
please protect me, so full of wrongdoing. ^2-50

To noble Ākāśagarbha,
to Kṣitigarbha,
and to all the protectors, so full of great compassion,
I cry out from my heart, searching for refuge. ^2-51

To Vajrapāṇi, seeing whom
the wrathful messengers of death and other such beings
grow afraid and flee in every direction,
I go for refuge. ^2-52

In the past, I ignored your words.
But now, seeing this great danger,
I go for refuge to you.
Please, quickly, clear away this fear. ^2-53

Even when afraid of an ordinary sickness,
we must follow a doctor's advice —
so what need is there to even mention
being struck again and again by the sickness of desire and a hundred other faults? ^2-54

If that alone is enough to destroy
every person living in this world,
and no other medicine to heal it
can be found anywhere, ^2-55

then to ignore the advice
of the one who knows all medicine,
who can remove every kind of pain —
that is truly a shameful, deeply confused thing to do. ^2-56

If I must be careful
even at the edge of an ordinary, small cliff,
then what need is there to even mention
the endless cliff that drops a thousand miles down? ^2-57

It is not right to relax comfortably, thinking,
"I surely will not die today."
The time when I will cease to exist
will certainly come. ^2-58

Who can give me real fearlessness?
"How can I be truly freed from this?" I wonder.
Since it is certain I will one day no longer exist,
how could my mind possibly stay at ease? ^2-59

What is left for me now
of all I once enjoyed, now gone?
Yet clinging tightly to it,
I went against the words of my teacher. ^2-60

In this life, and in the same way in the next,
I must leave behind every relative and friend,
and go on alone, wherever I am led —
so what use, then, are those I love and those I do not? ^2-61

"Suffering comes from wrongdoing.
How can I be truly freed from this?"
This alone is what I should be thinking,
always, day and night. ^2-62

Out of ignorance and confusion,
whatever wrongdoing I have done,
whether wrong by its very nature,
or wrong because it was forbidden, ^2-63

all of it, before the protectors,
with my hands joined, my heart full of fear of suffering,
bowing down again and again,
I confess completely. ^2-64

Guides, please accept
my wrongdoing and my mistakes as they are.
Since none of this was good,
I will never do it again. ^2-65

This is the end of the second chapter, called "Confessing Wrongdoing," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^2-a

## 3. Chapter Three: Fully Taking Hold of Bodhicitta ^3-0

I rejoice with joy in the goodness
that lets beings rest from the suffering of hard rebirths,
and I rejoice
that those who suffer come to dwell in happiness. ^3-1

I rejoice in the goodness gathered
that becomes the cause of awakening.
I rejoice
that beings are truly freed from the suffering of endless rebirth.
And I rejoice in the awakening of the protectors,
and in the levels reached by the children of the Buddhas. ^3-2

I rejoice with joy
in the ocean of goodness that is bodhicitta, which brings happiness to every being,
and in every act
that truly helps beings. ^3-3

To the Buddhas in every direction,
I join my hands and pray:
for beings lost in the darkness of suffering,
please light the lamp of the true teachings. ^3-4

To the Buddhas who wish to pass beyond suffering,
I join my hands and pray:
please do not leave this world blind,
please stay for countless ages. ^3-5

Through doing all of this,
whatever goodness I have gathered,
through it, may every being
be freed from all their suffering. ^3-6

For as long as beings are sick,
until they are healed,
may I become their medicine, their doctor,
and the one who cares for them in their sickness. ^3-7

May I pour down a rain of food and drink,
and clear away the suffering of hunger and thirst.
During the ages of famine in between,
may I myself become food and drink. ^3-8

For beings who are poor and have nothing,
may I become an endless treasure,
staying close before them,
as every kind of thing they need. ^3-9

My body, and in the same way, all I own,
and all the goodness of the three times,
I will give away completely, without holding back,
to accomplish the good of every being. ^3-10

Giving up everything is what passes beyond suffering,
and my mind aims for that very peace.
Since I must give up everything at once anyway,
it is best to give it all to beings. ^3-11

I have already given this body
freely to every being, in whatever way pleases them.
Let them always kill it, criticize it,
beat it, or do whatever else they wish. ^3-12

Even if they play with my body,
or make it the object of mockery and jokes,
since I have already given this body away,
what use is there in caring what happens to it? ^3-13

Let them do to it
whatever action does not truly harm them.
May no one who thinks of me
ever have that thought be without meaning. ^3-14

Whoever, thinking of me,
has a thought of anger or a thought of faith arise,
may that very thought always become
the cause that accomplishes everything good for them. ^3-15

May everyone who speaks badly of me,
who harms me,
or who mocks me,
all of them, have the good fortune to reach awakening. ^3-16

May I be a protector for those without a protector,
a guide for those on the road,
a boat for those who want to cross over,
a raft, and a bridge. ^3-17

May I be an island for those seeking an island,
a bed for those who want a bed,
and may I become a servant
to every being who wants a servant. ^3-18

May I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, a fine vase,
a powerful mantra, a great medicine,
a wish-granting tree,
and give beings everything they wish for. ^3-19

Just like earth and the other great elements,
and like the sky, may I always,
for countless beings,
become the ground that sustains their many kinds of life. ^3-20

In the same way, for beings
as vast and endless as the edges of space,
in every way, until they all pass beyond suffering,
may I be the cause that sustains their lives. ^3-21

Just as the Sugatas of the past
gave rise to the mind of awakening,
and just as they, one after another,
trained in the practice of a bodhisattva, ^3-22

in the same way, for the benefit of beings,
I will give rise to bodhicitta,
and in the same way,
I will train in its practice, step by step. ^3-23

In this way, whoever, with a wise mind,
takes up bodhicitta with true clarity,
in order to help it keep growing to the very end,
should praise their own mind like this: ^3-24

Today, my life has finally borne fruit.
I have truly made the most of this human life.
Today I am born into the family of the Buddhas;
today I have become a child of the Buddhas. ^3-25

Now, no matter what,
I will act in a way that fits this family,
and I will make sure never to bring any stain
to this flawless, noble family. ^3-26

Just as a blind person
might find a jewel in a heap of dust,
in that same way, by some rare stroke of luck,
this bodhicitta has arisen in me. ^3-27

This is the very best nectar,
the nectar that defeats the Lord of Death for beings.
It is the endless treasure
that clears away the poverty of beings. ^3-28

It is the finest medicine
that fully calms the sickness of beings.
It is the tree of rest
for beings wandering, exhausted, on the road of endless rebirth. ^3-29

It is the bridge that carries
every being across, away from hard rebirths.
It is the rising moon of the mind
that clears away the burning pain of beings' disturbing emotions. ^3-30

It is the great sun
that fully clears away the blinding fog of beings' ignorance.
It is the rich cream
that rises when the milk of the true teachings is churned. ^3-31

For every traveling guest journeying the road of endless rebirth,
who wishes to enjoy the comforts of happiness,
this bodhicitta stays close by, as the very best happiness,
and satisfies every guest, every being, completely. ^3-32

Today, before all the protectors,
I have invited every being to become a Sugata,
and, in the meantime, to happiness as well.
So let the gods, demigods, and everyone else rejoice! ^3-33

This is the end of the third chapter, called "Fully Taking Hold of Bodhicitta," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^3-a

## 4. Chapter Four: Being Careful and Mindful ^4-0

In this way, a child of the Buddhas,
having taken firm hold of bodhicitta,
should never be careless,
and should always work hard never to stray from this training. ^4-1

Whatever is begun carelessly,
or without thinking it through properly,
even if a promise was made about it,
it makes sense to think carefully about whether to continue or let it go. ^4-2

But whatever the Buddhas and their children
have examined with their great wisdom,
and whatever I myself have examined carefully too,
why would I ever turn back from that? ^4-3

If I make such a promise
and then do not carry it out in action,
I will have deceived every one of those beings —
what will happen to me then? ^4-4

It is said that even someone who, in their mind,
thinks of giving away some small, ordinary thing,
but then does not actually give it,
will be reborn as a hungry ghost because of it. ^4-5

So if, with a sincere heart,
I invite every being as my guest to the very best happiness,
and then deceive them all,
how could I ever go to a good rebirth? ^4-6

Even someone who gives up bodhicitta
can still, in the end, become free —
the way this works is beyond ordinary thought;
only the all-knowing Buddha truly understands it. ^4-7

For a bodhisattva,
this is the heaviest of all faults.
If this happens,
it fails the good of every being. ^4-8

Whoever, even for a single moment,
blocks or obstructs this goodness,
fails the good of beings,
and their hard rebirths will have no end. ^4-9

If destroying the happiness
of even a single being harms me this much,
then what need is there to even mention
destroying the happiness of beings as endless as the whole of space? ^4-10

So, since the power of this fault
and the power of bodhicitta
keep alternating in the cycle of rebirth,
reaching the bodhisattva levels gets delayed for a very long time. ^4-11

So, just as I have promised,
I will carry it out with real respect.
If I do not make the effort starting now,
I will just keep falling lower and lower. ^4-12

Countless Buddhas, who help every being,
have already come and gone,
but because of my own faults,
I was not within the reach of their healing care. ^4-13

If I keep acting the same way now,
then again and again, the same thing will happen:
in hard rebirths, I will experience
sickness, being bound, cut apart, and torn open. ^4-14

A Buddha appearing in the world,
having faith, and gaining a human life,
and being able to practice goodness —
such things are so rare; when will I have them all together again? ^4-15

A day like today, free from sickness,
with food, and no harm coming —
life is deceptive, gone in a moment;
this body is like something borrowed for just a little while. ^4-16

Behaving the way I do,
I will not even gain a human life again.
And if I do not gain a human life,
there will be only wrongdoing, and no goodness at all. ^4-17

So if, even now, while I have the good fortune to practice goodness,
I still do not do it,
then, confused and overwhelmed by the suffering of hard rebirths,
what will I be able to do then? ^4-18

If I do not do good,
and instead go on gathering wrongdoing,
then even after a hundred million ages,
I will not even hear the words "good rebirth." ^4-19

This is exactly why the Buddha said
that gaining a human life is as rare
as a blind turtle, in the vast ocean, happening to put its neck
through the single hole of a wooden yoke drifting on the waves. ^4-20

If even a single moment of wrongdoing
can lead to an age in the deepest hell,
then what need is there to even mention
the wrongdoing gathered since beginningless time keeping me from a good rebirth? ^4-21

After experiencing just that much,
I still will not be free —
because even while experiencing it,
more wrongdoing keeps arising all over again. ^4-22

Having found this kind of freedom and good luck,
if I still do not practice goodness,
there is no greater deception than this,
and no greater confusion than this. ^4-23

If I understand this,
and yet, out of confusion, still feel discouraged later,
then at the time when death finally comes,
a great grief will surely arise in me. ^4-24

If the unbearable fire of hell
burns my body for a long, long time,
then the unbearable fire of my own regret
will certainly torment my mind. ^4-25

Having found, by some rare stroke of luck,
this ground so hard to find, so full of benefit,
if I, even while having some understanding,
am led back to hell again, ^4-26

it would be as if I had been confused by some spell —
here, I would have no mind of my own at all.
I do not even know what confuses me like this;
what is it that lives deep inside me? ^4-27

Enemies like anger and craving
have no hands or feet at all,
and are not brave or clever either —
so how have they made me their servant? ^4-28

While living right inside my own mind,
they happily go on harming me,
yet I still do not get angry — this kind of patience
is really improper, a shameful patience. ^4-29

If all the gods and demigods
rose up against me as enemies,
even they could not lead me
into the fire of the deepest hell. ^4-30

But this powerful enemy, my own disturbing emotions,
when it meets even Mount Meru,
can burn it down without leaving even ash behind,
and can throw me there in a single instant. ^4-31

My enemy, disturbing emotion,
has no beginning or end, lasting so long —
no other enemy
could ever last that long. ^4-32

If I care for and rely on it kindly,
everything else brings help and happiness.
But if I rely on disturbing emotions instead,
they only bring me suffering and harm, again and again. ^4-33

So this enemy, constant and unbroken for so long,
the single cause behind every kind of harm —
if it stays firmly lodged in my own heart,
how could I ever feel safe or happy in endless rebirth? ^4-34

The guardians of the prison of endless rebirth, who become the killers
that send me to hell and other hard rebirths —
if they still stay caught in the net of my mind's attachment,
how could I ever find happiness? ^4-35

So, until I have truly and completely
defeated this enemy, right before my own eyes, I will not give up trying.
Even people who grow furious at some small harm someone does them
will not sleep, in their pride, until they have defeated that person. ^4-36

Even disturbing emotions, whose very nature will bring suffering and death anyway,
when people are determined to defeat them, facing them in battle,
they look past the pain of being struck by arrows, spears, and swords,
and refuse to turn back and flee until they have reached their goal. ^4-37

So now, when I am determined to defeat, once and for all,
this enemy that is by nature the cause of every suffering,
what need is there to even mention
not losing heart or feeling discouraged, whatever hundreds of sufferings come from it? ^4-38

Even scars left by a pointless enemy
are worn on the body like proud ornaments —
so why should suffering, that comes while I am truly working hard
to accomplish something truly great, ever hold me back? ^4-39

Fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and others like them,
thinking only of their own simple livelihood,
still bear the hardship of cold, heat, and everything else —
so why could someone like me not bear it for the happiness of beings? ^4-40

I promised to free
beings, as vast as the edges of space in all ten directions,
from their disturbing emotions —
yet I myself have not even freed myself from mine. ^4-41

Speaking like this,
without even knowing my own limits, how is this not madness?
So, in defeating disturbing emotions,
I will never turn back. ^4-42

I will hold firmly to this task,
and, with real resolve, fight this battle.
Against disturbing emotions of that kind,
only fighting to destroy disturbing emotions makes sense for me. ^4-43

Even if I am burned, or killed,
even if my head is cut off,
I will never, in any way,
bow down to the enemy of disturbing emotions. ^4-44

An ordinary enemy, even when driven out of one land,
settles in another, gathers strength there,
and then comes back again —
but the enemy of disturbing emotions is not like that. ^4-45

Once I have seen and let go of a disturbing emotion with the eye of wisdom,
once it has been cleared from my own mind, where could it possibly go?
Where could it stay and then come back to harm me from?
It is only my own weak mind and lack of effort that let it back in. ^4-46

Disturbing emotions do not truly live in objects, not in the senses, and not in between either.
They are not found anywhere else either — so where do they live, that they can harm every being?
They are just like an illusion — so let go of fear in your heart, and work hard to understand this.
Why, for no real reason, should I let them go on harming me, in hell and other hard rebirths? ^4-47

So, having thought this through carefully,
I will work hard to carry out this training just as it has been taught.
If you do not listen to your doctor's advice,
how can medicine ever heal a sickness that needs treating? ^4-48

This is the end of the fourth chapter, called "Being Careful and Mindful," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^4-a

## 5. Chapter Five: Watching Your Mind Carefully ^5-0

Whoever wants to keep this training
must work hard to guard their own mind.
If I do not guard this mind,
I will never be able to keep the training at all. ^5-1

An untamed, wild elephant
does not cause as much harm in this world
as the elephant of an unguarded mind,
which brings harm as bad as the deepest hell. ^5-2

But if I tie the elephant of my mind
tightly with the rope of mindfulness,
then every danger disappears,
and every kind of goodness comes into my hands. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, wild elephants, bears,
snakes, and every kind of enemy,
the guardians who punish beings in hell,
witches, and demons too — ^5-4

all of these are held back
simply by holding this one mind.
And by taming this one mind alone,
all of them are tamed as well. ^5-5

In this way, every danger,
and every kind of endless suffering,
comes from the mind alone —
this is exactly what the true teachings tell us. ^5-6

Who forged the weapons
used to torture beings in hell?
Who built the burning iron ground?
Where did all those masses of fire come from? ^5-7

The Buddha has said
that all of these come from a mind full of wrongdoing.
So in all three realms of this world,
there is nothing to fear more than the mind. ^5-8

If the perfection of generosity
means clearing away the poverty of the world,
then, since there are still poor beings now,
how did the past protectors ever perfect it? ^5-9

All that I own, together with its results,
given away in intention to every being —
this is called the perfection of generosity.
So it is a matter of the mind itself. ^5-10

Refusing to kill fish and other such creatures,
turning away from harming them —
that intention to hold back
is what is called the perfection of discipline. ^5-11

Difficult beings are as endless as the sky;
I could never finish defeating them all.
But if I defeat this one mind of anger,
it is the same as defeating every enemy. ^5-12

Where could I ever find enough leather
to cover the whole surface of the earth?
But with leather the size of just the sole of my shoe,
it is as if the whole earth were covered. ^5-13

In the same way, I cannot possibly turn back
every outer thing that could harm me.
But if I turn back this mind of mine,
what need is there to turn back everything else? ^5-14

A single moment of a clear, pure mind
can bring the same result as reaching the realm of Brahmā.
But actions of body and speech
without that clear mind bear little fruit at all. ^5-15

Even chanting mantras and doing every kind of hardship
for a very, very long time,
if the mind is distracted elsewhere,
the wise say it is all done for nothing. ^5-16

Whoever does not understand this secret of the mind,
the very finest of all the true teachings,
even while wanting happiness and wanting to end suffering,
wanders about pointlessly, achieving nothing. ^5-17

So I must hold this mind of mine well,
and guard it carefully.
Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind,
what use is any other kind of discipline? ^5-18

Just as someone standing among an unruly crowd
stays careful, guarding a wound,
in the same way, while living among difficult people,
I should always guard this wound that is my mind. ^5-19

If I am careful of a wound
even out of fear of its small pain,
why would I not guard this wound of the mind
against being crushed by the mountains of hell? ^5-20

Living this way,
whether among difficult people
or even among women,
my firm commitment to discipline should never weaken. ^5-21

Even if I lose my wealth, my honor,
my body, or my very livelihood,
and even if my other goodness fades,
I must never let my mind fade. ^5-22

To all those who wish to guard their minds,
I join my hands together
and say: with all your effort,
guard your mindfulness and your careful awareness. ^5-23

People troubled by sickness
have no strength left for any task.
In the same way, people whose minds are troubled by confusion
have no strength left for any task either. ^5-24

Whatever is heard, thought about, or meditated on
by a mind without careful awareness
does not stay in memory,
like water in a leaking jar. ^5-25

Even many people with learning, with faith,
and with real, devoted effort
still become stained by faults and wrongdoing,
simply for lacking careful awareness. ^5-26

Thieves without careful awareness
follow right behind a failing mindfulness,
and even goodness that has been carefully gathered
gets stolen away, leading straight to a hard rebirth. ^5-27

This band of thieves, the disturbing emotions,
is always looking for its chance.
Once it finds an opening, it steals away goodness,
and even destroys the very life of a good rebirth. ^5-28

So I must never let mindfulness
wander from the door of my mind.
And even if it wanders off,
I must remember the harm of hard rebirths and bring it straight back. ^5-29

Through staying close to a teacher,
through the guidance of an abbot,
and through a fearful, devoted respect,
mindfulness arises easily and naturally. ^5-30

The Buddhas and the bodhisattvas
see everything, everywhere, without any obstacle.
Right in front of all of them,
I am always standing — ^5-31

thinking this way, I should feel
a sense of shame, respect, and appropriate fear.
And in this way,
remembering the Buddhas again and again will come naturally. ^5-32

When mindfulness stays
at the door of the mind, to guard it,
then careful awareness comes too,
and even what has wandered off comes back. ^5-33

For now, understanding clearly
that this ordinary mind is full of faults,
I should hold it firm, like a piece of wood,
and stay steady in that resolve. ^5-34

I should never let my eyes
wander about looking at pointless things.
Instead, with real intention, I should always
keep my eyes lowered, and look with purpose. ^5-35

But to rest my eyes,
I may look around from time to time.
And if I see someone in my line of sight,
I should look at them and say a warm word of welcome. ^5-36

To check for danger on the road,
I may look in every direction, again and again.
And when resting, I should first look behind me,
then turn and look back the way I came. ^5-37

Checking both ahead and behind,
I should go forward, or turn back.
In every situation like this,
I should understand what is needed, and act accordingly. ^5-38

Deciding, "I will hold my body this way,"
and setting my task,
I should then check, from time to time,
how this body is actually positioned. ^5-39

Just as I would watch, with total effort,
a wild elephant of a mind tied to the great post of the true teachings,
so that it never slips away,
I should watch my own mind carefully in every way. ^5-40

Trying with all my effort to keep my meditation
from slipping even for a single moment,
I should ask myself again and again:
"Where is my mind wandering right now?" ^5-41

If I cannot manage this
during moments of fear or celebration, I may relax a little.
It is said that during times of generosity,
some flexibility in the rules of discipline is allowed. ^5-42

Whatever task I have thought through and begun,
I should not let my mind wander to something else.
With my whole intention fixed on it,
I should finish that task first. ^5-43

In this way, everything gets done well;
otherwise, neither task succeeds.
And the hardship that comes from a lack of careful awareness
will not keep growing this way either. ^5-44

Whenever idle talk of every kind,
or many kinds of amazing entertainment,
draw everyone's attention,
I should let go of any attachment to them. ^5-45

If I find myself pointlessly digging up ground, cutting grass,
drawing lines in the dirt, and things like that,
I should remember the Sugata's training,
and, out of a healthy fear, drop it immediately. ^5-46

Whenever I want to move,
or whenever I want to speak,
I should first check my own mind,
and act with firm, steady awareness. ^5-47

Whenever I notice my own mind
becoming attached, or wanting to grow angry,
I should not act, and I should not speak.
I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-48

Whenever my mind becomes restless or mocking,
whenever it fills with pride and arrogance,
whenever it wants to expose others' faults,
or turns deceptive and evasive, ^5-49

whenever I feel eager for my own praise,
or want to put someone else down,
whenever harsh words or regret arise —
at those times, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-50

Whenever I want wealth, honor, or fame,
whenever I want followers around me,
or whenever my mind wants to be served,
at those times, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-51

Whenever my mind wants to toss aside the good of others,
whenever it wants to look after only my own good,
and whenever it wants to speak up in that spirit,
at those times, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-52

Whenever impatience, laziness, fear,
rudeness, or thoughtless chatter arise,
or when my mind clings only to my own side,
at those times, I should stay still, like a piece of wood. ^5-53

In this way, I should check my mind
for disturbing emotions and pointless effort.
And then, like a hero, I should firmly
hold it steady with the right remedy. ^5-54

With full determination, real faith,
steadiness, respect, courtesy,
a healthy sense of shame, and appropriate fear,
I should work hard, calm and kind, to bring others joy. ^5-55

I should not feel discouraged
by the conflicting wishes of immature people.
Understanding that their disturbing emotions
give rise to those wishes, I should meet them with love. ^5-56

Acting for the sake of things that bring no fault,
taking charge over both myself and other beings,
and understanding that everything is like a magical illusion, with no real "I" in it,
I should hold onto this attitude always. ^5-57

Thinking again and again
how, after a very long time, I have finally found this precious freedom and good luck,
I should hold my mind
as unmoving and firm as Mount Meru. ^5-58

When vultures fight over a piece of flesh
and drag my body about in every direction,
mind, if you feel no anger at that,
then why do you cling so tightly to it now? ^5-59

Mind, you have claimed this body as your own —
why do you go on guarding it so closely?
Since you and this body are two separate things,
what real use is this body to you? ^5-60

Foolish mind, why do you not
take up instead a clean, wooden form?
Why do you bother guarding this rotting machine,
this heap of unclean things? ^5-61

First, with my own understanding,
I should mentally separate away this covering of skin.
Then, with the sword of wisdom,
I should separate the flesh from the framework of bones. ^5-62

And even the bones, once separated,
I should examine down to the very marrow.
I should ask myself:
what solid core is really there? ^5-63

If, searching this carefully,
you still find no solid core anywhere,
then why, mind, do you still cling
so tightly to guarding this body? ^5-64

You would not eat its unclean parts,
you could not drink its blood,
and you could not chew on its intestines —
so what real use is this body to you? ^5-65

It would make more sense to guard this body
only for the sake of feeding jackals and vultures.
This human body
is really only good for being put to use. ^5-66

Even if you guard it so carefully,
the merciless Lord of Death
will still seize it and give it to the crows and dogs.
What will you do then? ^5-67

If you would not even give clothing
to a servant who does no useful work,
then why feed and fatten this body,
when, once dead, it goes to serve someone else entirely? ^5-68

So, having paid this body its fair wage,
I should now put it to use for my own true purpose.
I should not give this body
everything, when it brings no real benefit. ^5-69

Thinking of this body simply as a boat,
a support for coming and going,
I should turn it into a wish-fulfilling vessel,
in order to accomplish the good of beings. ^5-70

In this way, with real freedom,
I should always keep a gentle smile,
letting go of any scowl or frown,
and become an open, honest friend to the world. ^5-71

I should not carelessly slam down
chairs and other such things with a loud noise.
I should not throw doors open roughly either,
and should always take quiet delight in gentleness. ^5-72

Just as a heron, a cat, or a thief
moves silently, without any sound,
to accomplish exactly what they intend,
a wise person should always act in that same careful way. ^5-73

I should skillfully accept good advice
that helps me, even without being asked,
taking it to heart with real respect,
and always become a student to everyone. ^5-74

To everything well said by anyone,
I should say, "Well spoken."
And when I see someone doing something good,
I should praise them, and feel real, warm joy. ^5-75

I should speak of others' good qualities behind their back,
and repeat with approval when I hear others praised.
And when someone speaks of my own good qualities,
I should simply recognize that they do exist. ^5-76

Every effort anyone makes is for the sake of joy,
yet that joy is rare, even if bought with money.
So I should take real delight and happiness
in the good qualities others have achieved. ^5-77

There is no real loss to me in this,
and it brings great happiness to others too, in the next life.
Being harsh brings no joy, only suffering,
and it brings great suffering in the next life too. ^5-78

When I speak, my words should be clear and warm,
easy to follow, and pleasant to hear,
free from attachment and free from anger,
gentle, and fitting for the moment. ^5-79

And when I look at other beings with my eyes,
I should think, "It is only by relying on them
that I will become a Buddha,"
and look at them honestly, with real love. ^5-80

Whether it comes from deep, sincere devotion,
or from applying the proper remedy,
in a field of real goodness, or one that helps others,
or with beings who suffer — in all these, great goodness grows. ^5-81

Skilled, and full of real energy,
I should always take on tasks myself.
In everything I do,
I should not simply wait on others. ^5-82

I should practice the perfections — generosity and the rest —
each one more important than the one before,
never giving up something greater for the sake of something smaller,
and thinking mainly of the good of others. ^5-83

Understanding this, I should stay
always devoted to the good of others.
The compassionate one, who sees far ahead,
has even permitted, for this reason, what is normally forbidden. ^5-84

I should share my food with those who have fallen into wrongdoing,
those without a protector, and those still in training,
and eat only what is truly needed,
giving away everything except my three robes. ^5-85

I should not harm this body,
which practices the true teachings, for the sake of something minor.
Acting this way,
the wishes of beings will be fulfilled quickly. ^5-86

Without a truly pure heart of compassion,
I should not give away this body.
Instead, in this life and in others,
I should use it as the cause for accomplishing something truly great. ^5-87

I should not teach the true teachings to someone without respect,
to someone acting as if sick, with their head wrapped up,
to someone holding a parasol, a stick, or a weapon,
or to someone with their head covered. ^5-88

I should not teach the deep and vast teachings to those of lesser capacity,
and never abandon the teachings meant for men when teaching women.
For both lesser and greater teachings,
I should treat them equally, with the same respect. ^5-89

For someone who is a vessel for the vast teachings,
I should not offer only the lesser teachings.
I should not abandon the practice of conduct,
and I should not mislead them with sūtras and mantras alone. ^5-90

When spitting or discarding a toothpick,
I should cover it up.
And urinating or discarding waste
into water or onto usable ground is something to avoid. ^5-91

I should not eat with my mouth stuffed full,
noisily, or with my mouth wide open.
I should not sit with my legs stretched out,
and I should not rub my hands together needlessly. ^5-92

In a vehicle, on a bed, or in a private place,
I should not be alone together with another man's wife.
I should look carefully and ask others
about anything the world might find improper, and avoid it. ^5-93

I should not point with my finger,
but instead, with real respect, gesture
with my whole right hand,
and even show the way like this. ^5-94

I should not wave my arms about wildly.
Instead, moving them just slightly, and making a small sound,
I should snap my fingers to get attention when needed.
Doing otherwise would be considered undisciplined. ^5-95

I should sleep lying in the direction the protector lay down when he passed beyond suffering,
facing the direction I wish.
And with careful awareness, I should decide clearly, right from the start,
to rise again quickly. ^5-96

The practices of a bodhisattva
are said to be truly limitless.
But the practice of training the mind
should definitely be carried out for as long as it takes. ^5-97

Three times during the day, and three times during the night,
I should recite the Confession in Three Sections.
Relying on the Buddhas and on bodhicitta,
I can calm whatever faults remain. ^5-98

Whether the situation is my own or belongs to someone else,
whatever I am doing, in whatever circumstance,
I should work hard to train
exactly as the training has been taught for that situation. ^5-99

There is truly nothing at all
that a child of the Buddhas should not train in.
For someone skilled who lives this way,
there is nothing that does not become a source of goodness. ^5-100

Whether directly or indirectly,
I should do nothing except what benefits beings.
And solely for the sake of beings,
I should dedicate everything toward awakening. ^5-101

I should never give up my life,
even for the sake of my own life,
for a spiritual friend who is skilled in the meaning of the Great Vehicle,
and who holds the finest discipline of a bodhisattva. ^5-102

From the life story of Śrī Sambhava,
I should learn how to rely properly on a teacher.
This, and other instructions taught by the Buddha,
I should come to understand by reading the sūtras. ^5-103

The trainings appear throughout the sūtras,
so I should read the sūtras themselves.
And the Sūtra of Ākāśagarbha in particular
I should look at first of all. ^5-104

And because the Compendium of Trainings
explains, at great length, what should always be practiced,
I should also look at it again and again,
very carefully. ^5-105

Or, for now, I should at least look
at the shorter Compendium of Sūtras,
and I should also make the effort
to look at the two works composed by noble Nāgārjuna. ^5-106

Whatever is not forbidden in these texts,
that is exactly what I should practice.
And in order to guard the minds of ordinary people,
having seen the training, I should practice it exactly as it is. ^5-107

Checking again and again
the state of my body and my mind —
that, in short, and only that,
is the very definition of watching your mind carefully. ^5-108

All of this should be put into practice with the body.
What can mere words alone accomplish?
Would simply reading a book of medicine
ever actually help a sick person? ^5-109

This is the end of the fifth chapter, called "Watching Your Mind Carefully," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^5-a

## 6. Chapter Six: Teaching Patience ^6-0

All the goodness gathered over a thousand ages—
gifts given, and offerings made to the Sugatas,
and every other good deed done—
a single burst of anger can destroy it all. ^6-1

There is no wrongdoing like anger,
and no hardship like patience.
So I will work hard at patience,
practicing it in every way I can. ^6-2

If I hold on to the sharp pain of angry thoughts,
my mind will never know peace.
I will not find joy or happiness,
sleep will not come, and I will feel unsettled. ^6-3

Even those who depend on someone
for their wealth, their honor, and their kindness,
may still rise up in anger
and try to kill that very person who cares for them. ^6-4

Because of anger, their friends grow sad,
and no longer want to stay close, even when given gifts.
In short, there is no such thing
as anger that brings peace or happiness. ^6-5

The enemy anger brings troubles just like this,
causing all kinds of suffering.
But whoever works hard to defeat anger
finds happiness, both now and later. ^6-6

When something happens that I don't want,
or something blocks what I do want,
unhappiness finds its food there,
and anger grows strong, destroying me. ^6-7

So I will completely destroy
the food of this enemy, anger.
This enemy has no other job at all
than to cause me harm. ^6-8

No matter what happens to me,
I will not let it disturb my happy mind.
Being unhappy does not get me what I want,
and it only makes my goodness weaker. ^6-9

If there is a way to fix a problem,
what reason is there to be unhappy about it?
And if there is no way to fix it,
what use is being unhappy anyway? ^6-10

For myself or for my friends,
I don't want suffering, insults, harsh words, or unpleasant things—
but somehow, for my enemies,
I want the very opposite. ^6-11

The causes of happiness appear only sometimes,
while the causes of suffering are very many.
Without suffering, there is no way to truly leave endless rebirth—
so, my mind, hold yourself steady. ^6-12

Even people who practice strange, painful rituals
bear burning, cutting, and other pain for no real purpose.
So why should I, seeking true freedom,
hold back from bearing hardship? ^6-13

There is nothing at all
that does not become easier once I grow used to it.
So, by growing used to bearing small troubles,
I can learn to bear even great troubles patiently. ^6-14

Snakebites, the bites of flies,
feeling hunger and thirst, and other discomforts like these—
don't I see people bear
all these kinds of pain, even for no real purpose? ^6-15

Heat, cold, rain, and wind,
sickness, being bound, being struck, and more—
I will not complain about these;
if I do, the harm will only grow. ^6-16

Some people, seeing their own blood,
grow even braver and stronger.
But others, seeing someone else's blood,
faint and fall down. ^6-17

This difference comes from the steadiness or weakness of the mind,
not from the pain itself.
So I will look down on harm,
and not let suffering overpower me. ^6-18

Even when suffering comes,
a wise person does not let their mind grow troubled or unclear.
When fighting against disturbing emotions,
there is naturally much difficulty in that battle. ^6-19

Those who look past their own pain
and defeat enemies like anger
are the true heroes;
everyone else is only fighting against what is already dead. ^6-20

Suffering also has a good side to it:
through feeling discouraged by it, pride is cleared away,
compassion arises for those caught in endless rebirth,
I become careful to avoid wrongdoing, and I find joy in doing good. ^6-21

I don't get angry at bile and other things,
even though they are a great source of suffering—
so why do I get angry at beings who have minds?
They too are only pushed into action by conditions. ^6-22

For example, just as sickness arises
even though we don't want it to,
in the same way, disturbing emotions
arise forcefully, even though we don't want them either. ^6-23

People don't think, "I will get angry now,"
and yet anger arises suddenly in them.
In the same way, without anyone deciding to create it,
anger simply arises on its own. ^6-24

All the many kinds of faults there are,
and every sort of wrongdoing,
arise through the power of conditions;
none of it happens under its own control. ^6-25

Even the group of conditions that come together
does not think, "I will create this."
And what is created by them
does not think, "I have been created." ^6-26

Whatever is imagined as a maker,
or whatever is thought of as a "self"—
that too does not deliberately think, "I will arise,"
and then come into being. ^6-27

If it did not exist before it was born,
what could there be that wants to be born then?
And since it always stays fixed on its object,
it could never really cease to exist either. ^6-28

If this self were truly permanent,
then, like empty space, it could not act.
And even when it meets with other conditions,
since it never changes, what could it possibly do? ^6-29

And when it acts, if it stays just the same as before,
what exactly did the action do to it?
What real connection could there be
between "the self" and "its action"? ^6-30

So in this way, everything depends on something else,
and has no power of its own over anything.
Understanding this, I will not grow angry at things,
seeing them as unreal, like something imagined. ^6-31

Someone might ask, "Then who is there to stop what?
Stopping something doesn't even make sense."
But relying on causes like these,
it is not unreasonable to want suffering to end. ^6-32

So, whether it is an enemy or a friend,
when I see them do something wrong,
I will think, "This came about because of conditions like these,"
and in that way, stay calm and at ease. ^6-33

If things happened just because we wished them to,
no one would ever want to suffer,
and so no living being anywhere
would ever experience any suffering at all. ^6-34

Out of carelessness, people even hurt themselves
with thorns and other things.
And to get what they want, like someone's love,
some starve themselves and act in desperate ways. ^6-35

Some even jump off cliffs, hang themselves,
eat poison, or eat food that is bad for them,
and harm themselves
through actions that bring no goodness at all. ^6-36

When people are overpowered by disturbing emotions,
they even try to kill the very self they love so much—
so how could they possibly
not harm the bodies of others too? ^6-37

Since disturbing emotions have arisen in them,
causing them to even try to kill themselves,
if I cannot feel even a little compassion for this,
then what is the point of getting angry at them instead? ^6-38

If it were simply the nature of foolish people to harm others,
it would make no more sense to be angry at them
than to be angry at fire
for its nature to burn. ^6-39

And if this fault only comes and goes,
while beings are gentle by their true nature,
then it still makes no sense to be angry at them—
like getting angry at the sky when smoke happens to drift across it. ^6-40

If someone throws a stick or another weapon at me,
and I get angry at the stick itself, that makes no sense—
the stick is only moved by someone's anger too.
So really, I should be angry at that anger instead. ^6-41

In the past, I have caused harm to beings
just like this myself.
So it makes sense
that beings would now cause harm back to me in this same way. ^6-42

Both their weapon and my own body
are the causes of this suffering.
They caused the weapon, and I caused this body—
so which one should I really be angry at? ^6-43

Blinded by craving, I have grasped this body,
shaped like a boil, so full of pain that I cannot even bear to touch it.
So when it is harmed,
who exactly should I be angry at? ^6-44

Foolish people don't want suffering,
and yet they crave the very causes of suffering.
So when they are harmed by their own fault,
why should they blame someone else instead? ^6-45

It's like the guardians of hell,
or a forest of sword-like leaves—
I created this harm myself, through my own actions.
So who exactly should I be angry at? ^6-46

Those who harm me only appear
because my own actions have stirred them into it.
And because of that harm, they themselves will go to hell—
so am I not the one destroying them? ^6-47

By relying on beings like this, and being patient with them,
my own wrongdoing lessens greatly.
But by relying on me,
they themselves go through long suffering in hell. ^6-48

So really, I am the one who harms them,
while they are the ones who actually help me.
So why, my twisted mind,
do you get angry, backward as that is? ^6-49

If I truly had a good quality of mind,
I would not go to hell in the first place.
And if I protect myself well,
what harm could ever come to me from them at all? ^6-50

But if I harm them back in return,
then they will not be protected from harm,
and my own practice, too, will be ruined—
this hard-won discipline will fall apart. ^6-51

My mind has no body,
so no one can ever truly destroy it.
But because I cling so strongly to this body,
it is the body that gets hurt by suffering. ^6-52

Being insulted, spoken to harshly,
or hearing unpleasant words—
none of this actually harms my body.
So why, my mind, do you get so very angry? ^6-53

If others' dislike of me will not eat me up,
either in this life or in any future life,
then why don't I want it,
instead of resisting it so much? ^6-54

If I don't want this because it blocks me from gaining things,
then it's really my own gains I am giving up here,
while my wrongdoing
stays firmly in place. ^6-55

It would be better for me to die today
than to live long doing wrong.
Even if people like me live a very long time,
we will still feel the very same suffering when death finally comes. ^6-56

Someone who enjoys a hundred years of happiness in a dream,
and then wakes up,
and someone else who enjoys just a moment of happiness,
and then wakes up— ^6-57

for both of them, once they wake up,
that happiness never comes back again.
In the same way, whether life is long or short,
at the moment of death, both end up exactly the same. ^6-58

Even if I gain a lot of wealth,
and enjoy happiness for a very long time,
in the end I will leave empty-handed,
just like someone robbed by a thief. ^6-59

Someone might say, "If I stay alive because of my wealth,
I can use up my wrongdoing and do good instead."
But if I get angry for the sake of wealth,
won't my goodness run out, and my wrongdoing only grow? ^6-60

If the very purpose I am living for
is ruined because of my own anger,
then what is the use of staying alive at all,
if all I do is create more wrongdoing? ^6-61

If I get angry at someone speaking harsh words
because it hurts a being's feelings,
then why don't I get angry at myself in the same way,
when I speak harsh words about others? ^6-62

If I can put up with someone's lack of faith in me,
since it depends on something else outside my control,
why can't I put up with harsh words spoken to me,
since they too depend on disturbing emotions arising in someone else? ^6-63

Even when people damage or destroy
statues, stupas, and the true teachings,
it does not make sense for me to feel anger,
since the Buddhas and such are not actually harmed by it. ^6-64

As for those who harm my teachers, my relatives, and my friends,
I will see, just as before,
that this comes from conditions,
and turn my anger away. ^6-65

Beings that have minds, and things that have no mind,
both can cause harm to me.
So why do I direct my anger only at those with minds?
Instead, I will be patient with harm. ^6-66

Some people do wrong out of confusion,
and some, out of confusion, become angry.
Who among them can we really say is faultless?
Who is it that truly has no fault at all? ^6-67

Why did the action that causes someone to harm others
happen to them in the first place?
Since everything depends on past actions,
why should I hold this against them now? ^6-68

Seeing things this way, I will work hard, in every way I can,
to make everyone feel loving-kindness toward one another,
and in this same way,
I will strive with real effort to do good. ^6-69

For example, when a fire from a burning house
spreads to another house,
catching on straw and other things that let it spread,
that straw and such should be cleared away and removed. ^6-70

In the same way, when my mind grows attached to something,
and the fire of anger spreads because of it,
I should fear that my goodness will burn away,
and let go of that attachment right away. ^6-71

If someone about to be executed
has their hand cut off instead and is set free, isn't that a good thing?
And if, through human suffering,
I become free from hell, isn't that also a good thing? ^6-72

If I cannot even bear
this small suffering happening right now,
then why don't I turn away from anger,
the very cause of the suffering of hell? ^6-73

For the sake of desires, I have already experienced
burning and other such things thousands of times in hell,
and yet through all of that,
I accomplished nothing good, neither for myself nor for others. ^6-74

But this present suffering is not nearly so harmful,
and it can help accomplish something truly great.
So I should feel only joy at this suffering,
which clears away the harm faced by all beings. ^6-75

If, when someone else is praised for having good qualities,
I feel happy and glad about it,
why, my mind, don't you feel that same joy
when you yourself are the one being praised? ^6-76

This joy you feel is a source of happiness
with nothing wrong in it at all.
It is even praised by those who have good qualities themselves,
and it is the very best way to bring people together. ^6-77

But if you don't want this kind of happiness,
even though it also brings joy to others,
then you should also give up paying wages and other generous acts,
and you will lose out both in this life and beyond, whether seen or unseen. ^6-78

When my own good qualities are being spoken of,
I want others to feel happy about it too.
But when someone else's good qualities are being spoken of,
why don't I want to feel happy myself? ^6-79

Since I have given rise to bodhicitta,
wishing for the happiness of every being,
why would I feel angry now,
when beings find happiness all on their own? ^6-80

If I say I want all beings to become Buddhas,
worthy of the offerings of the three worlds,
then why do I get so upset
just seeing them receive a little bit of ordinary respect? ^6-81

If a relative you are meant to support
finds their own way to make a living,
wouldn't you be happy about that,
instead of getting angry all over again? ^6-82

If I don't even want that much for beings,
then what kind of awakening do I really want for them?
And how can bodhicitta possibly exist
in someone who gets angry at another person's good fortune? ^6-83

Whether that gift stays in the giver's house,
or comes to the beggar instead,
either way, it was never yours to begin with—
so what does it matter to you whether it is given or not? ^6-84

So why would I throw away my own goodness,
my faith, and my own good qualities,
by refusing to hold onto this joy I have gained?
Tell me, who wouldn't be upset with themselves for that? ^6-85

You yourself have done wrong,
and you don't even feel sorry about it—
so why would you want to compete
with others who have done good instead?
[End of Volume Two] ^6-86

Even if my enemy is unhappy,
what good does that do for me?
Just wishing for it in my mind
will not actually cause them any real harm. ^6-87

Even if my wish makes that suffering happen to them,
what good does that bring me either?
If I say, "It satisfies me,"
what could possibly be worse than that? ^6-88

This hook, cast by the fisherman of disturbing emotions,
is truly unbearably sharp.
Once caught by it, I will be boiled
by the guardians of hell in a cauldron of hell. ^6-89

Being praised, and having a good reputation—
these do not bring goodness, and they do not bring long life.
They do not bring me strength, they do not keep me free from sickness,
and they do not bring comfort to my body either. ^6-90

If I truly understood my own real benefit,
what benefit would there even be in this?
If all I want is a happy mind,
I should rely on things like gambling and wine instead. ^6-91

For the sake of reputation, people waste their wealth,
and even kill themselves.
What good do mere words really do?
When I die, who will they make happy? ^6-92

When a sandcastle collapses,
children cry out in dismay.
In the same way, when my praise and reputation fade,
my own mind acts just like a child's. ^6-93

Since a sound has no mind of its own,
it cannot possibly have any intention to praise me.
Instead, it must be that others feel joy toward me—
that is the real reason for my own joy. ^6-94

But whether that joy belongs to someone else,
or even to me, what good does someone else's joy really do for me?
Their pleasure and happiness belong only to them—
I gain no share of it at all. ^6-95

If I were happy because they are happy,
then I should feel that way about everyone else too.
So why is it that when someone feels joy from something else's happiness,
I myself am not happy? ^6-96

So the thought, "I am being praised,"
that gives rise to my own private joy—
that too doesn't really make sense in this way;
it is nothing more than the behavior of a child. ^6-97

Praise and such things distract me from what matters,
and they destroy my very sense of urgency.
They create jealousy toward those with good qualities,
and they destroy my own excellence too. ^6-98

So, isn't it true that those who stay close by,
working to destroy my praise and reputation,
are actually protecting me
from falling into the lower realms? ^6-99

I am someone who seeks true freedom;
I don't need to be bound by gain and honor.
So why would I feel angry
at those who are actually freeing me from these bonds? ^6-100

I want to enter into suffering, and yet,
as if blessed by the Buddhas themselves,
a door that will not let me in has appeared before me—
so why would I feel angry at that? ^6-101

It doesn't make sense to be angry
at someone who is said to be blocking my goodness,
since there is no hardship equal to patience—
so shouldn't I stay firmly grounded in this instead? ^6-102

And if, because of my own fault,
I am not able to be patient with this,
then it is truly I myself
who is blocking the very cause of my own goodness. ^6-103

Whatever cannot arise without something,
and whatever exists when that something is present,
is truly the cause of that very thing—
so how can it be called an obstacle? ^6-104

A beggar who comes at just the right time
is not an obstacle to generosity.
And someone who helps me become ordained
cannot rightly be called an obstacle to ordination either. ^6-105

In this world, beggars are common,
but those who actually cause harm are rare.
Since I have caused no harm to others in return,
hardly anyone actually causes me harm at all. ^6-106

So, just like a treasure appearing in my own house
without any effort on my part,
since my enemy has become a helper
on the path of a bodhisattva's practice, I will feel real joy toward my enemy. ^6-107

Since this fruit of patience was accomplished
together by both my enemy and me,
it is right that the very first offering of it should go to my enemy,
since it is truly the cause of my patience. ^6-108

If I say this enemy should not be honored,
since they had no actual intention of helping me develop patience,
then why do I honor the true teachings,
even though they too are simply a fitting cause for my accomplishment? ^6-109

If I say this enemy should not be honored,
since their real intention was to harm me,
then how could I ever develop patience at all,
if I only worked hard to help others the way a doctor does? ^6-110

So, since patience arises
by relying on a mind that is truly full of hatred,
it is that hatred itself which is the real cause of my patience,
and it deserves to be honored just like the true teachings. ^6-111

This is exactly why the Sage said
that beings themselves are a field, just like the field of the Victors—
many people have gained the perfection of true excellence
by making beings like this happy. ^6-112

Since the qualities of a Buddha
come about equally from beings and from the Victors,
why is it fitting to show respect only to the Victors,
and not in the same way to beings? ^6-113

The greatness of a good intention doesn't come from the intention alone,
but from its result—
and in that same way, beings also have this same kind of good quality.
That is why beings and Buddhas are truly equal. ^6-114

Whatever offering is made to those who have loving-kindness,
that itself comes from the greatness of beings.
And whatever goodness comes from faith in the Buddhas,
that too comes from the greatness of the Buddhas. ^6-115

Since they each have a share
in accomplishing the qualities of a Buddha,
I hold that beings and Buddhas are truly equal in this way.
Yet nothing at all can equal the Buddhas, whose ocean of good qualities has no end. ^6-116

Even if only a small portion of that single, most excellent collection of good qualities
appeared in a being,
offering the three worlds themselves to honor it
would still be far too small. ^6-117

Since beings have a share
in bringing about the most excellent qualities of the Buddha,
it makes sense, in proportion to this share,
to make offerings to beings as well. ^6-118

Furthermore, to those who help me sincerely, without any deceit,
and who bring immeasurable benefit,
what other way is there to repay them,
except by making beings happy? ^6-119

Since the Buddhas repay even those who give up their own bodies
and enter the deepest hell for the sake of beings,
then even if beings cause me great harm,
I will still treat them all in every good way I can. ^6-120

Even my own true lord and teacher
does not spare even his own body for the sake of beings—
so how, confused as I am about this,
could I ever act with pride, and not simply become their servant instead? ^6-121

The Sages feel joy when beings are happy,
and feel sorrow when beings are harmed.
So by making beings joyful, I make all the Sages joyful too,
and by harming beings, I harm the Sages as well. ^6-122

Just as no pleasure at all could make someone's mind happy
while their whole body is engulfed in flames,
in the same way, when beings are harmed,
there is no way for the great compassionate ones to feel joy. ^6-123

So today I confess, one by one,
every wrongdoing by which I have harmed beings
and displeased the great compassionate ones.
I pray that the Sages will be patient with whatever I have done to displease them. ^6-124

From now on, in order to bring joy to the Tathāgatas,
I will become the servant of the whole world.
Even if crowds of beings trample on my head, or kill me,
I will not strike back—may the Protector of the World be pleased instead. ^6-125

There is no doubt that the compassionate nature of all these beings
has become the very nature of these Protectors themselves.
So why would I not show respect
to that very Protector, when I see him in the form of these beings? ^6-126

This alone is what truly pleases the Tathāgatas.
This alone truly accomplishes my own real benefit.
This alone clears away the suffering of the world.
So I will always do this. ^6-127

For example, even if one of a king's officials
harms many people,
those with far-sighted wisdom
do not strike back against him, even if they could. ^6-128

This is because that official is not acting alone—
the king's own power and strength stand behind him.
In the same way, I should never look down on or harm
anyone who seems weak and harms me, however small they seem. ^6-129

This is because the guardians of hell,
and those full of great compassion, stand behind such beings,
just as common people stand behind a fierce king—
so I will work to make all beings happy. ^6-130

Even if a king becomes angry with me,
could that alone bring me the kind of suffering that hell brings,
the suffering that comes
from displeasing beings? ^6-131

Even if a king were pleased with me,
could that alone give me
what comes from making beings happy—
which is nothing less than the gift of Buddhahood itself? ^6-132

Never mind that future Buddhahood
comes from making beings happy—
don't I already see that this very life
brings great glory, fame, and happiness too? ^6-133

Through patience, in the cycle of rebirth,
I gain beauty and other good qualities, freedom from sickness, and a good reputation.
I gain a very long life,
and I gain the vast happiness of a universal ruler. ^6-134

This is the end of the sixth chapter, called "Teaching Patience," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^6-a

## 7. Chapter Seven: Teaching Joyful Effort ^7-0

So, having practiced patience,
I will now take up joyful effort.
Awakening depends on joyful effort,
just as movement depends on wind—without it, no goodness can arise. ^7-1

Joyful effort means finding real enthusiasm for doing good.
I will explain what stands against it:
laziness, clinging to what is bad,
discouragement, and putting yourself down. ^7-2

Savoring the pleasure of idleness,
and craving the comfort of sleep,
not feeling any real sadness about the suffering of endless rebirth—
this is exactly how laziness comes to arise. ^7-3

Caught in the net of disturbing emotions,
trapped inside the snare of rebirth,
and already walking straight toward the Lord of Death—
how can I still not understand this? ^7-4

Haven't you seen your own group, one by one,
being led off to death?
And yet you still rely on sleep,
like an outcaste, or like a buffalo. ^7-5

With every road blocked off,
while the Lord of Death watches you closely,
how can you still enjoy eating,
and how can you still enjoy sleeping like this? ^7-6

Since death will come quickly,
I should gather all the goodness I can while there's still time.
Even if I give up laziness only then,
at that point, what use will it really be? ^7-7

For something not yet begun,
or something left only half done,
when the Lord of Death suddenly arrives,
I will think, "Oh no, I am finished." ^7-8

With eyes swollen red from grief,
tears streaming down my face,
hope gone in the eyes of those close to me,
I will see the face of death's own messenger. ^7-9

Tormented by the memory of my own wrongdoing,
and hearing the sounds coming from hell,
terrified, my body soiled,
what will I be able to do, driven half out of my mind? ^7-10

If I am filled with such fear now, in this life,
like a fish squirming while still alive,
then what need is there to even mention
the unbearable suffering of the hells that come from wrongdoing? ^7-11

Even the touch of hot water
is unbearable to my soft, tender skin—
so having created the causes for hell through my actions,
why do I still rest here so comfortably? ^7-12

Wanting results without any effort,
and being so sensitive that even small things cause great harm—
dying as though I were some kind of god—
oh no, I will be crushed by suffering. ^7-13

Relying on the boat of this human life,
I can cross the great river of suffering.
Since a boat like this is so hard to find again,
fool, don't fall asleep at a time like this! ^7-14

Giving up the best of joys,
the boundless joy found in the true teachings,
why do you instead find joy
in distraction, laughter, and other causes of suffering? ^7-15

Not feeling discouraged, gathering strength,
taking real interest, mastering myself,
treating myself and others as equal,
and exchanging myself for others—these are what I need. ^7-16

I should never feel discouraged, thinking,
"How could someone like me ever reach awakening?"
Since the Tathāgata, who speaks only truth,
has himself declared this truth. ^7-17

Even flies, mosquitoes, bees,
and other small insects like these,
if they develop the power of joyful effort,
can attain the unsurpassed awakening that is so hard to reach. ^7-18

Since I was born human, of a good enough family,
and can tell the difference between what helps and what harms,
why, if I don't give up the bodhisattva's way of acting,
would I not be able to reach awakening myself? ^7-19

If I say, "I'm afraid,
because I would have to give up my hands, feet, and other such things,"
that fear only comes from confusion—
not really weighing what matters more against what matters less. ^7-20

Through countless millions of ages,
I have been cut apart again and again,
burned, and pierced through,
and none of that ever brought me awakening at all. ^7-21

But this suffering, in working to accomplish awakening,
has a real limit to it—
like the pain of a surgeon's cut,
made to remove a harmful growth and end the deeper pain. ^7-22

All doctors cure sickness and discomfort
through methods that themselves may cause some pain.
So, to destroy so much suffering,
I should be willing to bear this small discomfort. ^7-23

But the finest doctor of all
does not use such ordinary methods—
through the very gentlest of means,
he heals boundless, serious sickness. ^7-24

The Guide first has us start
even with giving away small things like vegetables.
Once I've grown used to that,
later, step by step, I can even give away my own flesh. ^7-25

Once the thought arises in me
that my own body is no different from a vegetable,
then what difficulty could there be
in giving away flesh and such at that point? ^7-26

Once wrongdoing is given up, there is no more suffering;
through wisdom, there is no more unhappiness.
It is really wrong thinking and wrongdoing itself
that harm the mind and the body. ^7-27

Goodness brings comfort to the body,
and wisdom brings comfort to the mind—
so even while staying in endless rebirth for the sake of others,
why would someone with compassion ever feel discouraged? ^7-28

Through the power of bodhicitta,
past wrongdoing is used up,
and an ocean of goodness is gathered—
this is why it is said to be even greater than what the hearers accomplish. ^7-29

So, riding the horse of bodhicitta,
which clears away all weariness and exhaustion,
and traveling happily from joy to joy—
what wise person would ever feel discouraged on a journey like this? ^7-30

To truly accomplish the good of every being, I need four powers:
aspiration, steadiness, joy, and letting go.
Aspiration comes from thinking about the suffering and fear of endless rebirth,
and about the real benefits of practice. ^7-31

So, giving up whatever stands against it,
I will work hard, with real strength of aspiration,
confidence, joy, and letting go, mastering myself,
to help joyful effort grow. ^7-32

I need to overcome countless faults,
both my own and others',
and even for just one single fault,
it would take an ocean of ages to fully clear away. ^7-33

Yet I don't see myself even beginning
to work on clearing away a fraction of my own faults—
so why doesn't my heart break,
facing this place of boundless suffering? ^7-34

I need to accomplish so many good qualities,
both my own and others',
and it would take an ocean of ages of practice
to develop even one of them fully. ^7-35

Yet I have never even begun
to develop a single one of these good qualities—
it's astonishing how I have wasted
this precious human life that is so hard to find. ^7-36

I have not made offerings to the Blessed One,
I have not given the joy of a great celebration,
I have done nothing for the teachings,
and I have not fulfilled the wishes of the poor. ^7-37

I have not given fearlessness to the frightened,
or comfort to the distressed—
I have only caused the pain of my mother's womb,
and suffering, again and again. ^7-38

In the past, and even now,
I have lacked real aspiration for the true teachings—
this is why I have ended up in such poverty.
So who would ever want to give up their aspiration for the teachings? ^7-39

The Sage has said that aspiration
is the root of every kind of goodness,
and that the root of aspiration itself
is always meditating on the ripening of results. ^7-40

Suffering, unhappiness of mind,
every kind of fear,
and being separated from what I want—
all of this comes from wrongdoing. ^7-41

But whoever does good with a sincere mind,
wherever they may go from this life,
will be honored there
with the fruits of their own goodness. ^7-42

And whoever does wrong, even while wanting happiness,
wherever they may go from this life,
will be struck down completely
by the sword of their own wrongdoing's suffering. ^7-43

Resting in the heart of a vast, sweetly scented lotus,
given splendor by the delightful words of the Victor as food,
with the finest body, born from a lotus opened by the Sage's light,
in the presence of the Victor, one becomes a child of the Sugata through goodness. ^7-44

But if instead the messenger of death strips away all my skin, leaving me utterly wretched,
and pours molten, burning copper over my body,
and I am struck by blazing swords and spears, my flesh sliced into a hundred pieces,
and I fall onto ground of red-hot iron, ablaze—all of this comes from so much wrongdoing. ^7-45

So I will hold real aspiration for goodness,
and train myself in genuine respect for it.
Taking up the way of Vajradhvaja's ritual,
I will train in true confidence as well. ^7-46

First, I should carefully check what I am truly able to do,
and then either begin, or not begin at all.
It is better not to start at all,
than to start something and then turn back. ^7-47

If I turn back, that habit carries into future lives,
and my wrongdoing and suffering only grow.
And when the time for another task and its result comes,
that too will end up weaker, and won't be accomplished either. ^7-48

There are three things I need real confidence in:
my actions, disturbing emotions, and my own ability.
"I alone will do this"—
this is confidence in action. ^7-49

This world, so powerless under disturbing emotions,
cannot accomplish its own true benefit.
Since these beings cannot do what I can do,
I am the one who must do this instead. ^7-50

When others do lowly work,
how could I just stand by and do nothing?
I won't act out of arrogance, though—
having no real arrogance at all is what's truly best for me. ^7-51

When even a crow meets a dead snake,
it can act as bold as an eagle.
In the same way, if I let myself feel weak,
even a small setback can end up harming me. ^7-52

If I give up trying, feeling defeated before I even start,
is there really any way to become free from poverty and hardship?
But if I develop real confidence, and keep making effort,
even great difficulties become hard to overcome me. ^7-53

So, with a truly steady mind,
I will work to overcome every downfall.
If I let myself be defeated by downfalls instead,
wanting to conquer the three worlds becomes something to laugh at. ^7-54

I will overcome everything,
and let nothing overcome me.
As a child of the Victor, the Lion,
I should hold on to this kind of confidence. ^7-55

Beings overpowered by pride are not the ones with this true confidence—
theirs is only the pride of disturbing emotions.
The truly confident are never controlled by their enemies,
while the proud fall under the power of the enemy that is pride itself. ^7-56

Those puffed up with the pride of disturbing emotions
are led by that very pride down to the lower realms,
robbed of all human joy,
forced to live like servants eating the scraps of others, ^7-57

seen as dull, unattractive, weak,
and looked down upon by everyone everywhere.
Even those who practice hardship, if they are still puffed up with this kind of pride,
count as among the proud—
so tell me, what could be more lowly than that? ^7-58

But whoever holds on to true confidence in order to defeat the enemy that is pride—
that person is truly confident, a hero who wins complete victory.
Whoever fully destroys the enemy of pride when it tries to rise up again,
brings to completion, just as they wish, the fruits of victory for the sake of all beings. ^7-59

While living surrounded on every side by disturbing emotions,
I should hold my ground steady against them a thousand times over—
like a lion among jackals,
never letting the whole crowd of disturbing emotions overpower me. ^7-60

Just as someone in great danger
instinctively protects their own eyes,
in the same way, even when real danger arises,
I will never let myself fall under the power of disturbing emotions. ^7-61

Just as I long for the pleasure that comes from playing a game,
I should feel that same eagerness for whatever task lies ahead of me—
staying attached to that task, and never growing tired of doing it,
finding joy in it instead. ^7-62

People act for the sake of happiness,
though whether it will actually bring happiness is uncertain.
But how could there be happiness
in not doing the very actions that surely do bring happiness? ^7-63

If people are never satisfied by desires,
sweet as honey on a razor's edge,
then how could I ever be satisfied
with the peaceful happiness that ripens from goodness? ^7-64

So, to bring a task to its very completion,
I should throw myself into it fully,
like an elephant scorched by the midday sun
plunging straight into a cool lake. ^7-65

When my strength runs low,
I should set the task aside for a while, to take it up again later.
And when it is done well,
I should let it go, looking ahead to what comes next. ^7-66

Like a soldier facing an old enemy in the heat of battle, sword drawn and ready,
I should turn aside the weapons of disturbing emotions,
and press forward
to strike down the enemy that is disturbing emotions itself. ^7-67

Just as, in battle, if a sword falls,
one quickly picks it up again out of fear,
in the same way, if I lose hold of the sword of mindfulness,
I should quickly pick it back up, remembering the fear of hell. ^7-68

Just as poison, once it enters the blood,
spreads quickly through the whole body,
in the same way, once wrongdoing finds an opening,
it spreads quickly through the whole mind. ^7-69

Like someone carrying a jar filled with mustard oil,
closely watched by a person holding a sword,
threatened with death if they spill even a drop,
that is how carefully someone with real discipline should stay focused and alert. ^7-70

So, just as I would leap up quickly
if a snake landed in my lap,
in the same way, whenever sleepiness or laziness comes,
I should turn it away right away. ^7-71

Whenever I make a mistake,
I should look at it carefully each time, and blame myself for it,
thinking for a long time
about how to make sure it never happens to me again. ^7-72

Thinking, "How can I make mindfulness a true habit
in situations like these?"
I should seek out the company of people who can help with this,
or work that is truly worthwhile. ^7-73

So that I am ready to act even before a task begins,
and have real strength for anything,
I should recall the teaching on carefulness,
and rouse myself to be light and ready for action. ^7-74

Just as a wisp of cotton
is carried along by the wind's coming and going,
in the same way, I should let myself be carried along by real enthusiasm—
that is how everything gets accomplished. ^7-75

This is the end of the seventh chapter, called "Teaching Joyful Effort," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^7-a

## 8. Chapter Eight: Teaching Meditation ^8-0

So, having generated joyful effort like this,
I will now settle my mind in deep meditation.
Someone whose mind is scattered and distracted
lives right between the fangs of disturbing emotions. ^8-1

Through solitude of body and mind,
no real distraction can arise.
So I will let go of worldly life,
and give up all my restless, wandering thoughts completely. ^8-2

Because of attachment, and craving for gain and other such things,
I don't truly let go of worldly life.
So I will give all of this up completely,
and, like someone wise, I will examine it carefully like this. ^8-3

Knowing that insight, joined with true calm steadiness,
is what fully defeats disturbing emotions,
I should first seek out calm steadiness itself—
and this comes about through real joy in letting go of worldly attachment. ^8-4

I myself am so impermanent,
so how could I truly cling to someone else who is impermanent too?
Because of that clinging, for a thousand lifetimes,
I will never get to see someone I love. ^8-5

If I don't see them, I won't feel happy,
and my mind won't be able to settle either.
And even if I do see them, I'm never satisfied—
so, just as before, craving will keep torturing me. ^8-6

If I let myself cling to beings,
it completely blocks me from seeing things clearly.
It even destroys my sense of sadness about endless rebirth,
and in the end, only grief and torment come from it. ^8-7

Just thinking about them alone
can make this whole life pass by pointlessly.
And because of friends who never really last,
even the eternal truth can be destroyed in me. ^8-8

If I share the same fate as someone foolish,
I will surely end up in a lower rebirth.
And if they lead me somewhere completely different from where I want to go,
what's the use of relying on someone foolish at all? ^8-9

In one moment they're my friend,
and in the very next, they've become my enemy.
They get angry even about things that should bring joy—
it's so hard to keep ordinary people truly happy. ^8-10

If I speak to help them, they only get angry,
and they even try to turn me away from doing good.
And if I don't listen to what they say,
out of anger, they lead me toward a lower rebirth. ^8-11

They feel jealous of those above them, and compete with their equals;
they feel proud toward those below them, and grow arrogant when praised.
If I say something they don't like, anger rises in them—
when could I ever really benefit from someone foolish? ^8-12

Spending time with someone foolish,
I end up praising myself and criticizing others,
and talking about how great endless rebirth is—
something not good is bound to come from it, no matter what. ^8-13

So relying on each other like this,
foolish people, only ends up bringing harm to both of us—
they don't really help me,
and I don't really help them either. ^8-14

So I should stay far away from foolish company.
If I do happen to meet them, I will be pleasant and kind,
but without becoming too close—
it's best to just stay simply friendly. ^8-15

Like a bee taking only the nectar from a flower,
I should take just what's truly meaningful from the teachings,
and, as if I had never met them before,
stay without becoming too familiar with anyone. ^8-16

If I hold on to the proud thought,
"I have so many things, and so much honor, and so many people who like me,"
then after I die,
real fear will arise in me. ^8-17

So whatever my confused mind becomes attached to,
that very thing, once it's gone,
gives rise to a hundred, thousand kinds of suffering
that keep arising again and again. ^8-18

So someone wise will not let themselves grow attached;
real fear arises from attachment.
Since everything, by its very nature, will one day be lost anyway,
I will stay steady, and understand this deeply. ^8-19

Even if I gain a lot,
and become famous and well spoken of,
none of it—my gains, my fame, all of it together—
comes with me to wherever I go next. ^8-20

If someone criticizes me,
what real reason is there to feel happy about being praised?
And if someone praises me,
what real reason is there to feel upset about being criticized? ^8-21

Beings have so many different wishes—
even the Victors themselves cannot please them all.
So what need is there to even mention someone as ordinary as me?
So I will let go of caring about what the world thinks. ^8-22

Beings criticize those who have nothing,
and speak badly of those who have plenty.
By nature, they are so hard to please—
how could real happiness ever come from being close to them? ^8-23

Since a foolish person can never feel happy
unless it's for their own benefit,
the Tathāgatas themselves have said
that no foolish person is really a true friend. ^8-24

Deer, birds, and trees in the forest
never say anything unpleasant.
When will I get to live together
with such peaceful, easy company? ^8-25

Staying in a cave, an empty temple, or beneath a tree,
when will I finally be able to move on
without looking back,
completely free of attachment? ^8-26

In places that belong to no one,
naturally wide open and free,
when will I finally live there, moving freely,
without any attachment at all? ^8-27

With only a begging bowl and a few other simple things,
wearing clothes no one else would want,
not even bothering to hide this body of mine,
when will I finally be able to live without any fear at all? ^8-28

When will I finally go to a charnel ground,
and see clearly that other people's bones,
and my own body too,
are both things that will fall apart the same way? ^8-29

This very body of mine will one day smell so bad
that even jackals won't want to come near it—
that is exactly
what it will become. ^8-30

Even this one body of mine, born as a single whole,
with flesh and bone all grown together,
will fall apart and scatter into separate pieces—
so what need is there to even mention other loved ones? ^8-31

When I am born, I am born alone,
and when I die, I die alone too.
Since no one else can share my suffering with me,
what use are loved ones who only get in my way? ^8-32

Just as travelers on a road
take up lodging along the way,
in the same way, someone walking the road of endless rebirth
takes up one birthplace after another. ^8-33

So, before four people come to carry my body away,
while the whole world still mourns for me,
I should go now to the forest,
while I still can. ^8-34

Without friends and without enemies,
I will live alone, in solitude, with this body,
having already thought of myself as already dead—
so that when death actually comes, no one will grieve over it. ^8-35

With no one nearby to cause me grief or harm,
nothing will distract me
from remembering the Buddha,
and other such practices. ^8-36

So I will live alone,
in a forest that is truly delightful, peaceful, easy, and free of hardship,
where every kind of distraction
can be calmed completely. ^8-37

Letting go of every other concern,
with a mind focused single-pointedly,
I will work hard to settle my mind in meditation,
and to truly tame it. ^8-38

Ordinary desires cause disaster,
both in this life and in lives to come.
Here, they lead to being killed, bound, and cut apart;
in the next life, they bring about hell and other such places. ^8-39

For the sake of desire, in the past,
I have begged messengers, both men and women, again and again,
and, for its sake,
I have not hesitated even at wrongdoing or a bad reputation. ^8-40

I have thrown myself into danger for it,
and used up all my wealth because of it—
and yet the very thing I embraced so tightly
is exactly what brought me the greatest joy of all. ^8-41

It is really nothing more than a skeleton, nothing else at all—
it has no freedom of its own, and it is not truly "me" either.
So why, wanting it and clinging to it so completely,
do I not simply let it go and find real peace instead? ^8-42

Someone who once worked hard to lift her veil,
blushing to look down even when someone drew her forward,
whether seen by others before or not,
now has her face covered by only a plain cloth. ^8-43

That very face of yours, so troubled by disturbing emotions,
now lies right in front of everyone to see—
once a vulture tears the cloth away and you see it exposed,
why do you still shrink back from it now? ^8-44

Even when others simply look at it with their eyes,
you guard it so carefully—
but while it's being eaten by that very thing right now,
why, so full of stinginess, do you not guard it then? ^8-45

Seeing this mass of flesh being eaten
by vultures and other creatures,
why do you honor what is really just food for others
with garlands of flowers and sandalwood ornaments? ^8-46

If you're already frightened
just seeing it as a motionless skeleton,
then why aren't you afraid
when someone moves it around like a walking corpse? ^8-47

From the very same food,
both waste and saliva come about—
so why do you dislike the waste,
and yet feel drawn to the saliva instead? ^8-48

If you don't feel drawn to a soft pillow, gentle to the touch,
just because it doesn't seem to give off any smell,
then, desiring the body instead,
you are simply confused about what is truly unclean. ^8-49

If you're drawn to it even when it's covered,
why don't you want it just as much when it isn't?
And if you don't really need the covering at all,
then why do you hold onto it so tightly? ^8-50

If you're not really drawn to what's unclean,
then why do you embrace another person in your arms—
a frame of bones tied together with sinew,
plastered over with mud made of flesh? ^8-51

You yourself are full of so many unclean things,
and you constantly make use of them—
and yet, out of craving for what's unclean,
you also want this other bag of unclean things too. ^8-52

If you say, "I love this flesh,"
and want to touch it and look at it,
then how can you really want flesh
that has no mind at all, by its very nature? ^8-53

The mind you actually desire
cannot be touched or seen at all,
and what can be touched or seen has no awareness of its own—
so what's the point of this pointless embrace? ^8-54

It's not so surprising that you fail to see
that another person's body is unclean by its very nature.
But it's truly astonishing that you fail to understand
that your own body is exactly the same. ^8-55

Turning away from a young lotus,
freshly opened by the rays of a cloudless sun,
why, with a mind so drawn to what's unclean,
would you take joy instead in a mere basket of impurity? ^8-56

If you don't even want to touch a patch of ground
that's been soiled with something unclean,
then how can you want to touch the very body
that produced it in the first place? ^8-57

If you're truly not drawn to what's unclean,
then why do you embrace another person in your arms,
when they grew from an unclean field,
planted there by that very seed? ^8-58

You don't even want a tiny worm that grew from something unclean,
and yet you desire a body
that is unclean by nature through and through,
itself born from something unclean. ^8-59

Not only do you not find fault
with your own uncleanness,
but, craving it,
you go on desiring yet another bag of uncleanness too. ^8-60

Even nice things like camphor, cooked rice, or vegetables,
once put in the mouth and spat back out,
make the ground itself
unclean and dirty. ^8-61

If, even seeing something this unclean right in front of you,
you still doubt it,
then go and look at other unclean bodies,
discarded in the charnel ground. ^8-62

Knowing that, once the skin is peeled back,
great fear will surely arise,
how can you still, again and again,
feel drawn to that very same body? ^8-63

That pleasant scent applied to the body
is really the scent of sandalwood or something else, not the body's own smell—
so why would you feel drawn to another person
because of a scent that isn't even theirs? ^8-64

If its natural smell is so unpleasant,
wouldn't it be better not to feel drawn to it at all?
Why do people pointlessly perfume it
with sweet scents for no real reason? ^8-65

And if the sweet scent really comes only from the sandalwood,
what has that got to do with the body itself?
So why would you feel drawn to someone else
because of a scent that belongs to something else entirely? ^8-66

If the body's true nature—long hair and nails, yellowed teeth,
all covered with a smell like foul mud—
is, when stripped bare,
something truly frightening to see, ^8-67

then why work so hard grooming it,
as if polishing a weapon that will only harm you?
Confused effort spent on yourself like this
is exactly the kind of madness that has thrown this whole world into such turmoil. ^8-68

If just seeing a bare skeleton in a charnel ground
already fills you with dread,
then how could you feel drawn to a town,
so full of these very same moving skeletons? ^8-69

Something so unclean like this
is never gained without a cost—
pursuing it exhausts the body,
and even leads to the harm of hell and other such places. ^8-70

As a child, someone cannot yet earn wealth,
so how is any of this really enjoyable in youth?
And once life is spent gathering wealth,
once old age comes, what use is desire anyway? ^8-71

Some people, driven by desire,
work themselves completely exhausted all day long,
and then come home only to collapse into bed,
their bodies as lifeless as a corpse. ^8-72

Some travel far from home,
suffering from disturbing emotions and the pain of separation,
and even though they long for their family,
they can go for years without ever seeing them again. ^8-73

Confused about what would truly benefit them,
some even sell themselves for the very thing they want,
and yet, without ever gaining it,
end up swept along, for nothing at all, by the demands of someone else's work. ^8-74

Some sell their very own bodies,
and end up completely under someone else's control—
and when their wives give birth,
they must give birth wherever they happen to be, even beneath a tree or in some empty wilderness. ^8-75

Fooled by desire, foolish people, just to make a living,
throw themselves into battle even while fearing for their lives,
and, for the sake of profit,
willingly become someone else's servant. ^8-76

Because of desire, some have their very bodies cut apart,
some are impaled on sharpened stakes,
some are stabbed with spears,
and some are even burned alive. ^8-77

Understand that wealth brings endless trouble—
the anguish of gathering it, guarding it, and losing it.
Those whose minds are so caught up in craving for wealth
will never find any real chance to be free from the suffering of endless rebirth. ^8-78

For those driven by desire, troubles like these are many,
while the reward is so small—
just like an ox pulling a cart,
who only gets to eat a few mouthfuls of grass for all its labor. ^8-79

For the sake of such a small reward, one that even an ox could earn just as easily,
people driven by their own actions destroy
this precious human life, so hard to find,
with all its freedoms and advantages. ^8-80

Ordinary desires are certain to fall apart in the end,
and can even lead down to hell and other such places—
and yet, since the effort spent chasing them
is never really that great, ^8-81

if I spent even a tiny fraction of that same effort,
I could actually accomplish Buddhahood itself.
So compared to the bodhisattva path, someone chasing after desire
suffers far more, and gains no awakening at all. ^8-82

When I truly think about the suffering of hell and other such places,
I realize that not even weapons, poison, fire, a steep cliff,
or my worst enemies
could ever compare to the suffering caused by ordinary desires. ^8-83

So, feeling this weariness with ordinary desire,
I will find real joy in solitude instead,
in peaceful forests,
free from conflict and disturbing emotions, ^8-84

in a fine, spacious stone dwelling, cooled by moonlight and sandalwood,
delightful to be in,
where the silent, peaceful forest breeze stirs gently,
and, walking there, I will think only of how to help others. ^8-85

Staying as long as I like in an empty dwelling, beneath a tree, or in a cave,
I will give up the suffering
of guarding and holding onto possessions,
and live at ease, free and unconcerned. ^8-86

Living freely, without attachment,
tied down by nothing at all,
content and at peace—
even a great ruler would find happiness like this hard to come by. ^8-87

Thinking in this way, and in other ways too,
about the real benefits of solitude,
I will calm all my restless, wandering thoughts,
and meditate on bodhicitta. ^8-88

First of all, I will work hard
to meditate on the equality of myself and others.
Since happiness and suffering are the same for everyone,
I should care for every being just as I care for myself. ^8-89

Just as hands and other parts, though many and different,
are all treated as one body to be cared for,
in the same way, all beings, though different,
share this one same thing—they all equally want happiness, just as I do. ^8-90

Even though my own suffering
doesn't actually harm anyone else's body,
I still find that suffering unbearable,
simply because I cling so tightly to the thought, "This is mine." ^8-91

In the same way, even though someone else's suffering
doesn't actually fall on me,
I find it hard to bear
only because I cling so tightly to the thought, "This is myself." ^8-92

So I will clear away the suffering of others,
since it is suffering, just like my own.
And I will help other beings,
since they are beings, just as I care for my own body. ^8-93

Since both myself and others
equally want happiness,
what real difference is there between us,
that I should work so hard for my own happiness alone? ^8-94

Since both myself and others
equally don't want suffering,
what real difference is there between us,
that I should protect only myself and not others? ^8-95

If I say I won't protect others from suffering
because their suffering doesn't harm me,
then, by that same logic, why do I protect myself
from future suffering, which doesn't harm me either, right now? ^8-96

The thought, "I am the one who will experience that future suffering,"
is actually a mistaken way of thinking—
the one who dies is, in truth,
different from the one who is born. ^8-97

If it's true that only the one who suffers
should protect against their own suffering,
then my hand would have no reason to protect my foot from pain—
so why should one part protect another at all? ^8-98

If you say, "Even though it's not really logical,
we act this way because we cling to the idea of a self,"
then this same illogical clinging to "self" and "other"
is exactly what I should work as hard as I can to let go of. ^8-99

Things like "mindstream" and "collection" are only labels,
just like the words "garland" or "army"—not truly real in themselves.
Since there is no truly separate owner of suffering,
who exactly could have real power over it? ^8-100

Since suffering has no truly separate owner,
there is really no fundamental difference between one being's suffering and another's.
It should simply be cleared away, because it is suffering—
so what use is there in insisting on some fixed distinction here? ^8-101

There's no real reason to argue about why everyone's suffering
should be cleared away—if it should be cleared away at all,
then it should be cleared away for everyone.
Otherwise, I too am simply another being, no different from the rest. ^8-102

If someone asks, "Why work so hard to develop compassion,
when it brings so much suffering with it?"—
but when I truly think about the suffering of all beings,
how could compassion's suffering ever be considered too much? ^8-103

If one bit of suffering, felt through compassion,
could actually clear away so much greater suffering for everyone,
then someone with real love should be willing to bring about that suffering,
both for themselves and for others. ^8-104

This is exactly why Candraprabha, even knowing the king would harm him,
did not try to avoid his own suffering—
because it would bring an end
to the suffering of so many others. ^8-105

Someone whose mind has grown truly used to this,
finding real joy in calming the suffering of others,
will even enter the deepest hell
as easily as a swan settling onto a lotus pond. ^8-106

When beings are finally set free,
isn't the ocean of joy that comes from that alone truly enough?
So why would I
seek freedom only for myself? ^8-107

So, even while working for the good of others,
I will feel no pride or amazement about it—
since I find joy purely in helping others,
I have no need to hope for any reward in return. ^8-108

So, just as I protect myself even from small unpleasant things,
in the very same way, I will protect others too,
and develop this same kind of
protective, compassionate mind. ^8-109

Just as, through habit,
I have come to think of a mere drop of my parents' fluids,
once there was nothing else there,
as truly "me," ^8-110

in the same way, why shouldn't I also think of another person's body as "me"?
And it should be no harder
to place the thought of "myself"
onto someone else's body instead. ^8-111

Seeing clearly that I myself am full of faults,
while others have an ocean of good qualities,
I will meditate on completely letting go of clinging to myself,
and taking up others instead. ^8-112

Just as we naturally think of a hand
as simply a part of the body,
why shouldn't I think of every being as simply a part of the whole world of beings,
in the very same way? ^8-113

Just as, through habit, the thought of "me"
has come to apply to this body, which really has no self in it at all,
why, through that same habit,
could the thought of "me" not come to apply to other beings too? ^8-114

In this way, even while working for the good of others,
no pride or amazement will arise in me—
just as I don't expect anything in return
simply for feeding myself. ^8-115

So, just as I protect myself even from small unpleasant things,
in the very same way, I will make it a real habit
to protect all beings,
and to develop this compassionate, protective mind. ^8-116

This is exactly why the Protector Avalokiteśvara,
out of his great compassion,
even blessed his own name, so that just hearing it
could clear away the fears of beings caught in endless rebirth. ^8-117

So I will not turn back from this difficult task—
because through the power of real habit,
even something whose very name once caused fear
can, once it's gone, become something I actually miss. ^8-118

Whoever truly wants to quickly protect
both themselves and others
should practice this most sacred, secret instruction:
exchanging myself for others. ^8-119

Because of clinging to this body of mine,
even small dangers give rise to real fear in me.
So who wouldn't feel a kind of anger toward this very body, as if toward an enemy,
since it is the true source of all this fear? ^8-120

Wanting to cure the body's ailments, like hunger and thirst,
people go so far as to kill birds, fish, and wild animals,
and lie in wait
to hunt them. ^8-121

For the sake of profit and respect, some people even kill their own parents,
and steal offerings meant for the Three Jewels—
and because of this,
they will burn in the deepest hell. ^8-122

What wise person, then, would still want
to cherish, protect, and honor this body?
Who wouldn't instead look at it as an enemy,
and refuse to hold it in such high regard? ^8-123

"If I give this away, what will I have left to use?"—
thinking only of my own benefit like this is the way of a demon.
"If I use this myself, what will I have left to give?"—
thinking of others' benefit like this is the way of the gods. ^8-124

If I harm others for my own sake,
I will be tormented in hell and other such places.
But if I let myself be harmed for the sake of others,
I will gain every kind of true excellence instead. ^8-125

Wanting to raise myself above others
leads only to a bad rebirth, and to real foolishness.
But if I direct that same wish toward others instead,
I will gain a good rebirth, and real respect. ^8-126

If I use others for my own purposes,
I will experience being made into a servant myself.
But if I put myself to use for the sake of others,
I will experience becoming a true leader instead. ^8-127

Every happiness there is in this world
comes from wanting others to be happy.
Every suffering there is in this world
comes from wanting only my own happiness. ^8-128

What need is there to say any more?
Just look at the real difference
between someone foolish, working only for themselves,
and a Sage, working only for the good of others. ^8-129

If I never truly exchange my own happiness
for the suffering of others,
I will never actually reach Buddhahood,
and I won't find real happiness in endless rebirth either. ^8-130

Never mind the next life—even in this one,
if a servant refuses to do their work,
and a master refuses to pay them fairly,
neither one will really get what they want. ^8-131

By causing suffering to others, out of confusion,
people give up every kind of true happiness, both seen and unseen,
and, instead,
bring unbearable suffering upon themselves. ^8-132

Whatever harm there is in this world,
whatever fear and suffering there is,
all of it comes from clinging to the thought of "self."
So what use is this great demon of self-clinging to me at all? ^8-133

If I don't let go of this clinging to myself completely,
I will never be able to escape suffering—
just as, if I don't let go of fire,
I will never be able to escape being burned. ^8-134

So, in order to calm the harm caused by self-clinging,
and to calm the suffering of others,
I will give myself over to others completely,
and hold every being just as dear as I hold myself. ^8-135

So, my mind, understand this clearly:
I now belong to others.
From now on, don't think of anything at all,
except the good of every being. ^8-136

It's not right to use my eyes and other senses,
which now belong to others, only for my own benefit.
And it's not right to use them
against the very beings they belong to either. ^8-137

So I will hold beings as my highest priority—
whatever good things I find in my own body,
I will take them, one by one,
and use them to help others instead. ^8-138

Thinking of myself as lower, and of others as truly myself,
I will, without any confused overthinking,
practice feeling jealousy, rivalry, and even pride—
but now, on behalf of others. ^8-139

"This person is honored, but not me.
I haven't gained what they have.
This person is praised, while I am criticized.
They are happy, while I am the one who suffers." ^8-140

"I am the one doing all the hard work, while this person just enjoys themselves.
They are seen as great in the eyes of the world,
while I am known as lowly,
with no good qualities at all." ^8-141

"But what does it matter if I have no good qualities?
Everyone has some good quality in them.
There is always someone this person is better than,
and there is always someone I myself am better than too." ^8-142

"If my discipline or views have declined,
it's really not my own fault, but the power of disturbing emotions.
I should do everything I can to help myself heal,
and I am even willing to accept some hardship for it." ^8-143

"But if I'm not even someone worth helping,
why do you look down on me like this?
What use are this person's good qualities to me anyway?
I have good qualities of my own." ^8-144

"This person, right on the edge of a terrible lower rebirth,
has no real compassion for beings at all,
and yet, boasting outwardly about their own good qualities,
they still want to compete with those who are truly wise." ^8-145

"So, looking at someone equal to myself,
I will do whatever it takes, even competing openly,
to make sure I gain more,
and am honored more, than they are." ^8-146

"I will make sure my own good qualities become known to the whole world,
while making sure
that no one ever hears about this other person's good qualities
at all." ^8-147

"I will hide my own faults completely,
so that people honor me instead of this other person.
From now on, I should be the one who gains,
and I should be the one who is honored, not them." ^8-148

"I will watch, with real satisfaction, for a long time,
whenever bad things happen to this person,
and I will make sure they become a laughingstock
and an object of ridicule to everyone." ^8-149

"This troublesome person even claims to be my equal!
Do they really match me
in learning, wisdom, looks,
family, or wealth?" ^8-150

"Hearing my own good qualities announced like this to everyone,
with the hair on my body standing on end from sheer joy,
I will fully enjoy
this happiness." ^8-151

"Even if this person has some gains,
if they end up working for me,
I will just give them enough to get by,
and take the rest for myself, using my own power." ^8-152

"I will make sure this person loses their happiness,
and always faces trouble because of me—
after all, in a hundred different ways,
they have harmed me throughout endless rebirth." ^8-153

My mind, wanting only your own benefit like this,
has already let countless ages pass by,
and through all that immense effort,
you have accomplished nothing but suffering for yourself. ^8-154

So now, truly commit yourself
to working for the good of others instead—
since the Sage's words never deceive,
you will come to see the real benefit of this for yourself later on. ^8-155

If you had done this work of caring for others long ago,
you would never have ended up
in this state,
so far from the perfect happiness of Buddhahood. ^8-156

So, just as you have already learned, through habit,
to think of a mere drop of your parents' fluids as truly "you,"
in that same way,
learn to think of others as truly yourself too. ^8-157

Becoming a real spy on behalf of others,
take whatever good things you find in your own body,
and use them, one by one,
to help others instead. ^8-158

"I am happy, while others are not.
I am important, while others are lowly.
I am the one who helps, while others don't."—
so why don't I feel some real jealousy toward myself for thinking this way? ^8-159

I will let go of my own happiness,
and take on the suffering of others instead.
And whenever the thought arises, "What is this doing to me?"—
I will simply examine it as one more fault of my own to work on. ^8-160

Even a wrong done by someone else,
I will turn into an admission of my own fault.
And even a small mistake of my own,
I will confess openly to many people. ^8-161

By speaking up more about others' good reputation,
I will let my own be overshadowed.
And I will make myself useful to everyone,
like the lowest of servants. ^8-162

This body is, by nature, full of faults—
I will not praise it, even for whatever incidental good qualities it happens to have.
And I will make sure that no one ever even hears about
any good qualities it might have at all. ^8-163

In short, whatever harm you have caused others
for the sake of your own benefit,
mind, let that same harm fall onto yourself instead,
for the sake of every being. ^8-164

I should not let this mind become stubborn or unruly,
encouraging its bad habits.
Instead, like a shy new bride,
I will hold it back with real modesty and caution. ^8-165

"Do this, stay like that, don't do this at all"—
this is how I will keep control over my own mind,
and if it ever oversteps these bounds,
I will discipline it firmly. ^8-166

And if, even after being instructed like this,
you, my mind, still refuse to listen,
then, since every fault depends entirely on you,
I will simply have to discipline you firmly instead. ^8-167

That earlier time, when you led me so badly astray,
is now over and done with.
I see you clearly now, mind—where could you possibly go?
I will break down all of your pride. ^8-168

Give up, once and for all,
the thought that you still have some benefit of your own to seek.
I have already sold you to others, mind,
so don't be sad about it—just serve them well, without complaint. ^8-169

And if, out of carelessness,
you fail to give yourself over to beings,
then I promise you, mind,
I will hand you over to the guardians of hell instead. ^8-170

That's exactly how, in the past, you made me suffer for so long,
whenever you gave me over to your own selfish wishes.
So now, remembering all of that,
I will break down this very thought of self-interest in you. ^8-171

So if I truly want happiness,
I will not seek it selfishly for myself alone.
And if I truly want protection,
I will constantly work to protect others instead. ^8-172

The more I go on pampering and protecting this body of mine,
the more it falls, again and again,
into being overly delicate
and sensitive. ^8-173

And once it has fallen this far,
even everything in this whole world put together
could never fully satisfy its endless wants—
so who could ever really fulfill its desires? ^8-174

Wanting what I cannot have only brings on disturbing emotions,
and weakens my own resolve.
But whoever depends on nothing at all
will find their true excellence never runs out. ^8-175

So I will not give the body's desires
any opportunity to grow.
Whoever does not cling to pleasant things
is truly someone with real inner richness. ^8-176

In the end, this body only comes down to a pile of ashes,
motionless, moved only by others,
an unbearably unclean thing—
so why would I still cling to it as "me"? ^8-177

Whether alive or dead,
what real use does this mechanical contraption of a body have for me?
What real difference is there between it and a clod of earth?
Alas, why don't I let go of my pride about it? ^8-178

By flattering and indulging this body,
I've only piled up pointless suffering for myself.
What's the use of feeling attachment or anger
toward something as senseless as a piece of wood? ^8-179

Whether I care for it so carefully,
or vultures end up eating it, it makes no difference to the body itself—
it feels neither attachment nor anger.
So why do I still feel so attached to it? ^8-180

Since the body itself doesn't even know
what it means to feel anger at criticism, or pleasure at praise,
then who exactly
have I been working so hard for, all this time? ^8-181

If I say, "Whoever wants this body of mine is truly my friend"—
then since everyone wants their own body too,
why shouldn't I feel that same friendliness
toward everyone else as well? ^8-182

So, without any attachment,
I will give this body over for the benefit of all beings.
And even though it has so many faults,
I will keep using it, simply as a tool for good work. ^8-183

So, enough of acting like a foolish child—
I will follow instead in the footsteps of the wise.
Remembering the teaching on carefulness,
I will turn away sleepiness and dullness of mind. ^8-184

So, in order to clear away every obstacle,
I will turn my mind away from wrong paths,
and, resting it always on what is truly meaningful,
I will keep it settled in steady meditation. ^8-185

This is the end of the eighth chapter, called "Teaching Meditation," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^8-a

## 9. Chapter Nine: The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0

The Sage taught all these other practices
for the sake of wisdom.
So, if I want to bring an end to suffering,
I should develop wisdom. ^9-1

There are said to be two kinds of truth:
everyday truth, and ultimate truth.
Ultimate truth is beyond the reach of ordinary thought;
ordinary thought itself is said to belong to everyday truth. ^9-2

Within this, there are two ways of seeing the world:
the way of those who practice deeply, and the way of ordinary people.
Ordinary understanding is corrected
by the understanding of those who practice deeply. ^9-3

And among practitioners themselves,
those with deeper understanding correct those with less.
Both sides use the same kind of example,
without examining what they're really trying to prove. ^9-4

Ordinary people see things as truly real,
and think of them that way, not as something like an illusion—
this is exactly where practitioners
and ordinary people disagree. ^9-5

Even seeing things like form directly
is only true by common agreement, not by real, valid proof—
just as calling something unclean "clean"
is a common but mistaken way of speaking. ^9-6

Someone might object: "The Protector taught things as real,
simply to guide ordinary people into the teachings—
but if things aren't truly momentary,
doesn't that even contradict everyday truth itself?" ^9-7

There's no real problem with how practitioners understand everyday truth,
since they see things as they really are, in comparison to ordinary people.
Otherwise, if practitioners thought a woman's body were unclean the way ordinary people don't,
ordinary understanding would actually contradict theirs instead. ^9-8

Just as goodness comes from honoring the Victors, even though they're like an illusion,
in the very same way, goodness comes from honoring them as if they were truly real.
Someone might ask: "If beings are like an illusion,
how can they be reborn after death?" ^9-9

For as long as the right conditions come together,
even an illusion can keep appearing.
So how could beings be truly, solidly real,
just because that appearance lasts a long time? ^9-10

Killing an illusory person causes no real wrongdoing,
since it has no real mind.
But goodness and wrongdoing do arise toward beings who have a mind,
even if that mind is, in a sense, like an illusion too. ^9-11

Mantras and such things
have no power to produce an illusory mind.
An illusion that arises from many different conditions
is itself something varied and complex. ^9-12

There is no single condition
powerful enough to produce everything by itself.
Someone might say: "If ultimate truth is peace beyond suffering,
and endless rebirth is only everyday truth," ^9-13

"then even the Buddhas would still be part of endless rebirth—
so what would be the point of practicing the bodhisattva path?"
But as long as the flow of conditions doesn't stop,
even an illusion won't simply stop appearing either. ^9-14

But once the flow of conditions truly stops,
nothing appears anymore, even on the level of everyday truth.
And once confusion itself no longer exists,
who would there be left to perceive any illusion at all? ^9-15

If, according to you, illusion itself doesn't truly exist,
then what exactly is being perceived at that moment?
And if you say something else exists on top of that,
that something is really just the mind itself. ^9-16

But if the mind itself is like an illusion,
then who could ever perceive what, and by what?
The Protector of the World himself has said
that mind cannot truly see mind. ^9-17

Just as a sword's blade cannot cut itself,
the mind cannot see itself in the same way.
Someone might say: "But just as a lamp
truly illuminates itself," ^9-18

a lamp doesn't actually need to illuminate itself,
since it was never covered by darkness to begin with—
just as a blue crystal doesn't depend on anything else
to be blue. ^9-19

In the same way, some things are seen to depend on something else,
and some are not—
but nothing that isn't blue by nature
ever makes itself blue through its own power. ^9-20

If you say, "A lamp illuminates itself,"
and know this through some other awareness that knows it,
then, when you say, "The mind itself is naturally clear,"
what other awareness knows that, to be able to say so? ^9-21

If no one at all perceives it,
then whether it's clear or not clear,
talking about it is as pointless
as describing the graceful walk of a barren woman's daughter. ^9-22

Someone might ask: "If there's no self-aware mind,
how could we ever remember an experience?"
But memory arises through a connection to something else we experienced,
just like the memory of a mouse's poison bite arises through that same kind of connection. ^9-23

Someone might say: "The mind is naturally clear,
because it can perceive things when the right other conditions are present."
But just because applying a magic eye ointment lets someone see a hidden vase,
that doesn't mean the ointment itself becomes visible. ^9-24

I am not trying to deny that seeing, hearing, and knowing happen at all.
What I want to turn back here
is the mistaken belief that these things are truly, solidly real—
since that belief is the real cause of suffering. ^9-25

Illusion is not something separate from mind,
and yet it's not exactly the same as mind either, since mind itself isn't permanent.
If it were truly real, how could it be no different from mind?
And if it's not different, then it can't be truly real either. ^9-26

Just as an illusion isn't truly real,
the mind that perceives it isn't truly real either.
If endless rebirth depended on truly real things,
then it would become something else entirely, as empty and groundless as the sky. ^9-27

How could something unreal, depending on something real,
actually do anything at all?
If mind alone were truly real, all by itself,
with nothing to depend on, ^9-28

then, once mind is free of anything to grasp onto,
everyone would already be a Buddha.
So even accepting "mind only" as the final truth—
what real benefit would there be in that? ^9-29

Even if I understand things to be like an illusion,
how could that alone turn back disturbing emotions?
After all, even the illusionist who creates an illusory woman
can still feel desire for her. ^9-30

That's because the illusionist hasn't yet given up
the habitual patterns of disturbing emotions toward the object he's creating.
So when he looks at it, his habit of seeing emptiness
is still too weak to overcome it. ^9-31

By growing truly used to the habit of seeing emptiness,
the habit of seeing things as solidly real is given up.
And by growing used to the thought "nothing at all exists,"
even that thought, in time, is eventually let go of too. ^9-32

When, through examining something carefully,
no real thing at all can be found,
how could "nothing," which depends on that very thing to make sense of,
still stand there in front of the mind? ^9-33

When neither something nor nothing
remains standing before the mind,
since there's no other possibility left,
the mind rests completely at peace, free of anything at all to hold onto. ^9-34

Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-granting tree
fulfills every hope people bring to it,
in the same way, through the power of the prayers of those who need guidance,
the Victors appear to them in whatever form helps them most. ^9-35

For example, when a garuda ritual pillar
has been properly consecrated,
even long after the garuda spirit itself has passed away,
that pillar still keeps working to calm poison and other such harms. ^9-36

In the same way, a ritual pillar of the Victor, built in keeping with the bodhisattva path,
keeps accomplishing every good purpose,
even after the bodhisattva who established it
has already passed beyond suffering. ^9-37

Someone might ask: "How can making offerings to something without a mind
bring any real result?"
But it's taught that offerings bring the very same result,
whether the Buddha is still living or has already passed beyond suffering. ^9-38

Whether we understand this on the level of everyday truth or ultimate truth,
either way, the scriptures teach that it brings real results—
just as they teach that even honoring the Buddha as truly real
still brings real results too. ^9-39

Someone might object: "Freedom comes from seeing the truths—
what use is there in seeing emptiness?"
But the scriptures themselves say that without this very path of seeing emptiness,
there is no awakening at all. ^9-40

Someone might say: "But the Great Vehicle teachings aren't even proven to be authentic."
Then how could your own scriptures be proven authentic either?
Both are accepted as scripture by someone,
but the Great Vehicle isn't accepted by you from the very start. ^9-41

Whatever reason makes you trust your own scriptures
applies just as well to the Great Vehicle teachings.
And if something is true just because two people happen to agree on it,
then even the Vedas would have to be considered true. ^9-42

If you say, "The Great Vehicle teachings are disputed, so they can't be trusted,"
then, by that same logic, your own scriptures should be rejected too,
since they're disputed by non-Buddhists,
and even other Buddhist schools disagree with each other about their own scriptures. ^9-43

If the very root of the teachings is said to be the existence of true monks,
then even being a true monk is hard to establish.
And for those who still hold onto some solid basis in their mind,
real freedom from suffering is hard to establish too. ^9-44

If freedom comes simply from giving up disturbing emotions,
then someone should become free the very instant they let those emotions go.
But we still see the power of past actions continuing to affect people,
even after their disturbing emotions are gone. ^9-45

Someone might say: "It's certain there's no more craving
that leads to rebirth in such people."
But even craving that isn't a full-blown disturbing emotion can still exist,
the way basic confusion can still remain. ^9-46

Craving arises from feeling,
and feeling is still present in such people too.
So a mind that still has something to hold onto
can still remain, at least in some of them. ^9-47

A mind that lacks the understanding of emptiness, even once stopped,
can start up again,
just like coming out of a meditative state without perception.
This is exactly why I should meditate on emptiness. ^9-48

If you accept that whatever teaching is consistent with the sutras
was truly spoken by the Buddha,
then shouldn't you also accept
that most of the Great Vehicle teachings are consistent with your own sutras too? ^9-49

If you say that just because one part is unacceptable, the whole thing must be flawed,
then, by that same reasoning, since one part matches your own sutras,
why shouldn't the whole thing
be accepted as spoken by the Victor? ^9-50

Even great disciples like Kāśyapa
could not fully fathom certain teachings of the Buddha.
So who could really say these teachings shouldn't be accepted,
just because you yourself haven't understood them? ^9-51

Being freed from the extremes of both craving and fear,
for the sake of confused, suffering beings,
while still remaining in endless rebirth to help them—
this is the true fruit of realizing emptiness. ^9-52

So there's really no good reason
to argue against the teaching of emptiness.
This is why, without any doubt,
I should meditate on emptiness. ^9-53

Emptiness is the true remedy
for the darkness of both disturbing emotions and the obstacles to knowledge.
So if I truly want to quickly reach complete knowledge,
how could I not meditate on it? ^9-54

If real fear arises from whatever actually causes suffering,
then how could any fear arise
from emptiness,
which actually calms suffering instead? ^9-55

If some kind of solid self truly existed,
then it would make sense to be afraid of anything at all.
But since there is no such solid self anywhere,
who exactly is there left to be afraid? ^9-56

Teeth, hair, and nails are not myself.
I am not my bones, or my blood.
I am not mucus, or phlegm,
and I am not lymph, or pus, either. ^9-57

I am not fat, or sweat.
I am not my lungs, or my liver.
I am not any of my other inner organs either,
and I am not waste or urine. ^9-58

I am not flesh, or skin.
I am not body heat, or breath.
I am not any of the body's openings,
and none of my six kinds of awareness are truly myself either. ^9-59

If awareness of sound were something permanent,
it would have to perceive sound at every single moment.
But if there's nothing to be known, what exactly is being known?
And by what could we even call it "awareness" at all? ^9-60

If something with no awareness could still be called "awareness,"
then even a piece of wood would have to be called aware.
So it's certain that, without something present to be known,
there can be no such thing as awareness at all. ^9-61

If that very same awareness knows form,
then why wouldn't it also hear sound at the same time?
And if you say it can't, because sound isn't present to it,
then it wasn't really aware of sound in the first place either. ^9-62

How could something whose very nature is to perceive sound
also be the very thing that perceives form?
Something can be labeled "father" and "son" as one continuum,
but that doesn't make it truly, ultimately one and the same thing. ^9-63

For example, the qualities of courage, dust, or darkness
are not truly "son" or "father" either—
in the same way, we never actually see anything
whose very nature is to perceive sound also having the nature to perceive form. ^9-64

Like a dancer who takes on different forms,
if the same thing appears in a completely different way, it can't be truly permanent.
And if a different form is really the very same thing,
then that "one thing" never truly existed as one before. ^9-65

If you say the different forms aren't truly real,
then tell me what its own true nature actually is.
If you say its nature is simply "awareness itself,"
then, by that logic, everything that's ever been born would have to be one and the same thing. ^9-66

Even things with a mind and things without one
would have to become identical, since they'd all equally just "exist."
And if their real differences are just an illusion,
then what is it that makes them all seem so similar in the first place? ^9-67

Something without a mind can't be myself either,
since it has no mind, just like a vase.
And if you say it's myself because it has a mind,
then, once it becomes aware, its earlier unaware state would have to be destroyed completely. ^9-68

And if this self never changes at all,
then what could a thought ever really do to it?
If it truly has no awareness, and does nothing at all,
then it would end up no different from calling empty space "myself." ^9-69

Someone might object: "If there's really no self at all,
then it makes no sense to connect an action to its result—
since the doer, once the action is done,
has already changed and gone." ^9-70

But even though the doer, the deed, and the result are all different from one another,
and there's no single fixed "self" who performs it,
both sides already accept this same basic point.
So isn't this whole argument really pointless? ^9-71

It's simply not possible to actually see a cause together with its own result
as one single, unified thing.
It's only because they belong to the same continuous stream
that we speak of a "doer" and an "action" at all. ^9-72

A past or future thought cannot be my true self,
since it doesn't even exist right now.
And if you say a present thought is my true self,
then once that thought passes, that self would be gone too. ^9-73

Just as, when you break a banana tree apart piece by piece,
you find nothing solid at its core,
in the very same way, when I search carefully for a real self,
I find nothing truly solid there either. ^9-74

Someone might ask: "If beings don't truly exist,
who is there left to feel compassion for?"
But we still act with compassion toward beings,
accepted only in the way that ordinary confusion imagines them, for the sake of the good result it brings. ^9-75

It's true that if there are truly no beings,
there's no one to receive the result either—
but I still accept this out of the very same kind of helpful confusion.
So, for the sake of truly calming suffering, I won't try to turn back this helpful kind of confusion. ^9-76

The pride that is the real cause of suffering
only grows stronger through confusion about a solid self.
If someone asks, "Is there really no way to turn that back?"—
meditating on selflessness is the very best way to do it. ^9-77

The body is not the shins,
and it is not the thighs or the waist.
It is not the belly or the back,
and it is not the chest or the shoulders either. ^9-78

It is not the ribs or the arms,
and it is not the armpits or the shoulder blades.
It is not any of the inner organs either,
and it is not the head or the neck—
so, out of all of this, what exactly is "the body"? ^9-79

If this body exists partly in each and every one of these parts,
then, while each part rests within its own share,
where exactly does the body itself, as a whole,
actually rest? ^9-80

And if the whole body itself is said to rest entirely
within the hands and other such parts,
then there would have to be just as many separate bodies
as there are hands and other parts. ^9-81

If the body can't be found either outside or inside these parts,
then how could it exist within the hands and other parts themselves?
And if the body is nothing apart from the hands and other parts,
then how could it exist at all? ^9-82

So, out of confusion, we mistake the hands and other parts,
which have no real body in them, for a real body—
just as, because of a certain shape,
we mistake a pile of stones for a person. ^9-83

For as long as the right conditions come together,
a body appears to look like a real person.
In the same way, for as long as the hands and other parts
remain arranged together, a body appears to be there too. ^9-84

In the very same way, since a hand is just a collection of fingers,
what exactly is "the hand" itself?
And since a finger is just a collection of joints,
and each joint can be broken down into its own parts too, ^9-85

and those parts can be broken down further into tiny particles,
and even a particle can be divided into its different sides,
and even a side, once it has no parts left at all, is really no different from empty space—
so, in the end, not even a particle truly exists. ^9-86

So who, looking carefully, would still feel attached
to a form that is really just like something in a dream?
And once the body, examined this way, cannot be found at all,
then who is really a man, and who is really a woman? ^9-87

If suffering truly, solidly existed in something,
why wouldn't it also spoil the greatest joy?
And if pleasant taste were truly, solidly happiness,
why wouldn't it also bring joy even in the middle of grief and torment? ^9-88

Someone might say: "It's not actually experienced there,
because it's simply overwhelmed by something stronger."
But if what's felt has no real experiencer to begin with,
then how could it even be called a feeling at all? ^9-89

Someone might say: "A subtle form of suffering is still there—
hasn't only its coarser form been removed?"
Or: "What remains is really just a different, subtler kind of pleasure."
But that subtle pleasure would still belong to that very same suffering. ^9-90

And if you say that suffering simply hasn't arisen there,
because something opposing it has arisen instead,
then doesn't this just prove that the whole idea of "feeling"
is nothing more than a strong, mistaken attachment to begin with? ^9-91

This is exactly why I should meditate on this kind of careful analysis,
as the true remedy for it—
this kind of examination
is the very food of a true meditator's practice. ^9-92

If the sense faculty and its object are separated by some distance between them,
then how could they ever actually meet?
And if there's no distance between them at all, and they're simply one and the same,
then what exactly is meeting what? ^9-93

One tiny particle cannot enter into another,
since they're equal in size, with no room for one inside the other.
And what hasn't entered cannot truly mix,
and what hasn't mixed cannot truly meet. ^9-94

How could something with no parts at all
ever be said to truly "meet" anything?
If you claim to have actually seen something meet, while also having no parts,
then show me how that could possibly be. ^9-95

It makes no sense at all
for a formless consciousness to truly "meet" anything either.
And a collection of things has no real, solid existence of its own,
just as I've already shown through this same kind of careful examination. ^9-96

So, since real contact doesn't truly exist in this way,
where could feeling possibly come from?
What, then, is the point of all this hard work?
And what could possibly harm what? ^9-97

Since there's no real one who feels,
and no real feeling either,
once I truly see things this way,
why wouldn't craving simply turn back on its own? ^9-98

Seeing and touching, too, are, by their very nature,
just like something in a dream or an illusion.
And since feeling arises together with the mind at the very same moment,
that very feeling never actually "sees" anything at all. ^9-99

Even something that arises earlier or later than the mind
can only be remembered—it was never truly experienced by that mind at that moment.
It is not experienced by itself,
and it is not experienced by anything else either. ^9-100

There is no real "one who feels" anywhere at all—
so feeling itself cannot be truly, solidly real either.
So how could this collection of parts, which has no real self in it at all,
really be harmed by anything? ^9-101

The mind does not truly rest in the sense faculties,
nor in their objects like form, nor anywhere in between.
It is not found within, and it is not found outside,
and it cannot be found anywhere else either. ^9-102

Whatever is not the body, is not anything separate from it,
is not mixed with it, and cannot be found existing anywhere else at all,
is truly nothing whatsoever.
This is exactly why the true nature of every being is already peace beyond suffering. ^9-103

If awareness exists before the thing it knows,
then what could it possibly be focused on when it first arises?
And if awareness and its object arise together at the very same moment,
then, again, what could it possibly be focused on as it arises? ^9-104

And if awareness only arises after the thing it knows,
then what could it possibly have arisen from?
In this way, it turns out that the true arising of anything at all
can never really be established. ^9-105

Someone might object: "If everyday truth doesn't really hold up like this,
then how could there even be two truths at all?"
But this everyday truth is simply understood in relation to another kind of everyday truth—
otherwise, how could beings ever pass beyond suffering at all? ^9-106

This is really just a thought in someone else's mind about it,
not really "their own" everyday truth.
If it later turns out to be well established, then it exists on that level;
if not, then it simply doesn't exist as everyday truth at all. ^9-107

The one who analyzes and the thing being analyzed
both depend on each other.
All careful analysis is really only ever spoken of
based on what is commonly agreed upon in the first place. ^9-108

If the very mind doing the analyzing
is itself examined by yet another act of analysis,
then that act of analysis would also need to be examined too—
and this would go on forever, without ever reaching an end. ^9-109

But once the thing being examined has itself been properly analyzed,
there's nothing left for the analyzing mind to rest on.
And since it has nothing left to rest on, it can no longer even arise—
and this, too, is what is meant by peace beyond suffering. ^9-110

Whose view is really correct here
is genuinely very hard to establish.
If something is proven true simply because it's known by a valid mind,
then what does the existence of that knowing mind itself rest on? ^9-111

And if that knowing mind is instead proven true by the thing it knows,
then what does the existence of that known thing rest on?
And if you say they each depend on one another to exist,
then, in truth, neither one can really exist at all. ^9-112

If, without a son, someone couldn't be called a father,
then where could the son himself have come from in the first place?
Just as there's no father without a son,
in the very same way, neither one can exist without the other. ^9-113

A sprout grows from a seed,
and through that very sprout, we come to know the seed existed.
So why couldn't the same be true of a knowing mind that arises from something known—
proving that the known thing existed too? ^9-114

If we can know the seed existed through some other awareness, separate from the sprout itself,
then, by that same logic,
how could we ever know that the knowing mind itself,
which knows the known thing, truly exists? ^9-115

Ordinary people can directly see causes for themselves—
for example, the different kinds of lotus stems and such
are produced
by differences in their causes. ^9-116

If someone asks, "What makes those causes different?"—
it's simply because of differences in still earlier causes.
And why can a cause produce a result at all?
Because of the power of that still earlier cause. ^9-117

If you say that a creator god is the cause of the world,
then first tell me what exactly this creator god actually is.
If you say it's simply the elements, then fine—
but why bother arguing over a mere name? ^9-118

But the elements, like earth, are many, impermanent,
without real freedom, not divine,
something to be walked upon, and unclean—
so none of them could really be a true creator god. ^9-119

A creator god can't be empty space either,
since space has no real movement or activity.
And it can't be a truly permanent self,
since I've already refuted that idea earlier. ^9-120

If you say this creator acts without any real thought or intention at all,
then what's even the point of talking about such an inconceivable, unthinking maker?
And what exactly is it supposed to have created?
If it's the self, the elements, or its own being, ^9-121

aren't all of these already shown not to be permanent?
Doesn't a knowing mind arise dependent on the thing it knows?
And doesn't happiness and suffering, since beginningless time,
arise from past actions instead? ^9-122

So tell me, then, what exactly has this creator god ever truly created?
And if the cause itself has no real beginning,
how could its result
have a beginning either? ^9-123

If this creator doesn't depend on anything else,
then why doesn't it just keep creating constantly, all the time?
And if nothing exists that isn't already made by it,
then what exactly could it possibly depend on to create anything at all? ^9-124

And if it does depend on something, then it's really just the coming together of the right conditions
that is the true cause, not some independent creator god at all.
Once those conditions are present, it has no real choice but to arise,
and without them, it has no power to arise at all. ^9-125

And if this creator acts even against its own will,
then it must really be under the power of something else.
And if it acts because it wills to, then it's really controlled by its own wish—
so how could it be a truly independent creator at all, either way? ^9-126

Those who claim that tiny particles are permanent
have already been refuted earlier.
And the Sāṃkhya school claims
that a permanent "primal nature" is the true cause of the whole world. ^9-127

They say that when the three qualities—courage, passion, and darkness—
rest in perfect balance, that balanced state itself is called "primal nature."
And when that balance is disturbed,
the visible world comes into being. ^9-128

But it doesn't make sense for one single thing to have three different natures all at once—
so this "primal nature" cannot really exist.
And, in the same way, these three qualities themselves cannot really exist either,
since each one is, in itself, already made of three parts too. ^9-129

And if these qualities don't really exist,
then the real existence of sound and other such things becomes very hard to establish too.
And it's simply not possible for something without a mind, like a piece of clothing,
to actually contain real happiness and the other qualities within it. ^9-130

If you say that things like cloth simply share the very nature of their cause,
haven't I already shown that this kind of solid, real nature can't hold up under careful examination?
And if their cause is really just happiness and the other qualities themselves,
then something like felt cloth could never really come from that at all. ^9-131

They also say that happiness and the other qualities come from things like cloth—
but without the cloth, there could be no happiness in it either way.
And happiness and the other qualities have never actually been observed
to be truly permanent in the first place. ^9-132

If happiness and the other qualities really, clearly exist all the time,
why isn't that clear happiness always being felt?
And if you say it just becomes too subtle to notice,
then how could that very same thing be both coarse and subtle at once? ^9-133

Since its coarse form disappears and it becomes subtle instead,
this coarse and subtle happiness clearly isn't permanent at all.
So why not accept, in the very same way,
that everything else is impermanent too? ^9-134

If the coarse form of happiness is really no different from happiness itself,
then this shows that even clear, obvious happiness is impermanent.
And if you say that something that doesn't already exist
can never come into being at all, ^9-135

then, even though you don't want to accept that something clear can arise from what is unclear,
that's still exactly what you end up accepting.
And if you say the result already exists within its cause beforehand,
then eating food would really be the same as eating something unclean. ^9-136

And you should be able to buy cotton seeds with the price of cloth,
and wear them as clothing instead.
If you say ordinary confused people just can't see this,
then leave that claim to those who truly understand it. ^9-137

That kind of clear understanding exists among ordinary people too,
so why don't they see it this way as well?
And if ordinary perception isn't a valid way of knowing things,
then even their seeing of what seems clear and obvious can't be trusted as true either. ^9-138

And if ordinary perception isn't actually a valid way of knowing,
then wouldn't everything measured by it turn out to be false?
In that case, meditating on emptiness itself, using that same kind of reasoning,
would end up making no sense either. ^9-139

Without first truly examining something as if it were real,
you cannot even grasp its unreality.
So, whatever seemed real in the first place, once shown to be false,
its unreality is clearly false too, in that same sense. ^9-140

So, for example, when dreaming of a child's death,
the thought "the child no longer exists" works to stop the earlier thought "the child exists"—
but that later thought, too,
is ultimately just as false as the first. ^9-141

So, through this kind of careful analysis,
nothing at all is found to exist without a cause.
And nothing is found to truly rest in its conditions either,
whether taken separately or all together. ^9-142

Nothing truly comes from somewhere else,
nothing truly stays in place, and nothing truly goes anywhere either.
So what real difference is there between what confused minds take to be real,
and a mere illusion? ^9-143

Consider carefully: whatever is conjured up by a magician's illusion,
and whatever is produced by ordinary causes and conditions—
where does either of them really come from,
and where do they really go? ^9-144

Whatever appears only because certain conditions happen to be nearby,
and disappears once those conditions are gone,
is really just like a reflection produced by artificial means.
So how could anything like that ever be considered truly, solidly real? ^9-145

If something already truly, solidly exists,
what real need would it have for a cause at all?
And if it truly doesn't exist at all,
what real need would it have for a cause either? ^9-146

Even a hundred million causes together
could never turn something that truly doesn't exist into something real.
So how could that in-between state itself be called truly real?
And what else could it possibly turn into instead? ^9-147

If, while it doesn't yet exist, its real existence isn't even possible,
then when exactly could it ever come to truly exist?
And unless something truly real has already arisen,
its state of non-existence could never really be left behind either. ^9-148

And as long as it hasn't left behind its state of non-existence,
there's no real chance for it to exist.
And it can't simply become non-existent again either,
since that would mean it had two completely different natures at once. ^9-149

So, in truth, there is really no such thing as something truly ceasing to exist,
and no such thing as something truly, solidly existing either.
This is exactly why all these beings, in the world,
have never truly been born, and have never truly ceased, at any time at all. ^9-150

Beings are truly just like something in a dream—
when carefully examined, they're just like a banana tree, with nothing solid at its core.
And whether beyond suffering or still within it,
on the ultimate level, there's really no true difference between the two at all. ^9-151

So, when everything is truly empty like this,
what real thing is there to gain, and what real thing is there to lose?
Who is there to be truly honored by anyone,
and who is there to be truly looked down upon by anyone either? ^9-152

Where could real happiness or real suffering possibly come from?
What real thing is there to be unhappy about, and what real thing is there to be happy about at all?
And if you search for it carefully, on the ultimate level,
who is there to truly crave anything, and what is there left to crave? ^9-153

When you look at this world of the living carefully,
who is it, really, that will one day die here?
Who is it that will be born, and who has already been born?
And who, really, are our relatives and friends? ^9-154

So someone like me should learn
to hold everything as truly like empty space.
Beings who only want happiness for themselves,
driven by causes for conflict and fleeting excitement, ^9-155

end up alternating between real turmoil and fleeting joy,
facing grief, struggle, and conflict,
cutting and wounding each other,
and living through great hardship, caused by their own wrongdoing. ^9-156

They are reborn again and again in fortunate realms,
enjoying much happiness there,
but after death, they fall into lower rebirths,
into long-lasting, unbearable suffering. ^9-157

In endless rebirth, there are so many hidden dangers,
and things are never really as they seem to be.
And since even those very ideas contradict one another,
in truth, nothing in endless rebirth is ever truly what it seems. ^9-158

And beyond all this lies a boundless, unbearable ocean of suffering.
There, beings have so little strength,
and there,
their lives are so terribly short. ^9-159

Even in this short life, so much effort goes into just staying alive and healthy,
the exhaustion of hunger, the pull of sleep,
all kinds of harm, and, in the same way,
so much time wasted in the pointless company of the foolish. ^9-160

Life slips away quickly, wasted like this,
and real wisdom is so very hard to find.
And even then, is there really any way
to turn back a mind so deeply used to constant distraction? ^9-161

Even in the midst of all this,
demons work hard to make beings fall into terrible lower rebirths.
There are so many wrong paths to be led astray by,
and it's so hard to overcome doubt as well. ^9-162

A precious human life with real freedom is so hard to find again,
and it's even harder to encounter a Buddha appearing in the world.
The flood of disturbing emotions is so hard to escape—
oh, how suffering just keeps flowing on and on, one life after another. ^9-163

It's truly heartbreaking that beings caught in this river of suffering,
suffering so intensely like this,
still cannot even see
their own suffering for what it is. ^9-164

It's like someone who bathes again and again,
and then throws themselves into fire again and again—
even while living in such intense suffering,
they still imagine themselves to be truly happy. ^9-165

Living as if they will never grow old or die,
in places like these, beings first bring destruction upon themselves,
and then fall
into the unbearable suffering of a lower rebirth. ^9-166

When will I finally be able to calm this fire of suffering that torments beings,
with a truly wonderful rain of my own gathered happiness,
born
from the clouds of goodness? ^9-167

This is the end of the ninth chapter, called "The Perfection of Wisdom," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^9-a

## 10. Chapter Ten: Dedication ^10-0

Through the goodness that has come
from my writing this book, "Entering the Bodhisattva's Way,"
may every being everywhere
be inspired to enter the bodhisattva's way of life themselves. ^10-1

May everyone, everywhere, who suffers in body and mind,
however many there are,
find, through this goodness of mine,
a true ocean of happiness and joy. ^10-2

May they never lose their happiness,
for as long as they remain in endless rebirth.
May every being find the unbroken happiness
that comes from following the bodhisattva's way. ^10-3

May all the beings suffering in the hells,
wherever they are, throughout every world,
find joy instead
in the happiness of the Blissful Realm. ^10-4

May those suffering from bitter cold find warmth.
May those suffering from unbearable heat find cooling relief,
from the boundless rivers
flowing from the great clouds of the bodhisattvas. ^10-5

May the forest of sword-leaved trees
become, for them, a thick forest of sandalwood instead.
May the trees with thorns like knives
become wish-granting trees, blooming for them instead. ^10-6

May the places of hell become beautiful instead,
filled with lakes graced by the sweet calls
of geese, cranes, and swans,
and made delightful by lotus ponds, rich with lovely fragrance. ^10-7

May those heaps of burning coal
become heaps of jewels instead. May the burning ground
become a floor of crystal. May the crushing mountains of hell
become beautiful temples of offering, filled with Sugatas instead. ^10-8

May the rain of burning coals, hot stones, and weapons,
from this moment on, become a gentle rain of flowers instead.
And may the striking of weapons against each other,
from this moment on, become a playful game of throwing flowers. ^10-9

May those sinking in the river of scalding ashes, their flesh already gone,
their bones as white as jasmine flowers,
gain, through the power of my goodness, the body of a god,
and rest at ease in that gentle river, together with goddesses. ^10-10

May those frightened by the terrifying servants of death, and by crows and vultures there,
wonder in amazement, "Whose wonderful power is this, clearing away all this darkness and bringing such joy?"—
and, looking up, may they see blazing Vajrapāṇi in the sky above,
and, filled with real joy, become completely free of wrongdoing, and join him there. ^10-11

May the beings in hell see a rain of flowers, mixed with scented water,
falling down and putting out the burning coals of hell with a hissing sound,
and, wondering, "What is this sudden happiness that fills me now?"—
may they then see Padmapāṇi there too. ^10-12

"Friends, let go of your fear, and come quickly over here to me!"—
may they hear this call, and see approaching the youthful one with the topknot, Mañjuśrī, blazing bright,
whose power frees beings from every suffering, filling them with joy, protecting all beings everywhere,
born from bodhicitta and true loving-kindness, bringing complete fearlessness wherever he goes. ^10-13

"Look! Here he is, honored by the crowns of a hundred gods bowing at his lotus feet,
his eyes moist with compassion, showers of countless flowers raining down on his head,
surrounded by lovely towers and a thousand goddesses singing his praise!"—
may the beings in hell, seeing Mañjughoṣa like this, cry out with joy. ^10-14

So, through this goodness of mine, may the beings in hell see the bodhisattvas,
like Samantabhadra and others, appear as clouds of happiness, unobstructed,
sending down cool, sweetly scented rain,
and may they feel real joy because of it. ^10-15

May animals be freed
from the fear of being eaten by one another.
May hungry ghosts find happiness,
just as beings in the Northern Continent do. ^10-16

May the noble Avalokiteśvara's hands
pour down a stream of milk, that satisfies the hungry ghosts completely,
and may they always be washed clean,
and cooled by it. ^10-17

May the blind be able to see,
and may the deaf always be able to hear sound.
May pregnant women, just like the goddess Māyā,
give birth without any harm at all. ^10-18

May the naked find clothing,
and may the hungry find food.
May the thirsty find water,
and other delicious things to drink. ^10-19

May the poor find wealth,
and may those weakened by grief find joy.
May the discouraged find fresh hope,
and may they gain real strength and confidence too. ^10-20

May all beings who are sick, however many there are,
quickly become free from their sickness.
May every illness that could ever affect any being
simply never arise at all. ^10-21

May the frightened become fearless,
and may the imprisoned be set free.
May the powerless gain real strength,
and may everyone's hearts grow kind toward one another. ^10-22

May every road be safe and pleasant,
for every traveler, in every direction.
And may whatever purpose they are traveling for
be accomplished, without any real struggle at all. ^10-23

May those who set out on boats and ships
have their wishes fulfilled,
and, arriving safely at the shore,
find joy together with their loved ones. ^10-24

May those wandering lost, sorrowful, on unfamiliar roads,
meet with real companions,
and travel safely and easily, without exhaustion,
free of fear from thieves, tigers, and other dangers. ^10-25

May the gods protect children, the elderly, and those without any protector,
wandering in dangerous, trackless wilderness,
as well as those who are asleep,
or lost in confusion. ^10-26

May they be free from every kind of hardship,
and have real faith, wisdom, and loving-kindness.
May they have every good thing they need, in food and conduct,
and may they always remember their past lives clearly. ^10-27

May everyone have everything they need,
as endless as a treasury as vast as the sky itself,
and may they live freely,
without conflict, and without harming one another. ^10-28

May beings with little presence or confidence
gain real presence and confidence instead.
May those who practice hardship, even with an unattractive appearance,
gain a beautiful, radiant form. ^10-29

May however many women there are in the world,
if they wish, be reborn as men.
May those of lowly status gain real standing,
and may pride and arrogance be overcome everywhere. ^10-30

Through this goodness of mine,
may every single being, without exception,
give up every kind of wrongdoing,
and always do what is good instead. ^10-31

May they never be separated from bodhicitta,
and may they stay devoted to the bodhisattva's way.
May they always be cared for by the Buddhas,
and may they give up every kind of harmful action. ^10-32

May every one of these beings
have an immeasurably long life,
and may they always live happily,
without ever even hearing the word "death." ^10-33

May every direction be filled with gardens of wish-granting trees,
filled with Buddhas and their children,
teaching
the sweet sound of the true teachings. ^10-34

May the ground everywhere be completely free
of rocks and thorns,
smooth as the palm of a hand,
and soft by nature, like beryl itself. ^10-35

May many bodhisattvas remain
in every gathering, wherever they may be,
beautifying the whole earth
with their own goodness. ^10-36

May every being hear the sound of the true teachings without interruption,
from birds, from trees,
from every ray of light,
and even from the open sky itself. ^10-37

May they always meet with the Buddhas,
and with their bodhisattva children,
and may they honor these true guides of beings,
with boundless clouds of offerings. ^10-38

May the gods send down rain at just the right time,
and may every crop grow rich and full.
May rulers govern with true fairness,
and may the whole world flourish. ^10-39

May medicines be truly effective,
and may sacred prayers recited bring real results.
May spirits and demons everywhere
be filled with real compassion. ^10-40

May no being ever suffer, or feel fear, or be looked down upon by anyone.
And may no being
ever feel unhappy at heart, either. ^10-41

May temples be filled
with the beautiful sound of reading and recitation.
May the community of practitioners always live in harmony,
and may their true purpose be accomplished. ^10-42

May monks who wish to train find real solitude,
and, letting go of every distraction,
may their minds become truly workable
in meditation. ^10-43

May nuns have what they need,
and be free from conflict and harm.
And, in the same way,
may every ordained person's discipline remain unbroken. ^10-44

May those whose discipline has weakened feel real remorse,
and always work to clear away their wrongdoing.
May they gain a good rebirth,
and may their discipline remain unbroken there too. ^10-45

May the learned be honored,
and may they always receive the alms they need.
May their minds be completely pure,
and may their good reputation spread in every direction. ^10-46

May they never experience the suffering of the lower realms,
and, without needing to undergo any real hardship,
may they quickly reach Buddhahood,
with a body even greater than that of the gods. ^10-47

May every being make offerings to all the Buddhas,
again and again,
and may they always enjoy
the inconceivable happiness of Buddhahood itself. ^10-48

May the bodhisattvas accomplish
whatever they have in mind for the good of beings.
And may whatever the Protectors themselves intend for beings
come to pass. ^10-49

And, in the same way, may the solitary realizers
and the hearers find happiness too. ^10-50

And, through the kindness of Mañjughoṣa, until I myself reach
the first bodhisattva level, Perfect Joy,
may I always remember my past lives,
and may I always find the chance to live as an ordained practitioner. ^10-51

May I sustain myself with even the simplest food,
and always have real strength for practice.
And in every lifetime,
may I have everything I need to live in true solitude. ^10-52

And whenever I wish to see him,
or ask him even a small question,
may I always be able to see the Protector Mañjughoṣa himself,
without any obstacle at all. ^10-53

Just as Mañjughoṣa acts to accomplish the good of every being,
reaching to the very edges of space in all ten directions,
may my own way of acting
become just like his. ^10-54

For as long as space endures,
and for as long as beings remain,
may I too remain,
to clear away the suffering of every being. ^10-55

Whatever suffering there is for beings,
may all of it ripen upon me instead.
And, through the community of bodhisattvas,
may every being come to enjoy true happiness. ^10-56

May the true teachings, the one true medicine for the suffering of beings,
and the source of every kind of happiness,
be found, be honored,
and remain in this world for a very long time. ^10-57

I bow down to Mañjughoṣa, through whose kindness
this good thought first arose in me.
And I bow down as well to my spiritual friend,
through whose kindness I have been able to grow and flourish. ^10-58

This is the end of the tenth chapter, called "Dedication," from the Bodhisattva's Way. ^10-a

Later, the Indian scholar Dharmaśrībhadra, together with the chief editor-translators Bandé Rinchen Zangpo and Śākya Lodrö, revised and retranslated it to match manuscripts and commentaries from Central India, and finalized it. ^b-2

Still later, the Indian scholar Sumatikīrti and the chief editor-translator, the monk Loden Sherab, corrected and retranslated it, and finalized it carefully. ^b-3
