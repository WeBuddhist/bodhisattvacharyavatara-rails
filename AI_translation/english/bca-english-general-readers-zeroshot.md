# Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva ^0

## 0. Prologue ^I-0

In the language of India: *Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra*. ^I-1

In the language of Tibet: *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^I-2

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas. ^I-3

## 1. Chapter 1: The Benefits of the Awakening Mind ^1-0

I bow with respect to the buddhas — those who have gone to bliss and embody truth itself — together with their spiritual heirs, and to all who deserve reverence. Following the scriptures, I will now briefly explain how to take up the way of life of those who follow in the buddhas' footsteps. ^1-1

There is nothing here that has not been said before, and I have no skill in composition. For this reason I do not intend this work to help others; I have written it simply to acquaint my own mind with these teachings. ^1-2

And yet, through writing it, my own inclination toward what is good will grow for a while. And should another person, whose fortune matches mine, happen to see this, then it will have served some purpose after all. ^1-3

This state of freedom and opportunity we now have is extremely hard to find. Having gained it, capable of accomplishing what truly matters, if we fail to make good use of it now, how could such a chance ever come again? ^1-4

Just as a flash of lightning momentarily lights up the dark clouds of a night sky, so too, through the power of the buddhas, once in a great while a thought of goodness or wisdom flickers briefly in the minds of beings in this world. ^1-5

Thus, what is good is always feeble, while wrongdoing is powerful and utterly terrible. If not for the mind set on complete awakening — the intention to reach enlightenment for the sake of all beings — what other kind of goodness could overpower it? ^1-6

The buddhas, who have contemplated this deeply through countless ages, have seen that this awakening mind alone is what truly helps. Through it, boundless multitudes of beings will easily attain the highest happiness. ^1-7

Whoever wishes to overcome the hundreds of sufferings of conditioned existence, wishes to dispel the unhappiness of living beings, and wishes to enjoy hundreds of kinds of happiness, should never abandon this awakening mind. ^1-8

The moment this awakening mind arises in someone still bound in the prison of cyclic existence and weighed down by suffering, that person comes to be called a child of the buddhas, worthy of homage from the world, including gods and humans alike. ^1-9

Like the finest elixir that transforms base metal into gold, this awakening mind takes hold of our impure body and transforms it into the priceless form of a buddha's body. So hold firmly to this mind called bodhicitta, the wish for awakening. ^1-10

The one immeasurable wisdom that alone guides all beings has carefully examined this and found it most precious. Therefore, all who wish to be free from the states of suffering that beings wander through should hold firmly to this precious awakening mind. ^1-11

Every other kind of goodness is like a banana tree: having produced its fruit once, it withers away. But the tree of the awakening mind bears fruit constantly, without ever being exhausted, and only continues to grow. ^1-12

Even someone who has committed the most terrible wrongs can, by relying on a person of great strength, swiftly be freed from a great danger — so why wouldn't those who are careful about consequences rely on this mind as well? ^1-13

Like the fire at the end of an age, it consumes even the greatest wrongs in a single instant. Its benefits are beyond measure, as the wise and loving guide (Maitreya) explained to the excellent youth Sudhana. ^1-14

To summarize, this awakening mind should be understood as twofold: the aspiration to awaken, and the actual engagement in the path to awakening. ^1-15

Just as one understands the difference between wishing to travel somewhere and actually setting out on the journey, so too the wise should understand, in the same way, the difference between these two aspects of the awakening mind. ^1-16

Although the mind that merely aspires to awakening brings great results while one still wanders in cyclic existence, it does not generate a continuous stream of merit in the way that the mind actively engaged in the path does. ^1-17

But from the moment someone genuinely takes up this mind — with an unwavering resolve to liberate the boundless realm of living beings from suffering — ^1-18

from that time onward, even while asleep or otherwise inattentive, an unbroken stream of merit, vast as the sky itself, continually arises. ^1-19

This the buddha himself explained, with sound reasoning, in response to the question posed by Subāhu, for the sake of beings of modest aspiration. ^1-20

Even someone who merely thinks, "I will relieve the mere headaches of living beings," having such a beneficial intention, gains immeasurable merit. ^1-21

How much more so, then, for one who wishes to dispel the boundless suffering of each and every living being, and wishes to bring about immeasurable good qualities in each one of them! ^1-22

Does anyone — whether father or mother — have such a benevolent intention as this? Do even the gods, the sages, or the great god Brahmā have it? ^1-23

If those very living beings have never, even in a dream, had such a thought for their own sake, how could they ever give rise to it for the sake of others? ^1-24

That others should generate such a mind directed toward the welfare of beings — a thing not arisen even for their own sake — this is an extraordinary and unprecedented marvel, an exceptional jewel among states of mind. ^1-25

This precious mind is the source of joy for all beings and the very cure for their suffering — how could the merit of such a precious mind ever be measured? ^1-26

If merely intending to benefit others is more excellent than making offerings to the buddhas, how much more so, then, is diligent effort for the welfare and happiness of every single living being without exception! ^1-27

Beings wish to escape suffering, yet they rush straight toward its very causes. And though they wish for happiness, out of confusion they destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy. ^1-28

But for one who satisfies with every kind of happiness those who are destitute of happiness and burdened with much suffering, cutting through all their suffering ^1-29

and clearing away their confusion as well — what could ever equal such goodness? Where could such a friend be found? Where could such merit be found? ^1-30

If even repaying a kindness done for us deserves some praise, how much more so, then, does the bodhisattva deserve praise, who does good without even being asked! ^1-31

People honor as virtuous someone who gives a meal to a few beings just once, feeding them for only a brief moment with token, contemptuous fare that sustains them for half a day. ^1-32

How much more so, then, is there any need to speak of one who continually grants, to the boundless number of living beings across a long span of time, the fulfillment of every wish, up to and including the unsurpassed bliss of those who have gone to perfect peace! ^1-33

The teacher himself has said that whoever generates ill will toward such a benefactor — a child of the buddhas who gives in this way — will dwell in the hells for as many ages as there were moments of that malicious thought. ^1-34

But whoever generates a mind of genuine faith and joy toward such a person will produce a result that grows even more, surpassing the harm. Even in the face of great difficulty, no wrongdoing arises in the children of the buddhas; their goodness naturally increases of its own accord. ^1-35

To whomever this supreme and precious mind has arisen, to that person's very body I pay homage; and I go for refuge to that wellspring of happiness who, even when harmed, responds with kindness. ^1-36

This concludes the first chapter, "The Benefits of the Awakening Mind," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^1-a

## 2. Chapter 2: Confession of Wrongdoing ^2-0

In order to hold onto this precious mind of awakening,
I make excellent offerings to the buddhas who have gone to suchness,
to the sacred teaching, that stainless jewel,
and to the buddhas' heirs, oceans of good qualities. ^2-1

Whatever flowers and fruits there are,
whatever kinds of healing medicines exist,
whatever precious things the world contains,
whatever clean and pleasing waters there are, ^2-2

jeweled mountains too, and likewise
forests and secluded places that delight the mind,
trees adorned and covered with blossoms,
and branches bowed with beautiful fruit; ^2-3

in the realms of gods and others, fragrances,
incense, wish-fulfilling trees, and jeweled trees,
harvests that grow without being planted,
and any other ornaments fit to be offered — ^2-4

lakes and pools adorned with lotuses,
graced by wild geese whose calls are so sweet and pleasing,
all things unclaimed by anyone,
stretching to the very edges of boundless space — ^2-5

holding all of these in my mind,
I offer them beautifully to the Sage, supreme among beings,
and to his heirs. May these worthy recipients,
so full of compassion, look on me with kindness and accept them. ^2-6

I am destitute, without merit, with nothing else
of value to offer.
So may the protectors, who care only for others' good,
by their own power accept these for my sake. ^2-7

To the victorious ones and their heirs
I offer my entire body, forever.
Please accept me completely, supreme beings —
I will devotedly become your servant. ^2-8

Since you have taken me fully into your care,
I will act without fear in this existence, for the benefit of beings.
I will thoroughly transcend my past wrongdoing
and from now on commit no further wrong. ^2-9

In a bathing chamber sweetly fragrant,
with a floor of crystal, radiant and gleaming,
with beautiful pillars ablaze with jewels,
and a canopy shimmering with pearls — ^2-10

there I bathe the buddhas gone to suchness and their heirs,
pouring water scented with incense
from many precious vessels, filling them beautifully,
to the accompaniment of many songs and musical performances. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with cloths unmatched,
clean and deeply scented,
and then offer them fine garments
of excellent color, supremely fragrant. ^2-12

With soft, fine garments of many kinds,
and hundreds of the finest ornaments,
I adorn the noble Samantabhadra, Mañjughoṣa,
Avalokiteśvara — the Lord of the World — and others. ^2-13

With the finest perfumes, whose fragrance
spreads through a billion worlds,
I anoint the bodies of all the mighty sages,
gleaming like pure gold refined and polished. ^2-14

To the mighty sages, supreme objects of offering,
I make offerings of beautiful flowers —
mandārava, lotus, blue utpala,
and all fragrant blossoms, arranged into lovely, well-made garlands. ^2-15

I offer them clouds of incense too,
supreme scents that captivate the mind, spreading everywhere,
and I offer them feasts of food,
various foods and drinks fit for the gods. ^2-16

I offer rows of golden lotuses
set out as precious lamps.
On ground anointed with fragrant perfume
I scatter petals of beautiful flowers. ^2-17

Immeasurable palaces filled with pleasing songs of praise,
adorned with hanging pearls and jewels, radiant and beautiful,
and boundless ornaments filling the sky —
all these I offer to those whose nature is compassion. ^2-18

Beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles,
their rims adorned in lovely patterns,
upright, of fine shape, delightful to see —
these I constantly offer to the mighty sages. ^2-19

And beyond these, may every other array of offerings —
clouds of sweet, pleasing music,
clouds that soothe the suffering of all beings —
remain forever, each in its place. ^2-20

Upon the sacred teaching, all the precious ones,
the stupas, and the sacred images,
may showers of precious flowers and the like
fall in an unceasing stream. ^2-21

Just as Mañjughoṣa and others
make offerings to the victorious buddhas,
so too do I make offerings
to the buddhas gone to suchness, the protectors, and their heirs. ^2-22

I praise these oceans of good qualities
with oceans of praises, verse by verse.
May clouds of sweet praise
rise up before them without fail. ^2-23

To all the buddhas of the three times,
to the teaching, and to the supreme community,
I bow down my body as many times
as there are atoms in the universe, and pay homage. ^2-24

I pay homage to all the foundations of awakening mind
and to the stupas.
I pay homage as well to abbots and teachers
and to those of supreme discipline. ^2-25

Until I reach the very heart of awakening,
I go for refuge to the buddhas.
Likewise I go for refuge
to the teaching and to the community of bodhisattvas. ^2-26

To the perfect buddhas dwelling
in every direction, and to the bodhisattvas
who possess great compassion,
I join my palms together and make this plea: ^2-27

Throughout beginningless cycles of existence,
in this life and in others,
whatever wrong I have done unknowingly,
or caused others to do, ^2-28

or whatever I have taken delight in
while overcome by the confusion of ignorance —
seeing these faults as they are,
I confess them from the depths of my heart to the protectors. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have done through my afflictions,
by body, speech, or mind,
to the Three Jewels,
to my parents, or to my teachers and others, ^2-30

whatever wrong I, so full of faults,
have committed through my many failings —
this utterly unbearable wrongdoing,
I confess it all before the guides who lead beings to freedom. ^2-31

Before my wrongs are purified,
I may well die.
Please protect me swiftly and surely,
so that I may be freed from this for certain. ^2-32

This Lord of Death cannot be trusted,
he does not wait for tasks done or undone.
Whether I am healthy or sick, none of us
can trust that this fleeting life will last. ^2-33

I must leave everything behind and depart,
but not understanding this,
I have committed all kinds of wrongdoing
for the sake of loved ones and enemies alike. ^2-34

My enemies will cease to be,
and my loved ones too will cease to be.
I myself will cease to be as well,
and in the same way everything will cease to be. ^2-35

Just as things experienced in a dream
become only a memory of what was once experienced,
whatever I have done and enjoyed
will likewise pass and never be seen again. ^2-36

Even in this present life alone,
many loved ones and enemies have already passed away,
yet the unbearable wrongdoing I did for their sake
still stands before me, waiting. ^2-37

Because I failed to realize
that I myself am so fleeting,
I have committed many kinds of wrongdoing
driven by confusion, attachment, and hatred. ^2-38

Never resting, day or night,
this life is constantly slipping away,
and nothing extra ever comes to replace it —
so how could someone like me not die? ^2-39

Even lying on my own bed,
surrounded by all my family and friends,
I alone will be the one to feel
the sensation of my life force being cut off. ^2-40

When seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death,
what use are relatives, what use are friends?
At that time only merit can protect me,
and yet I have never relied on it. ^2-41

Careless as I am, O protectors,
not having recognized a danger such as this,
I have accumulated much wrongdoing
for the sake of this impermanent life. ^2-42

Someone being led today to a place of execution
grows terrified;
their mouth goes dry, their eyes grow wretched,
and their whole appearance changes from before. ^2-43

How much more so when one is seized
by the terrifying, ghastly messengers of death itself,
and struck by a great and terrible sickness —
utterly wretched, there is no need to say more. ^2-44

"Who will protect me
properly from this great terror?"
With eyes wide and starting from fear,
one searches in every direction for refuge. ^2-45

Seeing no refuge anywhere in the four directions,
one then sinks into utter despair.
If there is no refuge to be found in that place,
what then can I possibly do? ^2-46

Therefore, from this very day,
I go for refuge to the victorious ones, protectors of the world,
who strive with great power to protect all beings
and who dispel every fear. ^2-47

Likewise I go for true and proper refuge
to the teaching realized in their minds,
which dispels the terrors of cyclic existence,
and to the assembly of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Trembling with fear,
I offer myself to Samantabhadra.
To Mañjughoṣa as well
I offer my own body. ^2-49

To the protector Avalokiteśvara,
whose compassionate conduct never falters,
I cry out in anguish and despair,
"Please protect me, one so burdened with wrongdoing." ^2-50

Seeking refuge, I cry out from my heart
to the noble Ākāśagarbha and Kṣitigarbha,
and to all the protectors of great compassion. ^2-51

I go for refuge to Vajrapāṇi,
at the sight of whom the messengers of the Lord of Death
and other hostile forces
flee in terror in every direction. ^2-52

In the past I transgressed your word,
but now, seeing this great terror,
I go for refuge to you;
please swiftly dispel this fear. ^2-53

Even when threatened by an ordinary illness,
one must follow the doctor's instructions;
how much more so when constantly afflicted
by the disease of desire and a hundred other faults. ^2-54

If this single disease alone
destroys all the people living in this world,
and no other medicine to cure it
can be found anywhere in any direction, ^2-55

then to ignore the instructions of the physician
who knows all remedies and removes every affliction —
the one who knows how to cure every pain —
is the very depth of foolishness, deserving of blame. ^2-56

If one must remain careful
even near an ordinary, small pit,
how much more so regarding the pit
that plunges down for a thousand leagues and lasts so long. ^2-57

It is not right to relax comfortably,
thinking, "I surely will not die today."
The time when I will cease to exist
will inevitably come. ^2-58

Who will grant me freedom from fear?
How can I be certain of release from this?
If it is inevitable that I will cease to exist,
how can my mind possibly be at ease? ^2-59

Of all that I once experienced and that has now vanished,
what is left for me now?
Yet clinging to it as if it still mattered,
I have acted against the instructions of my teachers. ^2-60

Since I must leave behind, in this life and in others,
all my relatives and friends,
and go on alone to wherever fate leads me,
what use is it to distinguish loved ones from enemies? ^2-61

Suffering arises from what is not virtuous.
Thinking, "How can I be freed from this for certain?"
this alone is what I should be thinking of,
constantly, day and night. ^2-62

Whatever wrongdoing I have committed,
out of ignorance and confusion,
whether it is wrong by its very nature
or wrong because it was proscribed, ^2-63

all of this, in the presence of the protectors,
with palms joined together, trembling with fear of suffering,
I confess completely,
bowing down again and again. ^2-64

I ask the guides to accept
my wrongs and faults as they are.
Since these are not good deeds,
I will never do them again from now on. ^2-65

This concludes the second chapter, on the confession of wrongdoing, from *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^2-a

## 3. Chapter 3: Fully Accepting the Awakening Mind ^3-0

I rejoice with delight in the good deeds that give all suffering beings a rest from the pain of the lower realms, and in the happiness of those who suffer. ^3-1

I rejoice in the good actions that become the cause of awakening. I rejoice in the sure freedom of living beings from the suffering of endless rebirth. I rejoice, too, in the awakening of the protectors and in the grounds reached by their heirs, the bodhisattvas. ^3-2

I rejoice with delight in the vast ocean of good intentions to bring happiness to all beings, and in the deeds that actually help them. ^3-3

With palms joined, I ask the buddhas of every direction: for beings lost in the darkness of suffering, please light the lamp of the teaching. ^3-4

With palms joined, I ask the victorious ones who wish to pass into final peace: please do not leave this world blind, but remain for countless ages. ^3-5

Having done all this, whatever goodness I have gathered — by it, may I clear away all the suffering of every being. ^3-6

For as long as beings are sick, until their illness is cured, may I be their medicine, their doctor, and their nurse who tends them. ^3-7

May I fall as a rain of food and drink to clear away the suffering of hunger and thirst; and during the ages of famine, may I myself become food and drink. ^3-8

For beings who are poor and destitute, may I become an inexhaustible treasure, always at hand before them, supplying every kind of thing they need. ^3-9

My body, my possessions, and all the goodness I gather in the past, present, and future — I give away without holding anything back, so that the aims of all beings may be fulfilled. ^3-10

Final peace comes through giving up everything; my mind seeks that final peace. And since everything must be given up in any case, it is best to give it all to living beings. ^3-11

I have already given this body over for the use of all beings, to be treated however they wish — whether they constantly kill it, abuse it, or beat it. ^3-12

Even if others play with my body, mock it, or make it an object of ridicule, since I have already given it away, why should I care what becomes of it? ^3-13

Let them do to me whatever will not harm them. May no one who has anything to do with me ever fail to benefit from it. ^3-14

Whether those who encounter me feel anger or faith because of it, may that very encounter always become a cause for accomplishing everything they need. ^3-15

Whoever slanders me, whoever else harms me, and whoever mocks and insults me — may they all have the fortune to reach awakening. ^3-16

May I be a protector for those without protection, a guide for travelers on the road, and a boat, a ship, and a bridge for those who wish to cross over. ^3-17

May I be an island for those seeking land, a lamp for those who need light, a bed for those who need rest, and a servant for all beings who need one. ^3-18

May I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, a magic vase, a powerful healing spell, a great medicine, a wish-granting tree, and a cow of abundance for all living beings. ^3-19

Just as the earth and the other great elements, and space itself, serve as a foundation and support for the many needs of countless beings, may I too, in the same way, always be their support. ^3-20

And so, for all beings extending to the very ends of space, may I be a source of their sustenance in every way, until every one of them passes beyond suffering. ^3-21

Just as the buddhas of the past generated the awakening mind, and just as they progressed step by step through the trainings of the bodhisattvas, ^3-22

in the same way, for the benefit of all beings, I too will generate the awakening mind, and I too will train, step by step, in these same trainings. ^3-23

Now that a wise person has taken up the awakening mind in this way with genuine clarity, in order to make it grow ever further, they should praise it like this: ^3-24

Today my life has borne fruit. Having well obtained this human existence, I am born today into the family of the buddhas; now I have become a child of the buddhas. ^3-25

From now on, I must act in every way that fits this family, so that I never bring stain or fault to this noble and flawless lineage. ^3-26

Like a blind man who finds a priceless jewel in a heap of rubbish, so, by some rare chance, this awakening mind has arisen in me. ^3-27

This is the supreme nectar that overcomes death, the destroyer that ends the world's mortality. It is also the inexhaustible treasure that removes the poverty of all beings. ^3-28

It is the finest medicine that heals the sickness of the world. It is the tree giving rest and shade to beings exhausted from endless wandering on the road of existence. ^3-29

It is the universal bridge that carries all beings across, out of the lower realms. It is the rising moon of the mind, which cools the torment of disturbing emotions in beings. ^3-30

It is the great sun that clears away the darkness of beings' ignorance. It is the pure essence of butter churned from the milk of the genuine teaching. ^3-31

For travelers journeying the road of existence who wish to feast on the enjoyment of happiness, this awakening mind stands close by as their supreme host of joy, satisfying these great guests, all beings. ^3-32

Today, in the presence of all the protectors, I invite all beings to be guests, first to temporary happiness and, in the end, to the happiness of full awakening itself. Let gods, demigods, and all others rejoice! ^3-33

## 4. Chapter 4: Conscientiousness ^4-0

Having firmly taken hold of the awakening mind in this way,
a child of the victors should from then on
never grow careless, but always
make an effort not to stray from the trainings. ^4-1

Whatever is undertaken rashly,
or without careful examination beforehand,
even if one has made a commitment to it,
it may be right to reconsider whether to do it or abandon it. ^4-2

But what the buddhas and their heirs
have examined with their vast wisdom,
and what I myself have also examined again and again—
why would I ever turn back from that? ^4-3

If, having made such a commitment,
I fail to carry it out in action,
then I will have deceived all those beings—
what will become of me, where will I end up? ^4-4

It is taught that even someone who merely
thinks of giving away some ordinary trifle,
but then fails to give it,
becomes for that reason a hungry ghost. ^4-5

So if I sincerely and wholeheartedly invite
all beings to unsurpassed happiness,
and then betray them all instead,
how could I possibly go to a good rebirth? ^4-6

The way that even people who let go of the awakening mind
are nonetheless set free—
this workings of karma are beyond ordinary comprehension;
only an all-knowing buddha can fully understand it. ^4-7

For a bodhisattva, this
is the heaviest of all downfalls,
for if it happens,
it harms the welfare of all beings. ^4-8

Whoever obstructs, even for a single instant,
this bodhisattva's accumulation of merit,
because it harms the welfare of beings,
that person's suffering in bad rebirths will be without end. ^4-9

If destroying the happiness of even one being
brings about my own ruin,
then how much more so for destroying the happiness
of the boundless embodied beings filling all of space? ^4-10

Thus when someone with the power of past downfalls
and someone with the power of the awakening mind
alternately meet within cyclic existence,
reaching the bodhisattva grounds is greatly delayed. ^4-11

Therefore, exactly as I have vowed,
I must carry it out with respect and dedication;
if from now on I do not make the effort,
I will fall from one lower state to another still lower. ^4-12

Countless buddhas who benefit all beings
have already passed beyond,
yet through my own faults
I did not become someone they could heal and care for. ^4-13

If I continue to act just as before,
then again and again in just the same way
I will experience sickness, being bound,
being cut apart, and other sufferings in bad rebirths. ^4-14

The arising of a buddha in the world,
having faith, obtaining a human life,
and being fit to practice virtue—
such things are rare; when will I ever find them again? ^4-15

Even on a day like today, free from illness,
with food enough and no harm befalling me,
life is deceptive and passes in an instant,
and this body is like something borrowed for just a while. ^4-16

Given the way I am behaving,
I will not obtain a human body again;
and if I fail to obtain a human body,
there is only wrongdoing, no virtue at all. ^4-17

If, when I have the fortune to practice virtue,
I still fail to act virtuously,
then when I am utterly confused by the suffering of the lower realms,
what will I be able to do then? ^4-18

If I do not perform virtuous deeds
and instead pile up wrongdoing,
then even after a hundred million eons
I will not so much as hear the words "a good rebirth." ^4-19

This is exactly why the Blessed One said
that obtaining a human existence at all
is as extremely rare as a blind turtle
happening to put its neck through a yoke's hole floating on a vast ocean. ^4-20

If wrongdoing committed in a single instant
causes one to remain in the Avīci hell for an entire eon,
then what need is there to mention
that wrongdoing accumulated since beginningless time in cyclic existence
will not lead to a good rebirth? ^4-21

And having merely experienced the ripening of that suffering,
one is still not liberated from it,
for even while experiencing it in that very state,
further wrongdoing continues to arise. ^4-22

Having found such freedom and fortune as this,
if I still do not accustom myself to virtue,
there is no greater deception than this,
and no greater folly than this. ^4-23

If, having understood this,
I still lazily fall back into confusion out of delusion,
then when the time of death arrives,
overwhelming grief will surely arise in me. ^4-24

If my body burns for a long time
in the unbearable fires of hell,
then unbearable regret, like a blazing fire,
will certainly torment my mind. ^4-25

Having by some rare chance obtained
this basis so hard to find and so beneficial,
if I, even while possessing understanding,
am led back again into that very hell, ^4-26

it is as if I were rendered senseless here,
as though stupefied by some spell;
I do not even know what has confused me—
what is it, then, that dwells within me? ^4-27

Enemies such as hatred and craving
have no limbs such as hands or feet,
they are neither brave nor clever—
so how is it that they enslave me like this? ^4-28

While they dwell within my own mind,
delighting in causing me harm,
I do not even get angry at that—
this is a shameful, misplaced patience, patience where none is due. ^4-29

Even if all the gods and demigods
together rose up against me as enemies,
they would still be unable to lead me
into the fire of the deepest hell, the Avīci. ^4-30

But this powerful enemy, the mental afflictions,
can in a single instant hurl me into a place
where even Mount Meru itself
would be reduced to ash without a trace. ^4-31

No other enemy could persist for so long,
enduring without beginning or end,
the way my enemy, the mental afflictions,
endures within me. ^4-32

If treated well and served with kindness,
everyone else will bring me benefit and happiness;
but the mental afflictions, if I rely on and indulge them,
will only bring me suffering and harm again and again. ^4-33

Since this enemy has thus become
a constant, long-standing foe, the single cause
that increases every kind of harm—
if it remains lodged firmly in my heart,
how could I ever be joyful and unafraid within cyclic existence? ^4-34

If I remain caught in the net of attachment of mind,
tended by the guardians of this prison of cyclic existence—
these very afflictions that act as executioners
putting beings to death in hell and elsewhere—
how could there be any happiness for me at all? ^4-35

So, as long as I have not vanquished this enemy
face to face and with certainty,
I will not give up my effort here.
Even those who are simply proud and quick to anger
at some minor offense do not rest
until they have overcome that offender— ^4-36

so how much more should I, when facing in battle
these afflictions, whose very nature is to bring suffering through death,
resolve to crush them relentlessly;
even if wounded by arrows, spears, and weapons,
I would not turn back or flee without achieving my purpose. ^4-37

For me, who am now resolved to utterly destroy
these afflictions—by nature my enemies,
the constant cause of all suffering—
even should a hundred sufferings arise from this effort,
why would I ever grow discouraged or lose heart? ^4-38

If people wear even pointless wounds inflicted by ordinary enemies
as though they were ornaments on the body,
then why should the suffering that comes
from striving with genuine purpose toward a great goal
ever cause me harm? ^4-39

Fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and the like,
thinking only of their own bare livelihood,
bear the hardships of cold, heat, and so on—
so why, for the sake of the happiness of all beings, would I not bear the same? ^4-40

Having vowed to free all beings
throughout the ten directions to the very edge of space
from their mental afflictions,
while I myself am not yet free
from my own afflictions— ^4-41

not even knowing my own capacity,
to speak as I do—was that not madness?
Therefore I must never turn back
from destroying the mental afflictions, ever. ^4-42

I will hold fast to this task,
and, bearing a grudge, go to war—
though this kind of mental affliction
would itself be one that destroys other afflictions, and so is permitted. ^4-43

Let me be burned, let me be killed,
let even my head be cut off—
but under no circumstances at all
will I bow down to the enemy, the mental afflictions. ^4-44

An ordinary enemy, even if driven from the land,
will settle elsewhere, gather strength,
and then return again—
but the enemy of the afflictions does not work that way. ^4-45

Once cut away by the eye of wisdom that sees affliction as affliction
and banished from my mind, where could it possibly go?
Where could it stay, from which it might come back to harm me?
It is simply that my mind is weak and lacks the effort needed. ^4-46

The afflictions do not reside in the sense objects, nor in the sense faculties,
nor somewhere in between, nor anywhere else besides—
so where do they reside, from which they harm all beings everywhere?
This is merely like an illusion; so, freed from fear in my heart,
I should hold to effort for the sake of understanding—
why would I let myself be harmed pointlessly in hell and elsewhere? ^4-47

Having reflected in this way, I should strive
to accomplish the trainings just as they have been explained.
If a patient does not listen to the doctor's advice,
how could medicines that are needed ever cure that illness? ^4-48

This concludes Chapter Four, "Teaching Conscientiousness," from Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^4-a

## 5. Chapter 5: Guarding Awareness ^5-0

Those who wish to keep their training
must guard their mind with great care.
Without guarding this wandering mind,
one can never keep to the training. ^5-1

An untamed, drunken elephant
does not cause as much harm in this world
as does the elephant of an unchecked mind
when it is left to run wild, causing torment beyond measure. ^5-2

But if the elephant of the mind
is tied by the rope of constant mindfulness,
then all fears will disappear
and every kind of goodness will come into our hands. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears,
snakes, and all manner of enemies,
the guardians of the hell realms,
malevolent spirits, and demons of every kind— ^5-4

all of these are bound
simply by binding this one mind;
and by taming this one mind alone,
all of them are tamed as well. ^5-5

For the Truthful One himself has taught
that all these fears
and the boundless sufferings of the world
arise from the mind alone. ^5-6

Who forged the weapons
that torment beings in the hell realms?
Who made that burning ground of iron?
Where did those raging fires come from? ^5-7

All of these, the Sage has said,
are born from a mind gone wrong.
Thus in all three realms of existence
there is nothing to fear but the mind itself. ^5-8

If perfecting the practice of generosity
meant ending the poverty of all beings,
then, since there are still beings in want today,
how could the earlier buddhas have already perfected it? ^5-9

Rather, the perfection of generosity is said to be
the intention to give away everything one has—
all one's possessions and their fruits—
to every being, without exception.
So this perfection is a matter of mind. ^5-10

As for fish and other creatures—
where could one drive them all so they'd never be killed?
Only by attaining the intention to give up harming
is the perfection of ethical discipline explained to arise. ^5-11

Beings as unruly as the whole of space itself
can never all be overcome one by one.
But if this one thing—the angry mind—is overcome,
it is as if all those enemies have been overcome at once. ^5-12

Where could enough leather be found
to cover the whole surface of the earth?
Yet with leather the size of the sole of one's shoe alone,
it is as if the whole earth is covered. ^5-13

Likewise, I cannot possibly turn back
every external harm one by one.
But if I turn back this mind of mine,
what need is there to turn back anything else? ^5-14

Producing one clear moment of mind
can bring the fruit of rebirth in the realm of Brahma and the like;
but the fruits of actions of body and speech
are not so, if the underlying resolve is weak. ^5-15

Even if recitations and various austerities
are practiced for a very long time,
if the mind is distracted elsewhere while doing them,
the Knower of Reality has said they are pointless. ^5-16

Those who do not understand this—
that mind is the secret essence, the very heart of the teaching—
though they wish for happiness and to end suffering,
wander pointlessly, achieving nothing. ^5-17

Therefore I must take up this mind of mine
and guard it well.
Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind,
what use are countless other disciplines? ^5-18

Just as one takes care of a wound
when surrounded by an unruly crowd,
so, living among people who might do harm,
one should always guard this wound that is the mind. ^5-19

If one is careful of a wound
even for fear of its small pain,
why not guard this wound of the mind
against being crushed beneath the mountains of hell? ^5-20

Living in this way, with this kind of conduct,
even while dwelling among wicked people,
or even in the midst of women,
one's disciplined resolve will remain firm and undiminished. ^5-21

Let my possessions perish, let my honor go,
let my very body and livelihood be lost,
let other virtues too decline—
but never, ever, let this mind decline. ^5-22

To those who wish to guard their mind,
I say, with my palms joined together:
please guard mindfulness and vigilant awareness
with every effort you can make. ^5-23

People overwhelmed by illness
have no strength for any task;
likewise, when the mind is overwhelmed by confusion,
one has no strength for any task at all. ^5-24

Whatever a person lacking vigilant awareness
has heard, reflected on, or meditated upon,
will not remain in memory,
like water in a leaking pot. ^5-25

Many who have great learning, who have faith,
and who apply themselves with diligence
are nevertheless stained by the fault
of lacking vigilant awareness, and become tainted by wrongdoing. ^5-26

Thieves who lack vigilant awareness
follow in the wake of failing mindfulness;
even merit that has been carefully gathered
gets stolen by them, and one goes to a bad rebirth, as if robbed. ^5-27

This band of thieves—the destructive emotions—
is always looking for an opening;
finding one, they steal away our virtue
and destroy even our chance of a fortunate rebirth. ^5-28

Therefore mindfulness must never
be allowed to stray from the gate of the mind.
If it does stray, one should recall
the harms of bad rebirths, and restore it at once. ^5-29

Mindfulness arises easily
in those who, through respect and fear of wrongdoing,
associate with teachers,
receive guidance from spiritual masters, and act with reverence toward the fortunate. ^5-30

"The buddhas and bodhisattvas
see everything, without any obstruction.
In the presence of them all,
I am always standing"— ^5-31

thinking in this way, one develops
a sense of conscience, reverence, and wholesome fear;
and in this way, the recollection of the buddhas
will arise again and again. ^5-32

When mindfulness stands watch
at the gate of the mind for the sake of guarding it,
then vigilant awareness will come,
and even what had strayed will return. ^5-33

Having first recognized clearly
that the untrained mind carries this kind of flaw,
I should then, like a piece of wood,
remain firm and unmoved. ^5-34

I should never let my eyes
wander about pointlessly and distractedly.
Rather, with a settled resolve,
I should always keep my gaze lowered as I look. ^5-35

But to rest one's eyes,
it is fine to look around now and then.
If someone comes into view,
one should look at them and say, "Welcome, it is good you have come." ^5-36

To check for dangers on the road and so forth,
one may look in all four directions again and again;
and when resting, one may turn and look
behind oneself as well. ^5-37

Having checked ahead and behind,
one should then proceed to go or to come.
In this way, in every situation,
one should act only after knowing what is needed. ^5-38

Having resolved, "I will hold my body like this,"
and having begun some activity,
one should from time to time check
how the body happens to be positioned. ^5-39

Just as one carefully watches a maddened elephant
tied to a great post,
to see that the leash does not slip,
so with full effort one should watch
this maddened elephant of the mind
that has been tied to the great post of thinking on the teaching. ^5-40

"How can I make sure my effort at meditative concentration
does not slip away even for a single instant?"—
asking this again and again,
one should examine one's own mind in just this way. ^5-41

If, because of fear, or because of a festive occasion, and so on,
it is not possible to maintain this constant checking,
then one may act freely as the occasion demands.
For it is said that at the time of giving,
one may set aside the rules of ethical discipline. ^5-42

Having thought something through and begun a task,
one should not let the mind think of anything else
until that task alone
is accomplished, with full attention fixed on it. ^5-43

In this way, everything will be done well;
otherwise, neither task will succeed.
And in this way, the subsidiary destructive emotions
that come from lacking vigilant awareness will not increase. ^5-44

Whenever various kinds of idle talk
or various astonishing spectacles arise
and people everywhere are drawn to watch,
one should give up attachment to them. ^5-45

If, without any real purpose, one starts digging in the earth,
cutting grass, or drawing lines in the dirt,
one should recall the training of the Blessed One
and, out of a sense of caution, stop at once. ^5-46

Whenever I wish to move
or wish to speak,
I should first examine my own mind,
and then act appropriately, with steady awareness. ^5-47

Whenever I notice
that my mind is becoming attached or wishes to become angry,
I should not act, and should not speak,
but should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-48

When my mind is excited and mocking,
or filled with pride and arrogance,
or wishes to expose others' faults,
or wishes to be deceptive and evasive— ^5-49

whenever my mind is eager for self-praise
or wishes to belittle others,
or is contemptuous and full of regret-inducing scorn—
at such times, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-50

Whenever my mind seeks gain, honor, or fame,
or seeks servants and followers,
or wishes to be waited upon—
at such times, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-51

Whenever the wish arises
to abandon others' interests and pursue only my own,
or the urge arises to speak out of such a motive—
at such times, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-52

Whenever impatience, laziness, timidity,
shameless boldness, senseless chatter,
or partisan attachment to my own side arises in my mind—
at such times, I should remain like a piece of wood. ^5-53

Having in this way examined my mind
for every affliction and every pointless effort,
I should then, as a hero would,
firmly hold it in place with the appropriate remedy. ^5-54

With deep conviction, full confidence,
steadiness, respect, and courtesy,
with a sense of conscience and wholesome fear,
calm, and a wish to bring joy to others— ^5-55

I should not grow discouraged
by the conflicting desires of ordinary, immature people;
recognizing that their afflictions
give rise to such wishes, I should be filled with compassion. ^5-56

Acting always for the sake of both myself and all beings,
in matters free of any wrongdoing,
and without any sense of self-importance, as if I were an illusion,
I should hold my mind steady at all times. ^5-57

Reflecting again and again
that after a very long time I have finally obtained this excellent freedom and opportunity of human life,
I should hold my mind
utterly unmoving, like Mount Meru. ^5-58

If, when vultures fight over a piece of flesh
and drag it back and forth between them,
you, my mind, feel no displeasure,
why now do you cling so possessively to this body? ^5-59

Having claimed this body as your own,
why, mind, do you go on guarding it so?
Since you and it are, in truth, separate things,
what use is it to you? ^5-60

Foolish mind, why do you not instead
take hold of a clean form made of wood?
Why guard this filthy contraption,
this rotting machine made of impure things? ^5-61

First, with your own understanding,
strip away this layer of skin;
then, with the blade of discernment,
separate the flesh from the web of bones beneath it. ^5-62

Then, having broken open the bones as well,
look all the way down to the marrow within,
and examine for yourself
whether there is any essence to be found here. ^5-63

If, having searched so diligently,
you still find no essence there,
why, mind, do you go on
guarding this body out of attachment? ^5-64

You cannot eat its filth,
you cannot drink its blood,
you cannot suck out its entrails—
so what use is this body to you? ^5-65

It would be fitting to guard this body
only for the sake of feeding jackals and vultures.
As it is, this human body
is fit only to be put to use for a worthy purpose. ^5-66

Even if you guard it so carefully,
the merciless Lord of Death
will seize it and give it to the vultures and dogs;
what will you do then? ^5-67

If you would not give clothing
even to a servant who is of no use to you,
then why feed and pamper this body,
when, however it is nourished, it will go elsewhere in the end? ^5-68

So, having paid this body its wages,
let it now work for its keep.
Nothing should be given to it
without some benefit in return. ^5-69

Regarding the body only as a vessel
for going and coming,
I should transform it, for the sake of accomplishing the aims of beings,
into something like a wish-fulfilling vehicle. ^5-70

Being thus self-possessed,
I should always keep a smiling face,
giving up frowning and scowling,
and be a sincere and open friend to all beings. ^5-71

I should not carelessly and noisily
set down chairs and other such things,
nor throw open doors with force;
I should always take delight in being gentle and restrained. ^5-72

The heron, the cat, and the thief
accomplish what they intend
by moving quietly and stealthily;
a practitioner should always act in just this way. ^5-73

I should skillfully accept, with respect,
and hold above my head,
the words of those who are adept at guiding others
and who offer help even unasked,
becoming a student of everyone, at all times. ^5-74

To everything well said by anyone,
I should say, "Well spoken,"
and when I see someone doing good,
I should praise them and cause them heartfelt joy. ^5-75

I should speak of others' good qualities behind their backs,
and when their good qualities are mentioned, I should agree and add to the praise;
but when my own good qualities are mentioned,
I should recognize them as merely good qualities, without conceit. ^5-76

All undertakings are done for the sake of finding joy,
yet joy of that kind is rare, even for money.
Therefore, in the good qualities that others have achieved,
I should partake of the happiness of rejoicing. ^5-77

There is no loss to me in this life from doing so,
and great happiness follows in future lives;
whereas from doing wrong, one has no joy now and suffers greatly,
and even greater suffering follows in lives to come. ^5-78

I should speak in a manner that is heartfelt and connected,
clear in meaning and pleasing to hear,
free of attachment and hostility,
gentle, and fitting to the occasion. ^5-79

Even when merely looking at other beings with my eyes,
I should look at them directly and with kindness,
thinking, "It is in dependence on these very beings
that I will attain buddhahood." ^5-80

Great merit arises constantly—
whether motivated by genuine devotion
or by the intention to apply an antidote—
in fields that have good qualities, or that have helped me, or that are suffering. ^5-81

Being skilled and diligent,
I should always undertake my tasks myself,
and in all such tasks
I should not depend on anyone else to do them for me. ^5-82

The perfections—generosity and the rest—
each surpass the one before it in importance;
one should not give up something greater for the sake of something lesser.
Above all, one should keep the welfare of others foremost in mind. ^5-83

Understanding this, I should remain
constantly devoted to the welfare of others.
The One of Great Compassion, who sees far and wide,
has permitted, for such a person, even what is ordinarily forbidden. ^5-84

One should share one's food with those fallen into wrongdoing, those without protectors,
and those observing ascetic vows,
eating only a modest amount oneself,
and give away everything except the three robes of a monastic. ^5-85

One should not harm this body, which is dedicated
to the practice of the sacred teaching,
for the sake of some trivial matter;
acting in this way, one will swiftly fulfill the aspirations of all beings. ^5-86

One should not give away this body
with an impure motivation of compassion;
rather, one should give it away only
as a means of accomplishing great purposes, both now and in the future. ^5-87

One should not teach the sacred teaching to someone lacking respect,
to someone who, though not ill, has wrapped a cloth around their head,
to someone holding a parasol, a staff, or a weapon,
or to someone with their head covered. ^5-88

One should not teach the profound and vast teachings to those of lesser capacity,
nor teach without a male teaching a female listener where custom requires otherwise;
rather, one should apply oneself with equal respect
to both lesser and higher teachings alike. ^5-89

One who is a fit vessel for the vast teachings
should not be directed toward lesser teachings;
one should not abandon proper conduct,
nor mislead others through sutras or mantras. ^5-90

When disposing of a chewed toothpick
or spit, one should cover it over;
and it is considered shameful to relieve oneself,
or discard such things, into water or on open ground meant for others' use. ^5-91

One should not eat with a full mouth,
noisily, or with the mouth wide open;
one should not sit with legs stretched out,
nor rub one's hands together needlessly. ^5-92

One should not share a vehicle, a bed, or a seat
alone with a woman not one's own;
one should observe and ask about
whatever might cause others in the world to lose confidence, and avoid it. ^5-93

One should not gesture by pointing with a finger;
rather, with respect,
one should indicate directions, and everything else,
using the whole of one's right hand. ^5-94

One should not wave the arms about wildly;
a slight gesture, along with a soft sound made to draw attention,
such as snapping the fingers, is enough;
otherwise, one risks appearing unrestrained. ^5-95

One should lie down to sleep in the manner
of the Protector's repose at the time of his passing into peace,
facing the direction one intends to rise toward,
and resolve firmly, from the very start, to rise quickly and with full awareness. ^5-96

Since the conduct of bodhisattvas
is taught to be boundless,
one should definitely practice, for as long as that boundlessness lasts,
the conduct that trains and purifies the mind. ^5-97

Three times during the day and three times during the night,
one should recite the Three Heaps Sutra;
relying on the buddhas and on the awakening mind,
one can pacify the remainder of one's downfalls through this practice. ^5-98

Whatever the situation, whether it concerns
oneself or is under another's influence,
one should make every effort to train
in whatever training has been taught for that particular situation. ^5-99

There is nothing at all
that the children of the buddhas need not train in;
for one who dwells in this way, skilled in the practice,
there is nothing that does not become a source of merit. ^5-100

Whether directly or indirectly,
one should do nothing except what is for the sake of beings;
solely for the sake of beings,
one should dedicate everything toward awakening. ^5-101

One should never, even at the cost of one's life,
give up a spiritual teacher
who is constantly virtuous, skilled in the meaning of the Great Vehicle,
and who upholds the supreme discipline of the bodhisattvas. ^5-102

One should train according to the way of relying on a spiritual teacher
as taught in the Life of Śrī Sambhava;
this and other instructions given by the Buddha
should be understood by reading the sutras. ^5-103

The trainings are found within the sutras themselves;
therefore one should read the sutras.
The Sutra of the Essence of Space
should be looked to first of all. ^5-104

Because it explains at length
what must be practiced at all times,
one should also definitely look again and again
at the Compendium of Trainings. ^5-105

Or, for now, one may look briefly
at the condensed Compendium of Sutras,
and one should also make an effort to look
at both works composed by the noble Nagarjuna. ^5-106

Whatever is not prohibited in one of these texts
should indeed be practiced;
and having seen what is taught in the trainings,
one should practice it correctly, in order to guard the minds of ordinary people in the world. ^5-107

To repeatedly examine
the state of one's body and mind—
this alone, in brief,
is the very definition of guarding vigilant awareness. ^5-108

These things should be practiced with the body;
what use is it to merely recite words about them?
Does merely reading a medical text
actually help a sick person? ^5-109

This concludes Chapter Five, "Guarding Awareness," from *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^5-a

## 6. Chapter 6: Patience ^6-0

Whatever good I have gathered over a thousand eons—acts of generosity, offerings made to the buddhas, and other virtuous deeds—all of it can be destroyed by a single flash of anger. ^6-1

There is no wrongdoing equal to hatred, and no discipline equal to patience. Therefore I should make every effort to cultivate patience, approaching it in all its different forms. ^6-2

Whoever carries the festering wound of hatred in their mind will never experience peace of mind. They will find no joy or happiness; sleep will not come, and they will feel constantly unsettled. ^6-3

Even people who depend on someone for their livelihood and status, and who have been treated kindly by them, may still turn on that very benefactor and seek to destroy them, out of hatred. ^6-4

Because of hatred, one's friends grow weary and turn away, and even one's generosity fails to draw others near. In short, there is no way that anger can ever bring peace or well-being. ^6-5

The sufferings brought on by the enemy that is anger are many. But whoever strives with determination to conquer anger finds happiness both in this life and beyond. ^6-6

When something unwanted happens, or something we want is blocked, the mind finds fuel for its distress in this, and anger grows strong and destroys me. ^6-7

Therefore I must destroy the very fuel of this enemy, for this enemy has no other function than to cause me harm. ^6-8

Whatever happens to me, I must not let it disturb my mind's joy. Being upset does not get me what I want, and it only ruins my good qualities. ^6-9

If a problem can still be fixed, why be upset about it at all? And if there is truly nothing that can be done about it, what use is there in being upset? ^6-10

We do not want suffering, contempt, harsh words, or unpleasant speech to come to ourselves or to our loved ones—yet we wish exactly that upon our enemies. ^6-11

The causes of happiness arise only occasionally, while causes of suffering are extremely numerous. Without suffering, there is no way to grow weary of cyclic existence and seek freedom from it. So, my mind, hold yourself steady. ^6-12

People known as devotees of Karna and Durga endure meaningless burning and cutting to prove their fortitude. So why should I, seeking freedom from suffering, be a coward now? ^6-13

There is nothing that does not become easier through familiarity. So, by growing accustomed to bearing small hardships, I should train myself to bear great harm as well. ^6-14

Who does not see how the bites of snakes and flies, the discomfort of hunger and thirst, and afflictions like skin rashes, are all sufferings that accomplish nothing—yet we endure them all the same? ^6-15

I should not be oversensitive to heat, cold, rain, wind, travel, illness, captivity, or being struck. If I act that way, being overly sensitive, the harm will only increase. ^6-16

Some people become even braver at the sight of their own blood, while others faint and collapse at the mere sight of someone else's blood. ^6-17

This difference comes from the resoluteness or weakness of one's own mind. So I should treat harm with disdain and refuse to let sufferings overpower me. ^6-18

Even when suffering arises, a wise person does not let their mind become disturbed or troubled. For when waging war against the destructive emotions, there is naturally much harm to be endured in that battle. ^6-19

Those who disdain all suffering and conquer enemies like hatred are the true heroes who achieve victory; all others merely kill what is already dead. ^6-20

Suffering also has good qualities: through disillusionment with it, pride is dispelled; compassion arises for those trapped in cyclic existence; one comes to shun wrongdoing and delight in virtue. ^6-21

Since I do not get angry at conditions like bile disorders, which are major sources of suffering, why should I be angry at beings who have minds? They, too, are all driven by conditions beyond their control. ^6-22

Just as illness arises even though we do not want it to, so too, destructive emotions arise forcefully even though we do not wish for them. ^6-23

People become angry without ever intending, "I will get angry now." And likewise, anger arises on its own without anyone intending to produce it. ^6-24

All the many kinds of faults and varied forms of wrongdoing arise from the force of conditions; none of it happens independently, under its own power. ^6-25

The gathering of conditions that produces such things has no intention of producing them, nor does whatever is produced by them have any intention of being produced. ^6-26

Whatever is considered a "primal essence," and whatever is imagined as a "self," these do not arise through any deliberate intention to arise. ^6-27

If something does not yet exist, then whatever intention there might be for it to arise would have to constantly attend to some object; but such an intention itself does not cease to exist beforehand either. ^6-28

If this self were permanent, it would clearly be as inactive as space. Even coming into contact with other conditions, something unchanging could not do anything at all. ^6-29

Even at the moment of acting, if the self stayed exactly as before, what would its action have done to it? What connection could there be by saying "this is its action"? ^6-30

In this way, everything is under the power of other things, and because of being under that power, nothing has its own independent power. Understanding this, I will not become angry at things, which are like illusions with no substance of their own. ^6-31

Someone might object: "By what means could anything be prevented, and would such prevention even make sense?" But relying on such means, it is not at all unreasonable to wish for suffering to be brought to an end. ^6-32

Therefore, when I see someone—whether an enemy or a friend—doing something improper, I should simply reflect, "this has arisen from such conditions," and thereby remain at ease. ^6-33

If things happened simply according to what beings wished, then no one, none of the embodied beings at all, would ever suffer, since no one wants suffering. ^6-34

Yet out of carelessness, people harm even themselves, with thorns and the like; and driven by desire for things like a woman's affection, they get agitated and even starve themselves. ^6-35

Some hang themselves or leap off cliffs, eat poison or unwholesome food, or bring harm upon themselves through unwholesome conduct. ^6-36

When such people, overpowered by the destructive emotions, go so far as to kill even their own dear selves, how could they possibly refrain from harming the bodies of others? ^6-37

Since the destructive emotions arising in such a way drive people even to acts like self-destruction, if compassion does not arise for them, at the very least anger toward them makes no sense at all. ^6-38

If it is simply the nature of childish, unwise people to harm others, then it makes no more sense to be angry at them for it than to be angry at fire for its nature to burn. ^6-39

And if this fault is merely temporary while the nature of the being is basically good, even then anger makes no sense—it would be like being angry at the sky because smoke happens to rise into it. ^6-40

If I would be angry at the wielder of a stick for striking me with it, it would make more sense to be angry at the hatred that drives that person to strike, since it is that hatred, and not the person themselves, doing the striking. ^6-41

In the past, I too caused this kind of harm to other beings. So it is fitting that this harm should now come back to me, one who harms living beings. ^6-42

Both that person's weapon and my own body are causes of my suffering: they wielded the weapon, and I provided the body as its target—so at whom should I really direct my anger? ^6-43

This human body, resembling an open sore, is so tender it cannot bear even to be touched, and it is full of suffering. If I, blinded by craving, have taken hold of such a body, then whom should I blame when it is harmed? ^6-44

Childish beings do not want suffering yet greedily pursue its causes. So when harm comes to us through our own fault, why blame someone else for it? ^6-45

Just as the guardians of hell and the forest of sword-leaved trees are produced by one's own past actions, so who is there to be angry at for such harm? ^6-46

Those who harm me arise because they are stirred up by my own past actions; and because of this, they are led toward the hells. Am I not, in fact, the one who destroys them? ^6-47

By relying on these harm-doers and being patient with them, I purify much of my own wrongdoing; but because of relying on me, they will suffer for a long time in the hells. ^6-48

Since I am the one who harms them, while they in fact do me good, why, my wretched mind, do you get angry, so perversely reversing things? ^6-49

If I had the good quality of a wholesome intention, I would not have to go to hell in the first place. But since I do guard my own mind well through patience, then what have those beings really done to harm me here? ^6-50

If I retaliated by harming them in turn, that would not protect them from their own suffering, and it would also ruin my own spiritual practice, so that my discipline would be destroyed. ^6-51

Since the mind is not something with a physical body, no one is able to destroy it in any way at all. But because of clinging strongly to the body, the body is harmed by suffering. ^6-52

Contempt, harsh speech, and unpleasant words do not actually harm the body. So why, my mind, do you become so furiously angry? ^6-53

If others' displeasure with me does not, in this life or in future lives, consume me in any way, then why should I be so averse to it? ^6-54

If I dislike it only because it obstructs my gains, then in truth I am simply giving up my gains right here while my wrongdoing remains firmly in place. ^6-55

It would be better for me to die today than to live a long life through wrong livelihood, for even if I lived long in that corrupt way, the suffering of death would be exactly the same in the end. ^6-56

Consider someone who experiences a hundred years of happiness in a dream and then wakes up, and someone else who experiences just a moment of happiness and then wakes up. ^6-57

Once both of them wake up, that happiness never comes back to either one. It is exactly the same for a long life and a short life—at the moment of death, both come to the same end. ^6-58

Even if I gain great wealth and enjoy happiness for a long time, I will leave empty-handed and stripped bare, just like someone robbed by a thief. ^6-59

Suppose someone says: "If I live by means of my gains, I can exhaust my wrongdoing and create merit." But if I become angry for the sake of such gains, will I not instead exhaust my merit and create wrongdoing? ^6-60

If the very purpose for which I am alive comes to ruin, then what is the point of staying alive, if all I do is commit wrongdoing? ^6-61

Suppose I get angry at someone speaking ill of me, thinking it harms living beings. Then why am I not equally angry when I myself speak ill of others? ^6-62

If I can tolerate the lack of faith in others toward things I do not believe in, because it depends on causes beyond their control, why can I not tolerate unpleasant speech, since it too depends on the arising of destructive emotions beyond one's control? ^6-63

Even when people insult or destroy images of the Buddha, monuments, or the sacred teachings, I should not feel hatred toward them, since the buddhas and such cannot actually be harmed. ^6-64

As for those who harm my teachers, relatives, or friends, I should, as before, see this too as arising from such conditions, and turn my anger away. ^6-65

Since harm done to living beings comes either from someone with intention or without intention, why hold it against those with intention? Therefore, I should simply be patient with the harm. ^6-66

Some do wrong through delusion, and some become angry through delusion. Among these, who can we call blameless, and who can we call at fault? ^6-67

Why did that person, in the past, act in a way that now leads them to harm others? Since everything depends on past actions, why should I hold this against them now? ^6-68

Seeing things this way, I should strive, in every possible way, to bring about a state where everyone feels loving-kindness toward one another, and I will apply myself diligently to accumulating merit. ^6-69

For example, when a fire breaks out and spreads to a neighboring house, one removes and discards anything—like straw—that would let the fire spread further. ^6-70

In just the same way, whatever I am attached to becomes fuel that spreads the fire of hatred; fearing that it will burn away my merit, I should discard that attachment at once. ^6-71

If someone condemned to die has their hand cut off instead and is thereby freed, is that not fortunate? And if I am freed from hell through the mere suffering of being human, is that not also fortunate? ^6-72

If I cannot even bear this much suffering right now, then why do I not put a stop to anger, the very cause of the suffering of hell? ^6-73

For the sake of what I desired, I have already endured being burned in hell thousands of times, yet through all of that I have accomplished neither my own good nor the good of others. ^6-74

But this present suffering is nothing compared to that, and it can accomplish a great purpose. So I should welcome only this suffering—the kind that removes harm from all beings. ^6-75

If I feel joy and happiness when others praise someone possessing good qualities, then why, my mind, do I not feel that same joy when I myself praise them? ^6-76

This joy and happiness of yours is a source of happiness that is entirely faultless; it is what the good and virtuous themselves approve of, and it is the best way to draw others toward you. ^6-77

But if you do not want this happiness that comes from wishing others well, then you should also give up things like giving out wages and other benefits, and you will lose out both in this life and beyond. ^6-78

When I speak of my own good qualities, I want others to feel pleased about it. But when I speak of someone else's good qualities, I refuse to let myself feel pleased about it. ^6-79

Having generated the wish for full awakening for the sake of all beings' happiness, why then would I feel angry when beings find happiness on their own? ^6-80

If it is said that we wish for all beings, honored throughout the three worlds, to attain buddhahood, then why be so troubled at merely seeing them receive some small mark of respect now? ^6-81

If a relative provides for someone you yourself are supposed to care for and give to, would you not be glad about this, rather than getting angry all over again? ^6-82

If you do not even wish that for beings, then what kind of wish for their awakening do you really have? If someone gets angry at another's prosperity, how could the awakening-mind possibly exist in them at all? ^6-83

If a gift comes to that person either from someone else or remains in the house of the giver, either way it was never going to come to you—so what does it matter to you whether it is given or not given? ^6-84

Why would anyone reject the merit, the faith, and their own good qualities—by which I mean, why would anyone refuse to accept when something good comes their way, and instead get angry about it? Tell me. ^6-85

Not only do you fail to regret the wrongdoing you yourself have committed, but you also seek to compete with others who have done good deeds. ^6-86

Even if some enemy of yours is unhappy, what is there for you to be happy about in that? Your mere wish for it will not actually become a cause of their harm. ^6-87

And even if your wish caused that person's suffering to come about, what would you have to be happy about in that? If you say you would be satisfied, could there be anything more ruinous than that kind of satisfaction? ^6-88

That sharp hook cast by the fisherman of the destructive emotions is unbearable; once caught by it, the guardians of hell will surely cook me in their cauldrons. ^6-89

Being honored and praised brings no increase in merit, no increase in lifespan, no increase in strength, no freedom from illness, and no physical comfort. ^6-90

If I understood my own true benefit, what real benefit would I find in praise? If all I want is a pleasant feeling in my mind, then I might as well rely on things like ornaments and liquor for that. ^6-91

People waste their wealth for the sake of fame, and even kill themselves for it. What good can mere words do? When one is dead, who is there to enjoy that fame? ^6-92

When a sandcastle collapses, children wail in despair. In just the same way, when praise and fame collapse, my own mind acts just like a child. ^6-93

Since a mere sound has no mind of its own, it cannot possibly have any intention to praise me. Someone might say the true cause of my joy is that people find me pleasing. ^6-94

But whether it is someone else or even myself, of what benefit to me is someone else's pleasure? That pleasure and happiness belongs entirely to that other person; I get no share of it whatsoever. ^6-95

If I could actually feel happy because that other person is happy, then I should feel that way about everyone's happiness. So why am I not happy when others find happiness through pleasing someone else, yet unhappy when they find it through praising me? ^6-96

Therefore, the joy that arises in me merely from being told "I am praised" is, on examination, simply unreasonable—it is nothing more than childish behavior. ^6-97

Praise and the like distract me and undermine my sense of disillusionment with cyclic existence; they breed envy toward those who have good qualities, and they ruin my own excellence. ^6-98

Therefore, those close to me who work to destroy my praise and reputation are in fact working to protect me from falling into the lower realms—are they not? ^6-99

Since I am seeking liberation, I have no need to be bound up in gains and honors. So why would I be angry at those who work to free me from such bondage? ^6-100

Just as I wish to enter into suffering, if some being, as though blessed by the buddhas themselves, becomes like a door that will not open to let such suffering in, why would I be angry at them for that? ^6-101

Someone might say, "But this obstructs my merit," and think it right to be angry about that. Yet there is no discipline equal to patience—so should I not instead devote myself to that? ^6-102

If, because of my own fault, I fail to be patient in this situation, then it is I myself who obstruct the very cause of merit that stands right before me. ^6-103

Whatever exists only because something else exists, and does not arise when that other thing is absent, is itself the effect and that other thing is its true cause. So how could that cause ever be called an obstruction? ^6-104

The beggar who arrives at the right time is not creating an obstacle to generosity. In the same way, someone who helps me go forth into a life of renunciation cannot rightly be called an obstacle to that renunciation. ^6-105

In this world, beggars are common, but those who cause harm are rare. For if I have done no harm to others, no one, in turn, will do harm to me. ^6-106

Therefore, just as a treasure appearing in one's own house without any effort at all is a cause for joy, so I should rejoice in my enemy, since he has become an aid to my conduct as a bodhisattva. ^6-107

Since this fruit of patience has been accomplished together by both of us, him and me, it is right that the first offering of its fruit should go to him, since he is the true cause of my patience. ^6-108

Suppose someone objects: "This enemy had no intention of helping me develop patience, so he does not deserve to be honored." Then why do we honor the sacred teachings, which are also merely a fitting cause for accomplishing something, without themselves having any such intention? ^6-109

Suppose it is objected: "This enemy intends to harm me, so he does not deserve to be honored." But if instead he strove to help me, like a doctor, then how would my patience ever come about? ^6-110

Therefore, since patience arises in dependence on a mind full of hostility, that hostile mind is itself the true cause of patience, and so it deserves to be honored just as the sacred teachings are. ^6-111

This is why the Sage said that there are two fields in which merit grows: the field of living beings and the field of the victorious buddhas. Many people have attained the perfection of excellence by pleasing these two fields well. ^6-112

Since buddhahood, the very fruit of the Dharma, is attained equally through living beings and through the buddhas, why is it that beings receive less respect than the way we respect the buddhas? ^6-113

The nobility of an intention is not measured by itself alone but by its result; on that basis, living beings too possess this same kind of good quality, and so they are equal to the buddhas in this regard. ^6-114

Whatever offering is made to someone who has a loving mind belongs to the greatness of that being; and whatever merit comes from faith in the buddhas is likewise the greatness of the buddhas themselves. ^6-115

Since living beings share a part in accomplishing the qualities of buddhahood, they are, in that respect, considered equal to the buddhas. Yet none of them is truly equal to the buddhas, whose ocean of good qualities has no limit at all. ^6-116

Even if only a small portion of good qualities were found in one place, among the entirety of such supreme excellent qualities, offering all three realms in order to honor even that portion would still be too little. ^6-117

Since living beings possess a share in giving rise to the supreme qualities of buddhahood, it is fitting, on this basis alone, that living beings should be considered worthy of being honored. ^6-118

Furthermore, who else, besides pleasing living beings, could repay those who are our unwavering friends and who bring us immeasurable benefit? ^6-119

Since it is by pleasing living beings that one repays those buddhas who gave up their very bodies and entered the depths of hell for their sake, no matter how greatly beings may harm me, I should treat them all with the very best of conduct. ^6-120

Even those who were once my lords did not spare their own bodies for the sake of beings. So how could I, foolish about this truth, act with pride and refuse to serve as their servant? ^6-121

The sages rejoice when beings are happy, and feel displeasure when beings are harmed. So by making beings happy, I please all the sages, and by harming beings, I harm the sages themselves. ^6-122

Just as when the whole body is engulfed in flame, no pleasures at all can bring the mind comfort, so too, when living beings are harmed, there is no way to please the great compassionate ones. ^6-123

Therefore, for whatever harm I have done to beings that has displeased the great compassionate ones, I now confess all of it, one by one, and I ask the Sage to forgive whatever displeasure I have caused. ^6-124

From this day forward, in order to please the buddhas, I will become a servant to the world. Let the many people trample my head under their feet—even if it kills me, I will not complain, so long as it pleases the Protector of the World. ^6-125

There is no doubt that the compassionate one who embodies all beings has made these beings his very own self. So why would I not pay homage to that Protector, seeing that these very beings, in their true nature, are that Protector himself? ^6-126

This alone is what pleases the buddhas, this alone truly accomplishes my own good, this alone dispels the suffering of the world—therefore this alone is what I should always do. ^6-127

For example, though a certain minister of a king may harm many people, those with far-sighted vision, even if capable of retaliating, do not strike back against him. ^6-128

This is because he is not acting alone; the king's own might stands behind his might. In the same way, I should never look down upon or harm even the weakest person who does me wrong. ^6-129

For behind such a person stand the guardians of hell and the compassionate buddhas alike—just as common people please a fierce king, so I should work to please all living beings. ^6-130

Even if a king becomes angry, could his wrath ever inflict on me the kind of suffering that comes from displeasing living beings and thereby experiencing the torment of the hells? ^6-131

And even if a king were pleased with me, could he ever grant me what comes from pleasing living beings—namely, the attainment of buddhahood itself? Such a thing is impossible for a king to give. ^6-132

Setting aside even the future attainment of buddhahood, which comes from pleasing living beings, why do we not see how, even in this very life, it also brings great splendor, fame, and happiness? ^6-133

Through patience one gains, in the course of cyclic existence, beauty and other such qualities, freedom from illness, a good reputation, an exceptionally long life, and the vast happiness of a universal monarch. ^6-134

This concludes the sixth chapter of the *Way of the Bodhisattva*, called "Teaching Patience." ^6-a

## 7. Chapter 7: Diligence ^7-0

Having gained patience in this way, cultivate diligence — sustained enthusiasm for what is good.
For it is through diligence that awakening is reached,
just as nothing moves without wind:
without diligence, no goodness arises. ^7-1

Diligence means taking joy in what is wholesome.
Let me explain its opposite:
laziness, clinging to what is unwholesome,
discouragement, and putting oneself down. ^7-2

Laziness arises from savoring the pleasure of idleness,
from craving the comfort of sleep,
and from feeling no weariness with the suffering of endless rebirth (samsara). ^7-3

Bound tightly by the net of destructive emotions,
caught again in the trap of rebirth,
already fallen into the jaws of the lord of death —
how is it that you still don't understand? ^7-4

Don't you see that death is killing off
those around you, one after another?
Yet still you rest content in sleep,
like a water buffalo left in the care of a butcher. ^7-5

With every path of escape sealed off,
and the lord of death watching you,
how can you take delight in food,
and how can you enjoy your sleep? ^7-6

Since death will come quickly,
gather what is needed for that time now.
Even if you were to give up laziness then,
what use would it be — it would be too late. ^7-7

Thinking, "This I have not yet begun,
and this I have only half finished,"
when death suddenly arrives without warning,
you will think, "Oh no, I am finished!" ^7-8

Seeing your grieving relatives, their faces swollen with sorrow,
eyes red, tears streaming down,
their hopes for you exhausted —
you will see in their faces the messengers of death approaching. ^7-9

Tormented by memories of your own wrongdoing,
and hearing the sounds of hell,
terrified, you will soil yourself in fear
and lose your senses — what good will that do you then? ^7-10

If you are this frightened even now,
like a fish flopping helplessly on land,
what need is there to mention
the unbearable suffering that comes from wrongdoing in the hells? ^7-11

Merely touching hot water
scalds your tender young skin —
yet having done deeds that lead to hell,
why do you go on living so comfortably? ^7-12

Wanting results without effort,
overly sensitive, easily harmed,
yet caught in the grip of death as if you were a god —
oh no, such a person will be destroyed by suffering! ^7-13

Relying on this human vessel,
you can cross the great river of suffering.
Since a vessel like this is hard to find again later,
fool, do not fall asleep now — this is not the time! ^7-14

Having abandoned the supreme joy
of the sacred teaching — a source of boundless delight —
why do you instead take pleasure
in mere distraction and frivolous amusement, causes of suffering? ^7-15

Do not be discouraged; gather your strength.
Take charge of yourself with full commitment.
Treat yourself and others as equal,
and exchange yourself for others. ^7-16

Do not become discouraged, thinking,
"How could I possibly reach awakening?"
For the Buddha, who speaks only truth,
has spoken this truth: ^7-17

Even creatures such as flies, mosquitoes, bees,
and various kinds of worms
can attain the unsurpassed awakening, so hard to reach,
if they generate the power of diligence. ^7-18

So if I, born as a human of noble family,
able to distinguish benefit from harm,
do not abandon the conduct that leads to awakening,
why should I not be able to attain it myself? ^7-19

But if I fear having to give up
my hands, feet, and other such things,
that is only because, confused,
I fail to weigh what is truly serious against what is trivial. ^7-20

For countless millions of eons,
I will be cut, burned, and flayed
again and again, over and over —
yet I will still not attain awakening this way. ^7-21

But the suffering I undergo in accomplishing awakening
has a definite limit;
it is like the pain of a surgical incision
made to remove a deeper, more damaging affliction. ^7-22

All physicians cure illness
that causes discomfort by means of unpleasant treatment.
So too, in order to destroy the many sufferings of samsara,
one should bear this small amount of discomfort. ^7-23

Yet the supreme physician, the Buddha,
does not prescribe such ordinary treatment;
through methods that are utterly gentle,
he cures immeasurable, great illnesses. ^7-24

The Guide first has students practice giving
by starting with simple things, like vegetables.
Once accustomed to that, later on
they gradually give away even their own flesh. ^7-25

When the attitude arises
that regards one's own body as being like a vegetable,
then what difficulty is there
in giving away one's own flesh and so forth? ^7-26

Since wrongdoing has been given up, there is no suffering;
since one has become wise, there is no unhappiness.
It is wrong thinking and wrongdoing
that harm the mind and body. ^7-27

Through merit, the body becomes at ease;
through wisdom, the mind becomes at ease.
So even remaining in samsara for the sake of others,
why would someone compassionate ever feel discouraged? ^7-28

Through the power of the awakening mind (the resolve to reach enlightenment for all beings),
past wrongdoing is exhausted,
and oceans of merit are gathered —
for this reason it is taught to surpass even the path of the hearers (those seeking liberation for themselves alone). ^7-29

So, mounting the horse of the awakening mind,
which dispels all weariness and fatigue,
and traveling from joy to joy,
what wise person could possibly grow discouraged? ^7-30

To accomplish the aims of living beings, four powers are needed: aspiration, steadfastness, joy, and the willingness to let go when needed.
Aspiration is generated by reflecting on the suffering and fear of wrongdoing,
and on the benefits of wholesome action. ^7-31

Having thus abandoned what runs contrary to diligence,
in order to strengthen aspiration, steadfastness, joy, and the willingness to let go,
one should make effort through the power of enthusiasm and self-mastery. ^7-32

The faults of myself and others
are countless — I must eliminate them.
Yet for each single fault,
it may take an ocean of eons to overcome it. ^7-33

Yet I do not see myself
having even begun to exhaust a fraction of my own faults.
Why does my heart not break,
facing this boundless source of suffering? ^7-34

The good qualities of myself and others
that I must cultivate are many,
and cultivating even one of them
may take an ocean of eons. ^7-35

Yet I have never even begun
to develop a single fraction of such good qualities.
How strange that I have wasted
this life that I somehow managed to obtain! ^7-36

I have not made offerings to the Blessed One (the Buddha),
nor given the joy of a great feast to others,
I have not done what serves the teachings,
nor fulfilled the wishes of the poor. ^7-37

I have not given fearlessness to the frightened,
nor comfort to the distressed. All I have truly produced
is the pain of being in my mother's womb
and further suffering. ^7-38

Because I have lacked devotion to the teaching,
both in the past and now,
I have fallen into such destitution —
who would ever abandon devotion to the teaching, knowing this? ^7-39

The Sage (the Buddha) has said that aspiration
is the root of all that is wholesome,
and that the root of aspiration itself
is constant meditation on the ripening of karmic results (the working out of cause and effect). ^7-40

Suffering, mental unhappiness,
fear in its many forms,
and separation from what one desires
all arise from harmful action. ^7-41

Through wholesome deeds done with a sincere heart,
wherever one may go,
one will be honored there
with the fruits and rewards of that merit. ^7-42

But someone who does harm, even while wishing for happiness,
wherever they may go,
will there be struck down
by the weapon of suffering that is their wrongdoing. ^7-43

Dwelling in the heart of a vast, fragrant, cooling lotus,
nourished into splendor by the sweet nectar of the Victor's (the Buddha's) speech,
one whose supreme body emerges from a lotus opened by the Sage's radiant light
becomes, through virtue, a child of the Sugata (the Buddha) in the Victor's very presence. ^7-44

Whereas one whose skin is entirely flayed off by the henchmen of the lord of death, utterly wretched,
and whose body is drenched in molten copper, boiling with intense heat,
who is pierced by blazing swords and spears until struck into a hundred pieces of flesh,
and who falls onto a burning ground of iron — that is what happens through much unwholesome action. ^7-45

Therefore one should aspire to virtue
and train in it with devotion.
Following the method of the Vajra Banner Discourse,
one should cultivate a healthy sense of confidence. ^7-46

First, examine your capacity —
then either begin the task or do not begin it.
It is better not to begin at all
than to begin and then turn back. ^7-47

For that pattern carries over into future lives,
increasing wrongdoing and suffering,
and even at other times and when results come due,
one's task remains unaccomplished and one falls short. ^7-48

Confidence should be applied to three things: to actions, to destructive emotions, and to one's own capability.
Thinking "I alone will do this" —
this is confidence regarding action. ^7-49

This world, powerless under the sway of destructive emotions,
is unable to accomplish its own good;
living beings are not capable as I am —
therefore I myself must do this. ^7-50

When others engage in lowly deeds,
how can I just stand by?
Better not to act out of pride,
but it is best to have no false humility either. ^7-51

Even a crow will act like a garuda (a mighty mythical eagle)
when it comes upon a dead snake.
Likewise, if I am weak-minded,
even a small fault will be able to harm me. ^7-52

For one who gives up effort out of discouragement,
is there any escape from hardship?
But for one who generates confidence along with effort,
even great difficulties are hard to overcome by anything else. ^7-53

Therefore, with a firm and steady mind,
I must overcome every downfall.
For if I were defeated by downfalls,
my wish to conquer the three realms of existence would become a laughingstock. ^7-54

I must be victorious over all things;
I must not be defeated by anything.
As a child of the Victor, the lion among beings,
I should hold to this kind of confidence. ^7-55

Beings overwhelmed by pride
are not the ones with true confidence — theirs is only the pride of destructive emotion.
Those with true confidence do not fall under the power of their enemies,
while the proud fall under the power of the enemy that is pride itself. ^7-56

Those puffed up by the pride of destructive emotion
are led by that pride to lower states of rebirth,
robbed of human joy,
and reduced to eating the scraps of others as servants — ^7-57

foolish, unattractive, weak,
and despised everywhere they go.
Even ascetics who are puffed up with such pride
still belong to the category of the arrogant —
tell me, then, what could be lower than that? ^7-58

But one who holds confidence in order to conquer the enemy that is pride —
such a person truly has confidence and is a hero, truly victorious.
Whoever fully destroys the enemy of pride when it arises,
brings living beings, just as they wish, to the complete fruit of victory. ^7-59

While remaining in the midst of destructive emotions,
one should stand firm in a thousand ways,
like a lion among foxes and other lesser beasts —
never letting oneself be overpowered by the mass of destructive emotions. ^7-60

Just as a person guards their own eyes above all else
even amid the greatest danger,
so too, whatever dangers arise,
one must never fall under the power of destructive emotions. ^7-61

Just as one longs for the pleasure of play,
whatever task one undertakes,
one should become attached to that task itself,
and never tire of the joy found in doing it. ^7-62

Although actions are done for the sake of happiness,
whether happiness results is uncertain;
but how can there be happiness
in not doing the very actions which do bring happiness? ^7-63

If people cannot get enough
of sense pleasures — like honey smeared on a razor's edge —
how could one ever have enough
of merit, whose fruit is peace and happiness? ^7-64

Therefore, in order to finish a task,
one should throw oneself into it,
as an elephant scorched by the midday sun
plunges into a cool lake when it finds one. ^7-65

If one's strength gives out,
one should set the task aside in order to take it up again later.
When a task is well completed,
one should let it go in favor of the one that follows, valuing what comes next. ^7-66

Just as a seasoned warrior, facing an enemy in battle,
parries the enemy's sword,
so too one should skillfully evade the weapons of destructive emotions
and strike down the enemies that they are. ^7-67

If a sword falls to the ground in battle,
one quickly picks it up again out of fear;
likewise, if the weapon of mindfulness is dropped,
one should quickly retrieve it, recalling the terrors of hell. ^7-68

Just as poison, once it reaches the blood,
spreads through the entire body,
so too, if destructive emotions find an opening,
wrongdoing will spread through the whole mind. ^7-69

Just as someone carrying a bowl brimful of oil
walks carefully, watched by a man with a sword
who threatens to kill them the moment they spill a drop,
those committed to the practice should exercise that same kind of vigilant care. ^7-70

So, just as one would leap up at once
if a snake fell into one's lap,
in the same way, when sleepiness or laziness arises,
one should quickly drive it away. ^7-71

Whenever a fault occurs,
one should reproach oneself for it in every way,
and reflect at length on how
to ensure it never happens again. ^7-72

Thinking, "How can I make mindfulness
become a habit in situations like these?" —
one should seek out either the company of those who inspire this
or work suited to that purpose. ^7-73

Recalling that one should always be capable in every task before undertaking it,
and thus keeping conscientiousness in mind,
one should rouse oneself
to be ready and alert. ^7-74

Just as the wind moves cotton fluff
back and forth, controlling it completely,
so too should one be governed by joyful enthusiasm —
only in that way will things be accomplished. ^7-75

This is the seventh chapter, "Teaching Diligence," from *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva* (a bodhisattva being one who has dedicated their spiritual practice to the awakening of all beings). ^7-a

## 8. Chapter 8: Meditative Concentration ^8-0

Having generated energetic effort in this way,
one should rest the mind in meditative concentration.
For a person whose mind is scattered and distracted
lives suspended between the fangs of the mind's afflictions. ^8-1

Through solitude of body and of mind,
distraction simply does not arise.
Therefore one should give up ordinary worldly life
and abandon distracting thoughts entirely. ^8-2

People fail to give up worldly life
because of attachment and craving for gain and the like.
Therefore, in order to give these up completely,
the wise should reflect in the following way. ^8-3

Special insight joined together with calm abiding
is known to destroy the mind's afflictions completely.
Knowing this, one should first seek calm abiding,
and this is achieved through a mind unattached to worldly concerns. ^8-4

What impermanent being would become
genuinely attached to another impermanent being?
Through such attachment, for thousands of lifetimes,
one will not even get to see the one who is dear. ^8-5

If we don't see the one we're attached to, we won't feel joy,
and the mind won't be able to settle in equipoise.
Even when we do see them, we're never satisfied,
and we go on being tormented by craving, just as before. ^8-6

If we become attached to other beings,
this thoroughly obscures the way things really are,
destroys the very feeling of disillusionment with worldly life,
and in the end brings only the torment of grief. ^8-7

By fixating our thinking on that one alone,
this precious human life passes by meaninglessly.
And because of impermanent friends and companions,
even the unchanging truth of the Dharma gets destroyed. ^8-8

If we act just like a foolish, immature person,
we're certain to end up in the lower realms of suffering.
Since they lead us toward something so unequal to us,
what is there to gain by keeping company with the immature? ^8-9

In one moment they become a friend,
and in an instant they turn into an enemy.
They get angry even at things that should bring joy —
ordinary, immature people are hard to please. ^8-10

If we say something helpful, they get angry;
they even turn me away from doing what's beneficial.
And if I don't listen to what they say,
their anger will lead me to a bad rebirth. ^8-11

They feel envy toward those above them, and rivalry with equals,
arrogant pride toward those beneath them,
and get angry when praise is not to their liking —
when will any benefit ever come from immature people? ^8-12

Whatever comes from associating with the immature —
praising oneself, putting others down,
and delighting in talk of worldly pleasures and such —
some kind of unwholesome result is bound to follow. ^8-13

In this way, relying on others like myself
only ends up in ruin.
They gain nothing beneficial from me,
and I gain nothing beneficial from them either. ^8-14

Therefore, one should keep a long distance from immature people.
When we do meet them, we should please them with a friendly smile,
but without becoming overly familiar or close —
it's best to maintain simple, ordinary courtesy. ^8-15

Just as a bee takes only the nectar from a flower,
one should take only what serves the essence of the Dharma,
and then, as if we had never met these people before,
remain without forming any close attachment to them. ^8-16

"I have many possessions and much honor,
and many people are fond of me" —
if we hold onto this kind of conceit,
terror will arise after death. ^8-17

Therefore, whatever the confused mind
becomes attached to,
that very thing will multiply a thousandfold
and turn into nothing but suffering, rising up again and again. ^8-18

Therefore the wise should not become attached;
from attachment, fear is born.
Understand clearly that all these things
will inevitably be lost by their very nature, and stand firm. ^8-19

Even if we gain abundant wealth
and come to enjoy fame and a good reputation,
we have no idea where we will go
once we leave behind this pile of possessions and renown. ^8-20

If someone criticizes me, what is there to be happy about
if someone else praises me?
And if someone praises me,
what is there to be unhappy about if someone else criticizes me? ^8-21

Sentient beings have all kinds of different inclinations;
even the Buddhas themselves cannot please them all.
So how could someone as flawed as I hope to please everyone?
Therefore let go of concern for ordinary worldly opinion. ^8-22

People criticize those without wealth or possessions,
and speak badly of those who have them.
By their very nature these people are hard to get along with —
how could any real joy ever come from them? ^8-23

Since immature people never feel joy
unless it serves their own self-interest,
the Buddhas have said
that immature people are never true friends. ^8-24

Wild animals and birds in the forest,
and the trees themselves, never speak unpleasant words.
When will I get to live together
with such pleasant, easy companions? ^8-25

Staying in a cave, an empty temple,
or beneath a tree,
when will I be able to move forward
without ever looking back, free of all attachment? ^8-26

When will I come to dwell
in vast, open places that belong to no one,
by their very nature unclaimed,
living freely there without any attachment? ^8-27

Owning next to nothing — just a begging bowl and the like,
wearing clothes that no one else wants,
not even bothering to hide this body —
when will I live free of fear in this way? ^8-28

Going to a charnel ground,
and comparing the bones of others there
with my own body — both subject to the same decay —
when will I look on them with an equal, even mind? ^8-29

This very body of mine
will come to such a state
that even jackals, drawn by its smell,
will no longer want to come near it. ^8-30

Even though this body arose as one single thing,
the flesh and bones that came into being together with it
will decay and fall apart, scattering in all directions —
how much more so, then, other loved ones? ^8-31

Alone we are born,
and alone, too, that very self dies.
Since no one else shares in our suffering,
what use are friends who only get in the way? ^8-32

Just as a traveler on the road
takes lodging in whatever place they find,
in just the same way, one journeying on the road of existence
takes up residence in whatever rebirth comes along. ^8-33

Until four people carry this body away
while the whole world mourns and grieves,
until that time comes,
I should go off to the forest. ^8-34

Without friends and without enemies,
I should dwell alone in solitude with this body,
having already considered myself as good as dead —
so that even when death actually comes, there will be no grief. ^8-35

Since no one nearby
will cause me grief or harm,
nothing will disturb
my recollection of the Buddha and other virtuous practices. ^8-36

Therefore I should dwell alone
in a forest bright and full of joy,
free of hardship, pleasant and happy,
where all distraction is calmed. ^8-37

Giving up every other concern,
thinking of nothing but settling my own mind,
I should make effort
to place the mind in equipoise and tame it. ^8-38

In both this life and the next,
desires bring about disaster:
in this life, killing, imprisonment, and mutilation;
in the next, rebirth in hell and the like. ^8-39

For the sake of desire, people have long relied
on many go-betweens, male and female,
and for its sake have not hesitated
to commit wrongdoing or endure disgrace. ^8-40

For its sake, I have thrown myself into danger,
squandered my wealth completely,
yet embracing the very thing
that supposedly brings the greatest joy — ^8-41

is nothing but a skeleton, nothing more.
It has no freedom of its own and is not really "mine,"
yet I desire it so intensely and cling to it so completely —
why, then, do I not go instead toward liberation from suffering? ^8-42

Someone who first has to work hard just to lift up this face,
then looks down bashfully even once it's raised,
and whether it was seen before or not,
now covers the face with a veil — ^8-43

that same afflicted face of yours,
right now, exactly as it appears before you,
once stripped bare and exposed by vultures —
why do you now recoil from seeing it? ^8-44

Even though others merely look at this body with their eyes,
you jealously guard it as your own;
so as long as it is being devoured by those vultures,
why, you miser, do you not guard it then? ^8-45

Having seen this heap of flesh
being eaten by vultures and other creatures,
why then do you decorate it — food for others —
with garlands of flowers and sandalwood ornaments? ^8-46

If you're frightened seeing it become
a mere skeleton, motionless and still,
why are you not frightened
when it moves about like a walking corpse, animated by others' will? ^8-47

From the same single kind of food,
comes both excrement and saliva.
Since we don't delight in the excrement,
why then do you delight in the saliva? ^8-48

Someone attached to sensual pleasure, touching
a soft cotton pillow, feels delighted,
saying it "does not smell of decay" —
but such a person is simply confused about what the body truly is: unclean. ^8-49

If we're attached to this body
even when it's covered up,
then why do we not want it
when it isn't covered — what need is there then to embrace what merely covers it? ^8-50

If it's true that we're not actually attached to what's unclean,
then why, when you see someone else's body —
this frame of bones bound together with sinew,
plastered over with a covering of flesh and mud-like substance — do you long to embrace them? ^8-51

You yourself are full of what's unclean;
you make constant use of that very body.
And yet toward another sack of what's unclean,
you crave it and desire it as well. ^8-52

"I'm fond of this flesh," you say,
wanting to touch it and look at it.
But how can you desire this flesh,
which by its very nature has no mind of its own and feels nothing? ^8-53

The mind that experiences desire
cannot itself be touched or seen;
and that which can be touched and seen has no awareness —
so what is the point of this pointless embrace? ^8-54

It's not so strange that we fail to recognize
the unclean nature of someone else's body;
what is truly strange is that we fail to recognize
that our own body is unclean in exactly the same way. ^8-55

Why, with a mind attached to what is unclean,
do we abandon a young lotus flower,
opened wide by the sun's rays in a cloudless sky,
and instead delight in a sack filled with filth? ^8-56

If you don't even want to touch
ground that has been soiled with something unclean,
then how can you want to touch
the very body from which that came? ^8-57

If it's true you're not attached to what's unclean,
then why, since this body grew
from an unclean field, planted there
by that same seed, do you still want to embrace another such body? ^8-58

You have no desire even for a small worm
that is born from what is unclean,
yet you desire a body that is by nature filled
with what is unclean, and itself born from what is unclean. ^8-59

Not only do you feel no disgust
toward your own uncleanliness,
but, craving one sack of filth for another,
you desire other unclean bodies as well. ^8-60

Even pleasant things — camphor and the like,
cooked rice, or vegetables —
once put in the mouth and then spat back out
make even the ground unclean and repulsive. ^8-61

If something so plainly unclean as this
still leaves you in doubt even when it's right before your eyes,
then go and look at other unclean bodies too,
the ones cast away in charnel grounds. ^8-62

Knowing that when the skin is peeled away
great fear and horror will arise,
how is it that toward that very same thing
you continue to feel drawn and delighted? ^8-63

That fragrance applied to the body
is sandalwood or something else — it isn't the body's own scent.
So why would you feel desire for one thing
because of a fragrance that actually belongs to something else entirely? ^8-64

If it would be good not to feel desire for this body
because of its own natural bad odor,
then why do those with no purpose in the world
go on applying sweet perfumes to it? ^8-65

But if it's really the sandalwood's pleasant fragrance,
what does that have to do with the body itself?
So why would you feel desire for one thing
because of a fragrance that actually belongs to something else entirely? ^8-66

If, with its long hair and nails,
its yellowed, foul-smelling teeth,
and its odor like mud,
this body's true nature is something naked and frightening — ^8-67

then why go to such effort
to groom and adorn it, like polishing a weapon
that will only harm you?
The whole world is thrown into confusion by this insane effort born of delusion about the self. ^8-68

Having seen a mere skeleton,
we feel disillusioned in a charnel ground.
So how can we delight in a town, this so-called
"charnel ground" filled with moving skeletons? ^8-69

Moreover, something so unclean as this
cannot be obtained without a price;
in the effort to acquire it, we exhaust ourselves
and suffer harm, even rebirth in hell and the like. ^8-70

As a child one cannot yet accumulate wealth;
so what happiness is there when one reaches one's prime?
And if a lifetime is spent gathering riches,
what is the use of desire once one has grown old? ^8-71

Some poor souls ruled by desire,
utterly exhausted by a full day's toil,
come home, their bodies worn out,
and sleep like corpses. ^8-72

Some suffer the hardships of travel abroad
and the misery of being separated for a long time from home;
though they long to see their wives and children,
for years on end they never get to see them. ^8-73

Deluded, thinking it will bring them benefit,
some sell themselves for the sake of wealth,
yet without ever attaining what they sought,
they're driven along, pointlessly, by the winds of others' demands. ^8-74

Some sell their very own bodies
and end up, powerless, worked by others;
their wives may give birth
right there in the wilderness, beneath a tree, with no shelter at all. ^8-75

Fools deceived by desire, thinking
"I must make a living to survive,"
go off to war risking the loss of their very lives,
and become slaves merely for the sake of profit. ^8-76

Some driven by desire have their bodies cut apart;
some are impaled on stakes;
some are pierced through with spears;
and some, we see, are even burned alive. ^8-77

Understand that wealth brings endless trouble —
in accumulating it, guarding it, and losing it, there is nothing but torment.
Those whose minds are distracted by attachment to wealth
have no opportunity to be free from the suffering of cyclic existence. ^8-78

For those ruled by desire, the drawbacks are many
and the rewards are small —
like a beast pulling a cart
that gets only a mouthful of grass to eat. ^8-79

And even that small reward is not hard for beasts of burden to get,
yet for the sake of it, those tormented by their driving desires
destroy this precious human life,
so richly endowed with freedom and opportunity, so hard to obtain. ^8-80

Desires inevitably lead to ruin
and cause one to fall into hell and other states of suffering,
and even apart from that, they bring, all the time,
nothing but the exhausting hardship of pursuing them. ^8-81

If, with just a fraction — one ten-millionth —
of that same effort, one could achieve full buddhahood,
then compared to a bodhisattva's path,
those ruled by desire endure far greater suffering, and yet attain no awakening at all. ^8-82

If we consider the suffering of hell and the other lower realms,
then compared to what is caused by our desires,
not even weapons, poison, fire,
a precipice, or enemies can compare. ^8-83

Feeling this kind of disillusionment with desire,
I should generate delight in solitude instead,
in tranquil forests
free of conflict and free of the mind's afflictions, ^8-84

in a spacious dwelling on a flat stone slab,
cooled by sandalwood and moonlight, pleasant and delightful,
where the silent forest breeze stirs gently,
and I can walk about, my mind set on benefiting others. ^8-85

Staying as long as I wish
in an empty dwelling, beneath a tree, or in a cave,
free from the suffering of guarding and clinging to possessions,
I can move about freely, without a backward glance. ^8-86

Living independently, without attachment,
unconnected to anyone or anything,
content and living happily —
even a great ruler would find this hard to attain. ^8-87

Having reflected in this way and others
on the good qualities of solitude,
I should thoroughly calm all conceptual thinking
and cultivate the awakening mind. ^8-88

First of all, I should make effort
to cultivate the equality of self and others:
since happiness and suffering are the same for everyone,
I should protect all beings just as I protect myself. ^8-89

Just as the hand and other limbs,
though many and varied, are all protected as one single body,
so too all beings, different as they are, in their happiness and suffering,
all equally wish for happiness, just as I do. ^8-90

Even though this suffering of mine
does not cause harm to another person's body,
that suffering, too, is unbearable to that person
simply because they cling to it as their own self. ^8-91

Likewise, another's suffering
does not fall directly upon me,
yet that suffering, too, is hard for that person to bear
simply because they cling to it as their own. ^8-92

Therefore I should dispel others' suffering,
because it is suffering, just like my own;
and I should benefit others,
because they are sentient beings, just as I am a being with a body. ^8-93

When both myself and others
equally wish for happiness,
what is so special about me
that I should strive for my happiness alone? ^8-94

When both myself and others
equally do not wish for suffering,
what is so special about me
that I protect myself but not others? ^8-95

If we say we don't protect others from their suffering
because it doesn't harm us,
then why do we protect ourselves
against future suffering, which likewise causes no harm right now? ^8-96

The thought "I am the one who will experience it"
is itself a mistaken way of thinking,
since it is one person who dies
and an entirely different person who is reborn. ^8-97

If each person's own suffering
must be guarded against only by that very person,
then the suffering of the foot is not the hand's concern —
so why should the hand protect the foot at all? ^8-98

Even if this reasoning seems not entirely valid here,
being based on grasping at a self,
whatever is unreasonable for both self and other alike
should be rejected as far as one is able. ^8-99

What we call a "mental continuum" and an "aggregation"
are as unreal as a rosary or an army —
there is no independent owner of suffering,
so whose suffering, then, would it really be? ^8-100

Since suffering has no owner,
there is no fundamental distinction between one person's suffering and another's;
it should simply be dispelled because it is suffering —
what use is there in insisting on some fixed boundary around it? ^8-101

If someone asks, "Why should everyone's suffering
be prevented?" — there is no reason to even debate this;
if it is to be prevented, then all of it should be prevented,
and if not, then this applies to me as well, since I too am simply one being among all beings. ^8-102

If it's asked, "Since compassion brings so much suffering,
why should we go out of our way to cultivate it?" —
consider instead the suffering of all beings:
how could compassion ever bring too much suffering? ^8-103

If the suffering of many
could be ended by the suffering of one,
then a person filled with loving compassion
should bring about that suffering, for both self and others alike. ^8-104

Thus, though Supushpachandra himself knew
the harm the king would inflict on him,
he did not turn away from his own suffering,
because he wished to end the suffering of the many. ^8-105

In this way, those whose minds have grown accustomed
to delighting in calming others' suffering
will plunge even into the depths of the most intense suffering,
like a wild goose diving into a lotus pond. ^8-106

When all sentient beings are finally liberated,
what an ocean of joy that will be —
is that not enough in itself?
What use, then, is there in wanting liberation for oneself alone? ^8-107

Therefore, even though I work for the sake of others,
I should feel no pride or sense of wonder about it;
since I delight solely in benefiting others,
I have no hope for any resulting reward for myself. ^8-108

Therefore, just as I protect myself
even from something as small as an unpleasant word,
I should likewise cultivate that same protective attitude
and that same compassionate mind toward others. ^8-109

Through familiarity, people come to think of
what is really just a drop of another's semen and blood
as their own "self,"
even though it has no inherent existence as such. ^8-110

In just the same way, why should I not
adopt the thought "this is my self"
toward the body of another?
It is not so difficult to place the notion of "self" onto the bodies of others too. ^8-111

Having recognized that I myself am full of faults
while others possess an ocean of good qualities,
I should meditate on completely giving up
my clinging to self, and taking up concern for others instead. ^8-112

Just as we accept
that the hand and other limbs are parts of the body,
why should we not, in just the same way,
accept that all beings are like limbs of one single living whole? ^8-113

Just as, through familiarity,
a sense of "self" arose regarding this body, which has no true self,
why, in the same way, through familiarity,
should a sense of "self" not arise regarding other sentient beings too? ^8-114

If I think in this way, then even
when I act for the benefit of others,
there will be no arrogance or self-satisfaction —
just as when I feed myself,
I don't expect anything in return. ^8-115

Therefore, just as I protect myself
even from something as small as an unpleasant word,
I should cultivate that same protective attitude
and that same compassionate mind toward all beings. ^8-116

This is why the Protector Avalokiteshvara,
out of great compassion,
blessed even his own name
in order to dispel the fears of beings caught in the cycle of rebirth. ^8-117

I must not turn back from this difficult task,
for through the power of familiarity,
even something whose very name once caused fear
becomes something we cannot be happy without. ^8-118

Whoever wishes to swiftly protect
both themselves and others
should practice this supreme secret:
exchanging self for other. ^8-119

Because of our attachment to this body of ours,
even a small danger causes great fear to arise.
Who would not hate this body as an enemy,
this very body that gives rise to such fear? ^8-120

Wishing to cure the body's ailments —
hunger, thirst, and other such illnesses —
we kill birds, fish, and deer,
and lie in wait for them along their paths. ^8-121

For the sake of profit and position,
some even go so far as to kill their own father and mother,
or steal what belongs to the Three Jewels,
and as a result will burn in the deepest hell. ^8-122

What wise person, then, would still
desire, protect, and honor this body?
Who would not instead regard it
as an enemy, and look on it with contempt? ^8-123

"If I give this away, what will I have to enjoy?" —
this self-centered thought is the way of a hungry ghost.
"If I use this myself, what will I have left to give?" —
this concern for others is the way of the gods. ^8-124

If, for my own sake, I cause harm to others,
I will be tormented in hell and the other lower realms.
But if, for the sake of others, I bring harm upon myself,
I will attain every kind of excellence. ^8-125

Wanting to elevate oneself above others
leads to a bad rebirth and to foolishness.
Directing that same wish toward others instead
leads to a fortunate rebirth and to honor. ^8-126

If I put others to work for my own sake,
I will experience servitude and the like myself.
But if I put myself to work for the sake of others,
I will experience mastery and high position. ^8-127

Whatever happiness there is in the world
all comes from wishing for the happiness of others.
Whatever suffering there is in the world
all comes from wishing for happiness for oneself alone. ^8-128

What need is there to say more?
Just look at the difference between
the immature person who acts only for their own benefit,
and the Sage who acts for the benefit of others. ^8-129

If I do not genuinely exchange
my own happiness for the sufferings of others,
I will not achieve buddhahood,
and even within cyclic existence there will be no real happiness for me either. ^8-130

Setting aside concerns about future lives,
even the ordinary purposes of this present life will not be accomplished
by a master who does not reward his servants for their labor
or by a servant who does not do his master's work. ^8-131

Casting aside the complete happiness
that comes from benefiting others, whether seen or unseen,
and instead causing suffering to others out of confusion,
one ends up experiencing unbearable suffering oneself. ^8-132

Whatever violence exists in this world,
and whatever fear and suffering there is,
all of it arises from clinging to a self —
so what use is this great demon of self-clinging to me? ^8-133

If I do not give up this clinging to self entirely,
I will not be able to give up suffering,
just as a person who does not let go of fire
cannot avoid being burned. ^8-134

Therefore, in order to pacify harm done to myself
and to pacify the suffering of others,
I should give myself over to others
and cherish others just as I cherish myself. ^8-135

Mind, you must clearly understand:
"I now belong to others."
From now on, you must think of nothing else
except the welfare of all sentient beings. ^8-136

It is not right to use these eyes and other faculties,
which belong to others now,
for my own selfish purposes;
nor is it right to use them against the very beings to whom they belong. ^8-137

Therefore I should treat sentient beings as most important,
and whatever I see in this body of mine
that could be of use,
I should take from it and use it to benefit others. ^8-138

Having taken those beneath me and others as if they were myself,
and taken my own self as if it were another,
with a mind free from such deluded discriminating thought,
I should now cultivate envy, rivalry, and pride — but toward myself, on behalf of others. ^8-139

"This one is honored, but I am not;
I have not obtained what this person has.
This one is praised, while I am criticized;
this one is happy, while I am in misery." ^8-140

"I am the one who does all the work,
while this one simply rests in comfort.
This one is regarded as great in the world,
while I am said to have no good qualities at all." ^8-141

"What is the use of someone without good qualities?
I myself am the one endowed with every good quality.
There are those inferior to me,
and there are also those superior to me." ^8-142

"My moral discipline, my views, and the rest
have declined only because of the power of the mind's afflictions, not through any real choice of mine.
I need to be healed by whatever means possible,
and I will willingly accept even suffering for the sake of this." ^8-143

"But if I am not worth curing,
why is contempt being shown toward me?
What use is it to me
that this other person has good qualities?" ^8-144

"This one, standing at the very brink of the wretched lower realms,
has no compassion at all for other beings,
yet on the outside boasts of good qualities
and wants to rival the wise." ^8-145

Looking at those equal to myself,
in order to make myself superior,
I should be certain to secure
my own wealth and honor, even through open conflict, if need be. ^8-146

"However I can, I should make my own good qualities
known throughout the whole world,
while making sure that no one
ever hears of this other person's good qualities at all." ^8-147

"My own faults should be well hidden,
and I should be the one who receives worship, not this other person.
I should be the one who obtains fine possessions
and receives honor, not this other person." ^8-148

"With great satisfaction, I will watch for a long time
as this person suffers misfortune;
I will make them
the laughingstock and object of scorn for everyone." ^8-149

Now, this same self-cherishing mind of mine,
they say, is in competition with me —
so let us see: does it really equal me
in learning, wisdom, appearance, family status, or wealth? ^8-150

Hearing my own good qualities
proclaimed in this way to everyone,
I should feel my hair stand on end with joy
and fully enjoy that happiness. ^8-151

But if this self-cherishing mind has some wealth,
and it is working for my sake instead,
then I should give it just enough to survive,
and take the rest away by force for myself. ^8-152

I should deprive this self-cherishing mind of its happiness
and continually inflict harm upon it instead;
for it is this very mind that, hundreds upon hundreds of times,
has caused me harm throughout the whole of cyclic existence. ^8-153

Mind, though you wanted to work only for your own benefit
through countless eons that have already passed,
with all of that immense exertion
you have accomplished nothing but suffering for yourself. ^8-154

Therefore, without any doubt, apply yourself now
wholeheartedly to the benefit of others;
the Sage's words are never deceptive —
you will come to see the good results of this in time. ^8-155

If you, mind, had practiced this way of acting
in earlier times,
this present state of being — lacking the perfect bliss
of buddhahood — would never have come to be. ^8-156

Therefore, just as you once grasped and claimed as "I"
what was really nothing more than
a drop of another's semen and blood,
in that same way, you must now train yourself to do so with others as well. ^8-157

Having carefully investigated on behalf of others,
whatever you see in this body of mine
that could be of use,
you must take it from there and use it to benefit others. ^8-158

"I am happy, while others are unhappy;
I am superior, while others are lowly;
I am the one who helps others, while they do nothing for me" —
why do you not feel envy toward yourself for thinking this way? ^8-159

I should give up my own happiness
and take on the sufferings of others instead.
Whenever I think, "Why should I do this?" —
I should examine and confront this very fault in myself. ^8-160

Whatever wrongdoing others commit,
I should turn into my own fault instead;
and whatever small wrongdoing I myself commit,
I should openly confess it before many people. ^8-161

I should overshadow my own reputation
by praising the reputation of others instead;
like the very lowest of servants,
I should put myself to work for the sake of everyone's needs. ^8-162

This body, by its very nature full of faults,
should not be praised for whatever incidental good qualities it happens to have;
I should make sure that no one
ever comes to know of any good quality it has at all. ^8-163

In short, whatever harm
you, mind, have done to others for your own sake,
may all of that harm instead
fall upon me, for the sake of all sentient beings. ^8-164

I must not let this self-cherishing mind
grow bold and forceful;
instead, like a shy young bride,
it should be restrained, held back by modesty and fear. ^8-165

"You must act like this; you must remain like that;
you must not act in that other way;
in this way you shall be kept under control,
and if you transgress, you will be punished." ^8-166

But if, even after being instructed in this way,
you, mind, still refuse to act accordingly,
then, since you are the source of every fault,
you yourself must be subdued and punished without exception. ^8-167

"The time when you could destroy me
is now long past and gone;
I see you now clearly — where can you possibly go?
I will crush every bit of your arrogance." ^8-168

"Give up now, once and for all, the thought
that you still have some interest of your own to pursue.
I have sold you to others already;
do not grieve over this — simply serve them without complaint." ^8-169

"If, through carelessness,
I fail to hand you over to sentient beings,
then you will certainly be handing me over instead
to the guardians of hell." ^8-170

"In just this way you have handed me over before,
and I have suffered for a long time because of it.
Now, remembering all these past grievances,
I will destroy this self-centered thinking of yours." ^8-171

"If I wish to truly find joy,
I must not seek happiness for myself.
If I wish to truly protect myself,
I must instead always protect others." ^8-172

The more this body
is pampered and indulged,
the more it degenerates
and becomes ever more fragile and demanding. ^8-173

And when it has degenerated in this way,
not even everything in this entire world
would be enough to satisfy its desires —
so who could possibly fulfill them? ^8-174

For one whose desires cannot be fulfilled,
frustration and a degenerate state of mind will arise.
But for one who has no expectations from anyone at all,
their sense of contentment and well-being never comes to an end. ^8-175

Therefore, I should not give this body
any opportunity to increase its desires;
whoever does not grasp at what is merely pleasing to the senses
truly possesses something excellent. ^8-176

In the end this body comes down to nothing but ashes,
and being inert, has to be moved about by something else entirely;
this unclean, unbearable form —
why do I go on grasping it as "self"? ^8-177

Whether it's alive or whether it's dead,
what use is this mechanical contraption to me?
What real difference is there between it and a lump of earth or stone?
Alas, why do I not eliminate this pride of self-clinging? ^8-178

Through pampering and flattering this body,
I have accumulated nothing but pointless suffering.
What use is there in feeling attachment or anger
toward something no more significant than a piece of wood? ^8-179

Whether I look after it carefully like this
or whether it is instead devoured by vultures,
it feels neither attachment nor aversion either way —
so why, then, do I feel such attachment toward it? ^8-180

If I don't even understand
who gets angry at criticism and who is pleased by praise,
then for whose sake, really,
have I exhausted myself with all this effort? ^8-181

If someone says, "Whoever desires this body
is a friend of mine" —
then, since everyone desires their own body just the same,
why should they not be pleased to have me as a friend too? ^8-182

Therefore, without any attachment,
I should give up this body for the benefit of all beings.
Even though it has many faults,
I will keep it as an instrument for accomplishing virtuous deeds. ^8-183

That being so, enough of acting like an immature person —
I should follow in the footsteps of the wise instead,
recalling the teachings on conscientiousness,
and dispel sleepiness and mental dullness. ^8-184

Therefore, in order to remove these obscurations,
I should turn my mind away from wrong paths,
and constantly rest it in meditative equipoise,
focused correctly on its proper object. ^8-185

This is the eighth chapter, "Teaching Meditative Concentration," from *Engaging in the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^8-a

## 9. Chapter 9: The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0

All these practices we have discussed —
generosity, patience, and the rest —
the Sage taught them all for one purpose:
wisdom. So if we want suffering to end,
we must develop wisdom. ^9-1

Everything that exists is understood in two ways: as it appears on the surface, in ordinary experience, and as it truly is, once we see through that surface. These are called the "two truths" — the surface truth (how things conventionally seem) and the ultimate truth (how things actually are). The ultimate is not something the ordinary thinking mind can grasp; whatever the mind takes hold of belongs only to the surface level. ^9-2

Within this surface level, people see the world in two ways: as spiritual practitioners see it, and as ordinary people see it. Here, the view of ordinary people is undermined by the view of practitioners who have trained their minds. ^9-3

And among practitioners themselves, those with sharper insight overturn the views of those before them, level by level — using examples both sides already accept — without needing to examine what result each view is meant to serve. ^9-4

Ordinary people see things and take them to be real just as they appear — not like an illusion. This is exactly where practitioners and ordinary people disagree. ^9-5

Even things we perceive directly, like physical forms, are only established by common convention — not by any reliable, deeper form of knowing. This convention is as mistaken as when something unclean is conventionally called "clean," or similar false but widely accepted labels. ^9-6

The Protector taught that things exist "as they appear" in order to lead ordinary people gradually toward the truth. But someone might object: doesn't this contradict the deeper teaching that all things are momentary, not lasting even an instant — since that teaching, too, is spoken at the level of ordinary convention? ^9-7

There's no fault in what the practitioner sees, even at the conventional level, because compared to ordinary people, the practitioner sees more truly how things actually are. Otherwise — for example, if we followed only what looks true to unexamined perception — even seeing a woman's body as beautiful would have to count as correct, and the practitioner's insight that it is not beautiful would then wrongly be refuted by the ordinary view. ^9-8

Just as one gains merit by honoring a Buddha understood to be like a magical illusion, so too, one might ask: if living beings are like illusions, how do they get reborn after dying? ^9-9

For as long as the causes and conditions needed for an illusion to appear remain gathered together, that illusion continues to occur. Why would a being be any more truly, solidly existent, just because the underlying continuum lasts a long time? ^9-10

Killing an illusory person, or the like, carries no wrongdoing, since that illusion has no mind. But for a being who does have a mind — even if that mind, too, is like an illusion — both merit and wrongdoing do arise. ^9-11

A merely magical illusion has no power to produce a mind, since spells and other such causes cannot create it. But illusions arising from many different kinds of causes and conditions are themselves correspondingly varied — including, in this case, minds. ^9-12

There is no single cause capable of producing every kind of effect, anywhere. So if it were true that ultimately there is only nirvana — cessation — and everything of ordinary experience (samsara) exists merely on the conventional surface, then... ^9-13

...even a Buddha would still be caught within the round of rebirth — so what would be the point of practicing the path to awakening? But as long as the flow of causes and conditions is not cut off, even something illusion-like does not stop appearing. ^9-14

Once the flow of those causes and conditions is finally cut off, then even at the conventional level nothing further arises. And when even the mistaken perception itself no longer exists, by what means could any illusion still be perceived? ^9-15

If, for you, there is no separate perceiving mind observing the illusion, then what exactly is doing the perceiving at that point? And if you say something else exists to do it — some form of that same illusion-like process — then that "something" is simply the mind itself. ^9-16

But if the mind itself is also illusion-like — not solidly real — then who is doing the seeing, and by what means? Indeed, the Protector of the World himself taught that the mind cannot perceive itself, any more than a sword can cut its own blade. ^9-17

Just as a sword's edge cannot cut itself, the ordinary mind cannot know itself directly. But — one might object — doesn't a lamp illuminate its own nature perfectly well, just as it illuminates other things? ^9-18

A lamp does not, in fact, "illuminate itself" in any meaningful sense, because a lamp is never obscured by darkness in the first place — there's nothing there to newly reveal. It's like saying a piece of blue crystal is blue: its blueness doesn't depend on anything else to make it blue. ^9-19

In the same way, we do see some things that depend on something else to be perceived, and some things that don't need anything else — like the crystal, which is blue by its own nature and was never "made" blue by some outside cause. ^9-20

Suppose someone says, "The lamp illuminates" — and knows this through some awareness, which they then express in words. But by what awareness does anyone come to know that "the mind itself is luminous" — clear and self-revealing — such that they could make that same kind of statement about the mind? ^9-21

If, in fact, no one perceives it at all, then whether the mind is said to be "clear" or "unclear" makes no difference — it's as pointless to discuss as speculating about the graceful walk of a barren woman's daughter, someone who by definition doesn't exist. ^9-22

If there were no such thing as the mind being aware of itself, then how could we ever remember a past state of consciousness at all? One might answer: memory arises through a connection with something else that was experienced at the time — the way memory of a poison bite can arise later from an unrelated cause, like a rat's bite reminding one of it. ^9-23

One might say the mind clearly knows itself directly because it exists together with the various other conditions needed for perception. But consider: applying a magic ointment lets one see a hidden treasure vase — yet that does not mean the ointment itself becomes visible. Seeing one thing under certain conditions does not mean the means of seeing also becomes an object seen. ^9-24

We are not here denying ordinary things like seeing, hearing, or knowing as they occur in everyday experience. What we are refuting is the mistaken belief that these things are solid and truly, independently existent — since that belief is the very root cause of suffering. ^9-25

The illusion is not something separate from the mind that creates it, yet it's also not simply identical to the mind, since the mind is said to be permanent while the illusion is not. If it were truly, ultimately something, how could it not be different from the mind? And if you say it isn't different, then it can't really be a distinct, existing "thing" either way. ^9-26

Just as an illusion is not ultimately real even though it appears, the mind that perceives it is, likewise, not ultimately real. If you claim that samsara — the whole cycle of existence — has some truly existing basis, then that basis, being separate from the illusion, would end up being something like empty space: inert and unconnected to anything. ^9-27

How could something that isn't ultimately real (an illusion) rest upon and be produced from something that is ultimately real (a truly existent mind), and thereby become able to function and act? On your view, the mind ends up entirely alone, without any partner or object to depend on. ^9-28

If, at the point of full awakening, the mind is free of anything to grasp onto, then everyone would already be a fully enlightened Buddha at that point. So what good, then, is there in imagining that everything is "just mind" (this refers to the Buddhist philosophical position that all experience is nothing but mind, with no external objects) if that view still leaves things ultimately unresolved? ^9-29

Even if one understands things to be like illusions, how could that alone make disturbing emotions stop? After all, even the maker of a magical illusion may feel desire arising toward the illusory woman he himself created. ^9-30

The reason desire still arises is that the illusion-maker has not yet removed the deep habitual patterns of disturbing emotion regarding the object known. So when he looks at her, his habitual insight into emptiness — the understanding that nothing has any fixed, independent existence — is still too weak to counteract it. ^9-31

By repeatedly training in this insight into emptiness, one gradually gives up the habitual pattern of grasping things as solidly real. And by training in the further insight that ultimately nothing at all exists as a solid "thing," even that habit of grasping onto "emptiness" as a thing is eventually let go of too. ^9-32

When something being examined is found, upon investigation, not to exist at all, then how could its "non-existence" — which depends on there having been a "thing" to negate — stand before the mind as a solid object of its own? ^9-33

When neither existence nor non-existence remains before the mind, since there is no other possible way for the mind to hold onto anything, it becomes completely at peace, with nothing left to grasp. ^9-34

Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-granting tree can fulfill every hope without conscious intention, in the same way, a buddha manifests physical forms in the world purely through the power of the aspirations and needs of those to be helped — without the buddha needing to actively intend it. ^9-35

For example: suppose someone built a shrine to protect against snakebite-poison, dedicated to the garuda bird-deity, and then passed away. Long afterward, even though its builder is gone, that shrine still works to pacify poison and similar harms. ^9-36

In just the same way, a shrine built in accordance with the practices of a bodhisattva's path — dedicated to a buddha — continues to work for the benefit of all beings, even though the bodhisattva who attained buddhahood has since passed into cessation, without any conscious effort needed on his part. ^9-37

One might object: how can making offerings to something without a mind — like a buddha who has passed beyond, without ongoing conscious awareness — produce any result at all? But the scriptures teach that a buddha who continues to dwell in the world and a buddha who has passed into cessation are, in terms of the result their worship produces, exactly the same. ^9-38

Whether one takes this fruitfulness to belong to the conventional level or the ultimate level, either way it is established by the scriptures that a result does follow. It is just like how a buddha, understood in the ultimate sense, is nonetheless said to bring about a fruitful result for those who make offerings. ^9-39

One might argue that liberation comes about through seeing the truths — the basic teachings on suffering and its causes as commonly taught — so what is the use, then, of specifically seeing emptiness, the lack of any fixed, independent existence in all things? Because the scriptures state that without this insight into emptiness, there is no path to awakening at all. ^9-40

One might say: but the scriptures of the Great Vehicle (Mahayana) are not accepted by us as authoritative in the first place — so how can you use them to prove your point? This is answered: since both sides agree these scriptures are valid on some point, it is only regarding the very issue at hand — this particular passage — that you refuse to accept its validity. ^9-41

Whatever grounds you rely on to trust the scriptures you do accept, those same grounds apply equally to the Great Vehicle's scriptures. And if a text counts as true merely because two parties happen to agree upon it, then even the Vedas and other non-Buddhist scriptures would have to be considered true by the same reasoning. ^9-42

One might object that the Great Vehicle's teachings should be rejected because they are disputed. But then the basic scriptures should likewise be discarded because they are disputed too — by non-Buddhist schools regarding the scriptures in general, and by different Buddhist schools disputing each other's scriptures as well. ^9-43

If the very foundation of the teaching depends on someone being a properly ordained monk, then even being a proper monk becomes hard to establish by the same strict standard. For any mind that still has objects to grasp onto, reaching final liberation (nirvana) likewise becomes hard to establish. ^9-44

Someone might say: once disturbing emotions are abandoned, one is liberated right at that very moment. But we still observe the power of past karmic actions operating in such people, even though the emotional afflictions themselves are gone. ^9-45

One might insist: it is certain that craving—the specific craving that leads to further rebirth—no longer exists for such a person. But even if that craving is not an emotional affliction in the usual sense, why would it not still exist in the form of basic confusion, the way ignorance in general persists? ^9-46

Craving arises conditioned by feeling, and such feeling continues to exist even in these persons. And a mind that still has something to grasp onto — some object to focus on — will indeed continue to exist, for some persons at that stage. ^9-47

A mind that lacks the insight into emptiness will cease for a while but then arise again later — just like the mental state that arises in the meditative absorption free of ordinary perception, which is not a final release. Therefore, one must meditate on emptiness itself, not just suppress ordinary thought. ^9-48

If you insist that only what is explicitly stated in the (non-Mahayana) discourses counts as the Buddha's actual teaching, then consider: most of the Great Vehicle's teachings resemble your own accepted discourses closely enough — wouldn't you have to accept them as equally valid, or not? ^9-49

If the fault lies in some single point not found elsewhere, and this makes the whole teaching flawed, then by the same logic, why not say that all your scriptures are equally invalidated, since they resemble the Great Vehicle's teachings in some single point too — the reasoning would have to run both ways. ^9-50

Anything whose depths even a figure as great as Mahakashyapa could not fully fathom — simply because you yourself have not understood it, who are you to say it should be rejected outright? ^9-51

For the sake of beings tormented by confusion and suffering, freeing them from the extremes of both attachment and fear, while they remain within the cycle of existence — this liberating outcome is achieved through realizing emptiness. This is the very fruit that comes from understanding emptiness. ^9-52

So there is no valid ground for objecting to the position of emptiness. Therefore, without any further doubt, one should meditate on emptiness — this understanding that nothing has fixed, independent existence. ^9-53

Emptiness is the direct remedy for the darkness of both the disturbing emotions and the more subtle obstacles to complete knowledge. So for anyone who wishes to swiftly attain omniscience, why would they not meditate on it? ^9-54

Whatever thing gives rise to suffering is naturally something we come to fear. But emptiness is what calms and pacifies suffering itself — so how could fear of emptiness possibly arise? ^9-55

If there really were some solid, independent self that could be harmed, then fear of anything might make sense. But since no such self exists at all, who exactly is there left to feel afraid? ^9-56

Teeth, hair, and nails are not the self. Bones and blood are not the self either. Mucus and phlegm are not the self, nor are pus and lymph. ^9-57

I am not fat, nor sweat. The liver and other internal organs are not me. Other bodily fluids are not me either — I am not excrement or urine. ^9-58

Flesh and skin are not the self. Neither are body heat or breath. None of the bodily cavities are the self in any way, and the six kinds of sensory consciousness are not the self either. ^9-59

If the consciousness that hears sound were permanent, then it would have to be grasping sound at all times, constantly. But if there is no sound present to be known, then what exactly is it aware of, that we could call it "consciousness"? ^9-60

If something with no object of awareness could still be called "consciousness," then even a piece of wood would absurdly have to count as conscious. Therefore, it must be certain: without an object of knowledge actually present, there is no awareness of it either. ^9-61

If that same awareness that hears sound were also what perceives visual form, then why would it not also perceive what is heard at the very same time as seeing? One might object that it doesn't perceive sound at that moment simply because sound isn't present then — but in that case, its "knowing" of sound isn't happening either, at that time. ^9-62

How could something whose very nature is to grasp sound also, by that same nature, grasp visual form? A single thing being both "father" and "son" is only a conventional way of speaking; it isn't literally, ultimately true. ^9-63

In the same way, the three basic qualities that some propose as building blocks of reality — activity/passion, stability, and darkness/inertia — are like father and son only by label, not truly. That which grasps sound is not, by its intrinsic nature, seen to be the same thing as any of these three basic qualities. ^9-64

One might say: like a dancer changing roles and appearances, the same one thing can show up in different forms — meaning what perceives sound and what perceives sight could still be one underlying thing appearing differently. But reply: if it truly appears in a different form, then it is not permanent and unchanging as claimed. And if you say it is the same underlying thing even in its different guises, then this "sameness" is something never encountered before — an entirely new, unjustified claim. ^9-65

If you say the different guises are not ultimately true, then state plainly what its own true, essential nature actually is. If you answer "its nature is simply awareness itself," then it would absurdly follow that everything that has ever been born would have to be one single thing. ^9-66

Things that have deliberate mental activity and things that lack it (like inanimate matter) would then both equally have to be "one," since, on your account, both equally count as existing. But if you say these are all genuinely different from one another, mistakenly appearing similar, then what could possibly be the common underlying basis making them seem alike in the first place? ^9-67

Something without mind cannot be the self either, since it lacks awareness, just like a clay pot. But if you say it has a self because it is associated with a mind, then this "self" would absurdly have to be destroyed the moment it stopped knowing something — an unacceptable consequence. ^9-68

And if the self is said to never change at all, then what would be the point of it engaging in any mental activity whatsoever? If that's so, then this unaware, inactive "self" would be as good as making empty space itself into "the self" — utterly pointless. ^9-69

Someone might object: if there really is no self at all, then the connection between an action (karma) and its result makes no sense. Since the one who acted has already ceased to exist by the time the result ripens, whose action would it even be? ^9-70

We both — you and I — agree that an action and the person who experiences its result are, in an important sense, different from one another in each successive moment, and that there is no single unchanging agent who "does" both. Since this basic point is already accepted by both sides, isn't debating it here rather pointless? ^9-71

We do not maintain that a cause and its later effect can literally be observed together as belonging to one and the same enduring thing. Rather, based on one continuous stream of moments, it is simply said, by convention, that there is "an agent" and "an experiencer" of the results. ^9-72

A past mind or a future mind is not the self, since neither exists right now. If instead you say the mind that has just arisen in this present moment is the self, then once that particular mind has ceased, that "self" too would no longer exist. ^9-73

Just as when a banana-tree trunk is cut apart piece by piece, nothing solid or essential is found inside it, in just the same way, when we carefully search and analyze for a "self," no truly existing self is found there either. ^9-74

One might ask: if there really are no sentient beings at all, then toward whom should compassion be directed? The answer: it is directed toward beings as they are conventionally imagined and accepted, precisely for the sake of achieving the intended goal — even though, examined closely, this notion of "being" is itself a product of ignorance. ^9-75

It is true that, ultimately, there is no independently existing being to whom compassion is directed as a fixed target; nevertheless, compassion is cultivated toward beings as conceived through this basic confusion, for the sake of achieving the goal of pacifying suffering. So one should not try to remove this particular kind of "confusion" — the compassionate way of relating to beings — since it serves this purpose. ^9-76

The pride and arrogance that is the root cause of suffering grows precisely out of confusion about a solid self. If someone objects that this confusion, too, cannot then be removed, the answer is: meditating on selflessness is in fact the very best way to remove it. ^9-77

The body is not the legs or the shins. The thighs and the hips are also not the body. The belly and the back are not the body, and neither are the chest or the shoulders. ^9-78

The ribs and the hands are not the body, nor are the armpits or the forearms. The internal organs are not it either, and the head and neck are not the body. So, in all this, what exactly is "the body"? ^9-79

If you say this "body" exists by being spread out, with each part occupying its own portion across the whole, then the various parts would simply be resting within their own respective portions — but then where would the body itself, as a single whole, actually reside? ^9-80

If you say the whole body — understood as a single self-standing thing — resides within the hands and other parts individually, then there would have to be as many separate "bodies" as there are hands, feet, and other such parts. ^9-81

If there is no body to be found either outside the parts or as some inner essence within them, then how could a body exist merely "in" the hands and other parts? And if there is nothing apart from the hands and the other individual parts, then how could this whole "body" be said to exist at all? ^9-82

Therefore, there really is no body as such; but out of confusion, the mind constructs the notion of "body" based on the hands and other parts — much the way people mistake a stack of stones arranged in a particular shape for a human figure at a distance, due to that particular arrangement. ^9-83

For as long as the necessary conditions remain assembled in a certain way, the body appears like a person there. In just the same way, for as long as the hands and other parts remain present in a certain arrangement, the "body" appears there too, in that arrangement. ^9-84

In just the same way, since a hand, too, is simply a collection of fingers, one might ask what a "hand" itself really is. And since even a finger is a collection of joints, and a joint, too, can be divided further into its own parts... ^9-85

...and a part can likewise be broken down further into tiny particles, and even a particle can be divided according to its different spatial directions or facets, and that directional division, too, would have to lack any further parts of its own — so, like empty space, in the end, no truly indivisible particle can be found to exist at all. ^9-86

So, given all this, what sensible person who has carefully examined the matter would still feel attachment to a physical form that is, in the end, just like something seen in a dream? Since, when carefully analyzed in this way, there is no truly existing body at all, then what exactly is "male" and what exactly is "female," in any solid sense? ^9-87

If suffering truly, inherently exists as such, then why doesn't it interfere with feelings of great joy when they occur? And in states of pleasure, why do things like delicious food and other delights fail to bring joy to someone who is instead absorbed in grief and anguish? ^9-88

One might answer: it's because a stronger feeling of joy overwhelms and masks the suffering, so it isn't actually experienced at that moment. But then, something that is not experienced by the one who would supposedly feel it — how could that even count as "a feeling" at all, in any meaningful sense? ^9-89

One might say suffering still exists, only in a subtle form, and it's the coarser, grosser suffering that has simply been removed or masked by pleasure. But then that supposedly "subtle" remainder is really just a slightly lesser form of pleasure itself, in relation to the greater pleasure — so it belongs to pleasure's own category, not a hidden layer of true suffering. ^9-90

If it's said that suffering simply fails to arise because its opposing condition (pleasure) has arisen instead, then doesn't this show that the whole idea of "feeling" a truly existent suffering or pleasure is really just a matter of strong mental fixation and belief, rather than something inherently, solidly real? ^9-91

Precisely because of this, one should meditate using careful, analytical insight as the direct remedy for this fixation. Such analytical meditation, arising from careful investigation, is what nourishes a practitioner absorbed in meditative concentration — it is, so to speak, their spiritual food. ^9-92

If you claim that a sense faculty and its object meet through some intermediate space between them, then where exactly would this "meeting" actually take place? And if there is no such gap between them at all, so that they are essentially one and the same, then what exactly would be meeting with what? ^9-93

One tiny particle cannot enter into another tiny particle, since there is no room for it to do so — both are understood to be equal in size, with nothing smaller to fit inside. And if a particle cannot enter another, then there is no mixing or merging of them; and if there is no such mixing, then there can be no real "meeting" or contact between them at all. ^9-94

How could it make sense to say that something without any parts at all could still "meet" or make contact with something else? So if you have actually observed a genuine case of two truly partless things meeting in contact, please point it out and demonstrate it. ^9-95

It is simply not tenable for a form of consciousness — which has no physical body or spatial location — to be said to "meet" or make contact with anything. And a collection or aggregate of such things doesn't have any truly existing single object either, for the same reasons already examined earlier regarding a whole made of parts. ^9-96

Given that no true contact exists in this way, then from what could feeling possibly arise? So what would be the point of all this effort we make over sensations — and, in that case, who is really harming whom, and by what means? ^9-97

Since there is no truly existing one who feels, and no truly existing feeling as such either, then having seen clearly this true state of affairs, why would craving not simply cease of its own accord? ^9-98

What we call "seeing" or "touching" arises together with the mind, having the nature of something dreamlike or illusion-like — it does not actually perceive by means of any solid, independently existing "feeling." ^9-99

Since the mind arises and passes before and after each moment, what actually happens is merely a kind of remembering afterward — not a direct, immediate experiencing as it happens. The mind does not experience itself directly at that very moment, and neither can something else experience it for it. ^9-100

There is, in truth, no one who feels at all, and so there is no truly existing feeling as such either. Given this collection of processes that has no fixed self anywhere within it, how could this whole discussion of feeling possibly cause it any harm? ^9-101

The mind does not reside in the sense faculties, nor does it dwell in physical forms and other sense objects, nor in between the two. It is not found inside as some separate inner mind, nor outside, nor is it to be found located anywhere else either. ^9-102

Whatever is not the body, not something separate from it, not mixed together with it, and not found to exist anywhere else on its own — that thing is, in truth, nothing at all. And because of this, the fundamental nature of every sentient being is, in this very sense, already free of suffering — already, in essence, at peace. ^9-103

If awareness already existed before its object of knowledge came to be, then what could it possibly be focused on when it first arises? And if awareness and its object arise together, at exactly the same moment, then, again, what would it be focusing on as it comes into being? ^9-104

If, instead, awareness comes only after its object already exists, then at that point, from what would this awareness itself actually arise? In this way, careful examination shows that the arising of any phenomenon whatsoever cannot ultimately be established or fully understood. ^9-105

One might object: if it's true that, in this way, even the conventional level of reality cannot withstand analysis, then how could there be any "two truths" (conventional and ultimate) at all? And, further, if the conventional truth is itself established only in relation to some other person's conventional understanding, then how could any being ever actually pass beyond suffering into liberation? ^9-106

This is simply a thought occurring in someone else's mind, and it does not amount to that person's own valid conventional understanding of the matter. If later, upon investigation, it is found to hold up, then it becomes established as a conventional truth; if not, then it simply does not count as a conventional truth at all. ^9-107

The mind that does the analyzing and the thing being analyzed are, in fact, mutually dependent upon each other. All careful investigation and analysis that we do is expressed and carried out by relying on what is already commonly accepted and understood in ordinary convention. ^9-108

When some further form of careful analytical insight is used to examine the analytical insight that came before it, then that further analysis, too, needs to be examined by yet another act of analysis — resulting in an endless regress with no final resting point. ^9-109

Once whatever was originally meant to be analyzed has, in fact, already been thoroughly analyzed and understood, there remains no further basis on which the analyzing mind itself could continue to operate. And since it then has nothing left to rest upon, it naturally ceases to arise further — and this very cessation is itself referred to as full liberation, nirvana. ^9-110

Determining, between these two positions, which one is ultimately correct is an extremely difficult matter to settle. If you claim that the true nature of things is established through the power of a valid, knowing mind, then what, in turn, does that very mind's own existence rest upon and depend on? ^9-111

If, on the other hand, you say that the validity of the knowing mind is established through the object it knows, then what does the actual existence of that object, in turn, rest upon and depend on? And if you then say that mind and object depend on each other mutually for their existence, then, in fact, both of them end up having no independent existence of their own at all. ^9-112

If there were no son, then there could be no father either — so from what, then, would the "son" himself have arisen in the first place? In just the same way, without a son, there is no father; and, likewise, without either one, neither of the two — father or son — can be said to truly exist. ^9-113

A sprout arises from a seed, and it is precisely through that same sprout that we come to understand and infer the prior existence of that seed. In just the same way, since our knowledge of an object arises from that object itself, why should we not, in the same way, be able to infer that this object truly exists, based on our knowledge of it? ^9-114

If, instead, one comes to know — through some awareness distinct from the sprout itself — that a seed must have existed causing it, then, likewise, by what means could we ever come to know, in a similarly independent way, that the very awareness which knows an object actually, truly exists? Since knowledge of the object is precisely what we were trying to establish in the first place, we cannot then turn around and use that very awareness to prove its own true existence. ^9-115

For the time being, ordinary people directly perceive, through everyday experience, all sorts of causes at work in the world — for instance, they see that the various different types of lotus stalks arise due to differences among their respective causes. ^9-116

One might ask: what, in turn, produces this difference among the various causes themselves? The answer given is: it comes from a prior difference among still earlier causes. And why is a cause able to produce its particular effect at all? Because of the inherent power belonging to that still earlier cause. ^9-117

Some claim that a creator God (Ishvara) is the ultimate cause of the world of living beings. In that case, please first explain clearly just what, exactly, this "Ishvara" actually is. If you say he is simply identical with the basic elements of matter (earth, water, fire, and so on), then that may be so — but then why go to all this trouble merely over a name, when we already have perfectly good names for those elements? ^9-118

But in any case, the elements — earth and the rest — are numerous, not a single unified thing; they are impermanent, not unchanging; they have no inherent movement or agency of their own; they are not divine in any special sense; they are things to be stepped upon and used; and they are impure. For all these reasons, they simply cannot be what is meant by this all-powerful, divine "Ishvara." ^9-119

Ishvara cannot be identified with empty space either, since space has no inherent activity of its own; nor can he be the self, since the existence of a permanent self has already been refuted earlier in this chapter. And if he is said to be an inconceivable, incomprehensible agent beyond ordinary logic, then what use is there in even discussing or describing such an entirely inconceivable, undefinable "creator" at all? ^9-120

What exactly is it that this creator supposedly wishes to bring into being? Is it a self? Is it the physical elements like earth? Or is his own essential divine nature itself supposedly not permanent and unchanging after all? And what about the fact that consciousness plainly arises dependent on its object of knowledge, not from some independent divine will? ^9-121

And what about pleasure and pain, which everyone agrees arise from each person's own past karmic actions, going back beginninglessly? Please explain clearly, then, what exactly this creator god is supposed to have brought into being, given all this. And if a cause is said to have no ultimate beginning point at all, then how could its effect possibly be said to have any true, original starting point either? ^9-122

Why would such a creator not simply be creating constantly, at all times, without pause? After all, on this view, he is said not to depend on anything else at all to act — he is entirely independent and self-sufficient. And if there is nothing else at all that was not already created by him beforehand, then what could this creator possibly be depending on now, in order to newly create anything at all? ^9-123

If you say that Ishvara's act of creating does, in fact, depend on some prior gathering of the right conditions coming together, then it is really this very gathering of conditions itself that should properly be called the true cause — not Ishvara. For if the necessary conditions have already come together, he has no actual power or choice left to refrain from creating; and if those conditions are absent, then, likewise, he has no power at all to create anything, regardless of his supposed will. ^9-124

And if Ishvara is said to act and create even against his own will and intention, then this would absurdly mean that he is really acting under the control and power of something else entirely, not acting as a truly independent, sovereign creator. And if he does act willingly, in line with his own intentions, then his action would still simply be governed by and dependent on that very intention or wish of his — so how could he still count, in that case, as being a fully independent, all-powerful sovereign creator at all? ^9-125

Those who claim that indivisible, permanent atoms are the true underlying cause of the world have already had their position refuted earlier in this chapter (in the analysis of partless particles). And the Samkhya school, meanwheel, claims that a permanent, primal "Nature" (prakriti, sometimes called "the Principal") is the true cause underlying the entire world of living beings. ^9-126

They describe this primal "Nature" as consisting of a perfect equilibrium among three fundamental qualities: activity/passion (called "vigor" here), inertia/stability (called "particles"), and darkness/dullness. And it is this state of perfect balance among the three that they call the "Principal" or primal Nature, from which the whole unequal, differentiated world of living beings is then said to emerge. ^9-127

For a single, unified thing to simultaneously have three completely distinct natures is simply not logically coherent; therefore, this so-called unified "Principal" or Nature does not truly exist as claimed. In just the same way, the three qualities themselves cannot truly exist either, since each of the three would, in turn, also have to be broken down into three further distinct sub-qualities of its own, and so on endlessly. ^9-128

If these three basic qualities themselves don't truly exist, then the real existence of sound, and other sense objects derived from them, becomes an exceedingly remote and doubtful possibility indeed. And it would likewise be impossible for feelings such as pleasure to exist within something mindless, like a piece of clothing or cloth, contrary to what this theory would require. ^9-129

If you claim that ordinary things simply have the very nature of their underlying cause (that is, that cloth is essentially made of "pleasure" and the other qualities, since those qualities are its cause), then haven't we already, in effect, already examined and refuted the real existence of such "things" earlier in this analysis? And in your view, the true cause of feelings like pleasure would then have to be pieces of cloth and so on — yet cloth simply does not actually arise or come forth from feelings of pleasure and the rest, as a matter of plain observation. ^9-130

Rather, it is precisely from things like cloth that feelings of pleasure and the rest are said to arise, according to your own account. But because pleasure does not exist without cloth and similar physical things being present (on your own view of causation), true, independent pleasure and so forth would then have no real existence of their own. And feelings such as pleasure could never, under this view, be found to exist as something permanent and unchanging, at any time at all. ^9-131

If pleasure and similar feelings truly exist in a clear, "manifest" form at all times, as claimed, then why is such a clearly manifest pleasure not always directly experienced and perceived as such? And if you say that, in such cases, it has simply become extremely subtle rather than absent, then how, in that case, could it still meaningfully be called both "coarse" and "subtle," as two genuinely different states of the very same one thing? ^9-132

Since the coarse form is said to be given up, and only the subtle form remains afterward, this coarse and subtle nature of pleasure clearly show that it is not, in fact, a permanent and unchanging thing at all, as this school otherwise claims. So, in the very same way, why not accept that absolutely all things whatsoever are similarly impermanent, rather than making a special exception for the underlying "Nature"? ^9-133

If the "coarse" state is really nothing other than the very same pleasure, only appearing more manifestly, then this manifest form of pleasure itself must then also be impermanent and constantly changing, contrary to what is claimed. And if you insist that something which never previously existed simply cannot newly arise at all — as this school assumes regarding effects supposedly "pre-existing" within their cause — then... ^9-134

...you would still, whether you like it or not, have to accept that this "manifest" pleasure somehow arises even where it was previously entirely non-manifest and unclear. And if you insist that the effect must already fully exist, in some hidden way, within its cause even before it appears, then this would absurdly mean that someone eating plain rice food would, in effect, also be eating the very excrement that the rice will later, after digestion, become. ^9-135

By the same twisted logic, one should, with the very same money used to buy ordinary cotton cloth, also be able to purchase, and then wear, the raw cotton-seeds from which that cloth cotton originally grew — since, on this view, the cloth is already fully and simply present within the seed. If you say that ordinary confused people in the world simply fail to perceive this hidden identity between cause and effect due to their own confusion, then reply: those with genuine wisdom and insight, at least, should be able to clearly perceive and establish this same claimed identity — yet they do not. ^9-136

That very worldly perception which the Samkhya wants to invoke here — since it does, after all, actually exist as ordinary experience among people — why then does it not perceive things this way, in line with your theory, if your theory is correct? And if you say that worldly perception in general isn't ultimately a fully reliable means of valid knowledge on such matters anyway, then, by that same reasoning, that same worldly perception's apparent, direct seeing of "manifest" things in the world can't be treated as reliably true either, on your own account. ^9-137

And if you say that a normally valid means of knowledge (like direct perception) is not, in fact, valid or reliable in this particular case, then wouldn't whatever such perception measures or apprehends have to be considered false and unreliable as well, quite generally? And in that very same way, then, meditating on emptiness itself — the lack of any fixed, independent existence in things — would, by your own reasoning, likewise turn out to be simply invalid and untenable, since it too relies on some form of valid cognition. ^9-138

Without first having some genuine conceptual contact with the thing being conceptually posited or imagined, one cannot, in turn, genuinely grasp or understand the absence, or non-existence, of that same thing either. Therefore, whatever thing is itself merely false or illusion-like to begin with, its negation — its clearly recognized non-existence — must likewise, quite obviously, also be understood only in a similarly conventional, non-ultimate way. ^9-139

Therefore, in a dream, when a son has died, the thought that arises afterward, "he does not exist," is itself precisely what counteracts and dispels the earlier, mistaken thought that "he does exist" — yet, notably, that later corrective thought too is, in the end, just as false and merely conventional as the first one was. ^9-140

Therefore, having examined and analyzed things carefully in this way, we find that nothing anywhere truly exists without some cause; and nothing exists as truly residing within its various conditions either, whether those conditions are taken separately, one by one, or all gathered together as a whole. ^9-141

Nothing has truly, ultimately come here from somewhere else; nothing truly, ultimately remains fixed in place; and nothing truly, ultimately goes anywhere else either. So what real, meaningful difference is there, then, between what confused people take to be solidly real and true, and a mere magical illusion? ^9-142

Whatever is conjured up by a magician's illusion, and whatever else is similarly produced through the ordinary workings of various causes and conditions — in either case, one should carefully investigate and ask: from where, exactly, does this thing come, and to where, exactly, does it go? ^9-143

Whatever is seen only when its immediate causes and conditions happen to be present nearby, and which is not seen at all in their absence — such a thing closely resembles nothing more than an artificially constructed reflected image in a mirror. So how, then, could such a thing possibly be said to have any true, solid, independent existence of its own? ^9-144

For something that has, in fact, already truly come to exist as a solid, independent thing, what real further need would there be for some cause to bring it into being? And, on the other hand, for something that, in truth, simply and utterly does not exist at all, what possible need or use could a cause serve for such a non-existent thing either? ^9-145

Even hundreds of millions of causes acting together could never transform something that has no real, independent existence in the first place into something that does. For how, indeed, could something that is presently in a state of pure non-existence become a real, existing thing at all? And, similarly, what other different, real thing could it possibly change into instead? ^9-146

If, at the time something does not yet exist, there is no possibility whatsoever of a real, existing thing being present, then at what point, exactly, could such a thing ever actually come to newly exist at all? Moreover, as long as a truly existing thing has not yet actually come to be born or arisen, its prior state of simple non-existence could never actually be said to depart or cease either. ^9-147

And if this state of simple non-existence never actually departs or ceases at any point, then there would never be any real opportunity or occasion for a truly existing thing to come into being at all. Moreover, a truly existing thing could never, in that case, become nonexistent either — since that would absurdly require the very same single thing to simultaneously possess two entirely different, contradictory natures at once. ^9-148

Therefore, in this way, there is, in truth, no real ceasing or ending of anything, nor is there any truly, independently existing thing to begin with, either. And so, all these countless beings wandering through cyclic existence are, in truth, from the very start, never truly born, and never truly ceasing to exist, in any ultimate sense. ^9-149

This wandering existence of beings is just like a dream: when carefully examined and analyzed, it resembles nothing more solid than the pith of a banana tree trunk, which has no solid core at its center at all. And, seen in this ultimate light, there is truly no real, essential difference at all between someone who has passed beyond suffering into liberation (nirvana) and someone who has not yet done so. ^9-150

Given that all things are, in this way, ultimately empty of any fixed, independent existence, what would there really be left to gain, and what would there be left to lose? Who is there, in truth, to be honored and respected by anyone, and who is there, likewise, to be scorned or looked down upon by anyone else? ^9-151

From where, in truth, would pleasure or suffering ever really come? What is there, ultimately, to be disliked, and what is there to be enjoyed and delighted in? And if one carefully searches for the ultimate, true nature of all this, one must ask: who, in truth, is doing the craving, and for what object, exactly, is that craving even directed toward? ^9-152

If one examines and analyzes carefully, this whole world of apparently living beings — who, in truth, within it, is the one who is actually dying? Who is it, in truth, that will yet come to be born in the future, and who is it, in truth, that has already been born in the past? And who, in truth, among all of them, is really one's own close relative or dear friend, in any solid, ultimate sense? ^9-153

May all of us who share this understanding come to fully grasp and hold everything as being, in truth, just like empty space itself — without any solid, independent existence anywhere. Yet those who, out of basic ignorance, still wish only for their own personal happiness, driven only by the causes and conditions that lead to conflict and to fleeting excitement and pleasure... ^9-154

...become extremely agitated and disturbed on one hand, and overly excited and joyful on the other. They experience grief, exhausting struggle, and constant disputes and arguments; and they injure, wound, and cut each other, sustaining their lives with great difficulty through repeated harmful and unwholesome actions of many kinds. ^9-155

Again and again, they arrive at more fortunate states of rebirth, and there they repeatedly enjoy various forms of worldly happiness and pleasure. But then, after dying from those fortunate states, they fall once more into the lower, unfortunate realms, experiencing forms of suffering that are prolonged and utterly unbearable there. ^9-156

Within this cycle of rebirth (samsara), there are many perilous pitfalls and downfalls of many kinds; and the true nature of things there is not at all as it superficially appears to ordinary confused perception. And since even different worldly viewpoints there directly contradict and conflict with one another, there simply is no single, fixed "way things truly are" that could be found to hold consistently for that confused, ordinary way of seeing things, within cyclic existence. ^9-157

And within this cyclic existence, there is a vast, virtually incomparable and unbearable ocean of suffering, one that goes far beyond any ordinary limit or measure. And within that same existence, beings generally possess only rather weak spiritual strength and capacity to cope with it, and their own individual lifespans there, too, tend to be extremely short and fleeting. ^9-158

And within that same existence, beings must endlessly struggle simply to preserve their health and avoid illness; they experience exhausting labor and hunger; they experience sleep, and various forms of harm and injury from others; and, moreover, they waste much of their time and energy in pointless association and involvement with foolish and immature people. ^9-159

Precious human life, meanwhile, passes by swiftly and is largely squandered for no truly meaningful purpose at all; and genuine analytical wisdom and insight, in the midst of all this, is exceedingly rare and difficult to attain or develop. And within that ordinary existence, moreover, ingrained habitual patterns of distraction and mental scattering are, likewise, extremely difficult to reverse or overcome by any easy means. ^9-160

And within that same cyclic existence, evil, obstructive forces work diligently and actively to cause beings to fall down into the greater, more severe lower realms of suffering and misfortune. And there, within that existence, wrong and misleading spiritual paths of many kinds abound, and it becomes correspondingly extremely difficult for beings to overcome their own persistent doubts and uncertainty about the true path. ^9-161

Moreover, once lost, the precious freedoms and advantages of a favorable human existence are, again, exceedingly difficult and rare to find and regain afterward; and it is likewise exceedingly rare and difficult for a fully awakened buddha to ever actually appear and arise in the world at all. The overwhelming, powerful flood-like force of one's own disturbing emotions and afflictions is, similarly, extremely difficult to fully overcome and abandon. Oh, how truly tragic it is that beings must continue, again and again, to endure such endless, repeated suffering throughout this cycle! ^9-162

Thus, even though beings are, in truth, undergoing such extremely severe and relentless suffering all the time, they still remain unable to perceive clearly their own actual suffering for what it truly is. For these beings who remain caught helplessly within this raging flood-like torrent of suffering — alas, how truly sad and lamentable their whole situation genuinely is! ^9-163

It is just as if someone were to repeatedly bathe themselves in cold water again and again, and then, right afterward, also repeatedly throw themselves into a blazing fire again and again, without any apparent concern. In exactly the same distorted way, even while beings remain caught in the midst of such extremely severe and relentless suffering, they nevertheless still continue to fondly imagine and believe that they themselves are, in fact, perfectly happy and comfortable all the while. ^9-164

Given that beings act and behave in exactly this deluded way, living as though they were somehow entirely exempt from old age, sickness, and death, they thereby effectively bring about their own metaphorical execution beforehand, through such heedless conduct — and, as a direct result, a truly unbearable fall into the lower, unfortunate realms inevitably awaits them afterward. ^9-165

Given that beings are, in this way, constantly tormented and burned by the raging fire of such suffering, when, then, will I myself finally be able to bring about genuine relief and peace for them — through the beneficial, cooling rain-cloud of merit, born from wholesome and virtuous actions, bringing about a full abundance of true and lasting happiness for them all? ^9-166

When will I, then, finally be able to gather and accumulate a vast and profound store of merit, respectfully and diligently, all while maintaining a clear and correct understanding that ultimately nothing whatsoever is truly, independently existent to be grasped onto or fixed upon? And, for the sake of all those beings who continue to suffer greatly precisely because they still cling to such fixed, solid notions of existence, may I then be able to reveal and teach to them this same profound understanding of emptiness — this liberating truth that nothing has any fixed, independent existence at all. ^9-167

This concludes Chapter Nine, "The Perfection of Wisdom," from *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^9-a

## 10. Chapter 10: Dedication ^10-0

Through the goodness I have gained
by writing this book on entering the way of the awakening life,
may all beings everywhere
enter that same way of awakening. ^10-1

May all those in every direction
who suffer in body or in mind
gain, through my merit,
oceans of joy and happiness. ^10-2

For as long as they wander in this cycle of birth and death,
may their happiness never fade;
may the beings of the world find
the bodhisattvas' unbroken, ever-flowing joy. ^10-3

However many hell-realms there are
throughout the universe,
may the beings trapped in bodies there
find joy in the bliss of a pure land instead. ^10-4

May those who suffer from cold find warmth;
may the boundless rivers of water
flowing from the great clouds of the bodhisattvas
cool those who suffer from unbearable heat. ^10-5

May the forests of sword-like leaves
become instead thick groves of sandalwood;
may the trees whose spikes tear the flesh
grow instead as wish-fulfilling trees. ^10-6

May those hellish regions become lovely,
graced with lakes covered in lotuses,
beautified by the sweet cries
of geese, wild ducks, and swans. ^10-7

May the heaps of burning coals become heaps of jewels;
may the burning ground become a floor of crystal;
may the mountains that crush the damned become instead
mansions of offering, filled with buddhas. ^10-8

May the rain of coals, molten rock, and weapons
from now on become a rain of flowers;
may the striking of weapon against weapon
become instead a playful shower of blossoms. ^10-9

May those sinking helplessly in the river of scalding ash,
their flesh stripped away to reveal bones the color of white jasmine,
gain, through the power of my goodness, bodies like the gods',
and dwell at ease with goddesses beside cooling waters. ^10-10

May those terrified there, who wonder in astonishment—
"Why do the fearsome guards of the Lord of Death, the crows, and the vultures now flee,
and what excellent power dispels this darkness and brings such joy?"—
look up and see Vajrapani blazing in the sky above,
and, filled with joy, be freed from wrongdoing and join him there. ^10-11

May they see a rain of flowers falling mixed with scented water
extinguish the smoldering coals of hell,
and, suddenly satisfied and at ease, wonder what has happened—
may the beings of the hell realms then see Avalokiteshvara,
the bodhisattva of compassion, holding a lotus. ^10-12

"Friends, cast aside your fear and come quickly here to me!"—
may they hear such words from one whose power
has freed them from suffering and brought them to great joy,
one filled with love who protects all beings and holds the awakening mind—
a youth with a topknot, blazing, who removes all fear: has he not already arrived? ^10-13

"Look—here is one whose lotus feet are honored
by the crowns of a hundred gods, whose eyes, moist with compassion,
receive a shower of many flowers upon his head,
in a lovely mansion resounding with the praise-songs of a thousand goddesses."
May the beings of hell, seeing such a gentle, radiant one, cry out in joyful relief. ^10-14

In this way, through the roots of goodness I have gathered,
may the beings in hell see clouds—unobstructed, blissful,
cool and sweetly scented, sent by Samantabhadra and the other bodhisattvas—
pour down rain, and may they rejoice completely. ^10-15

May the animals be freed
from the fear of being devoured by one another;
may the hungry ghosts find happiness
like the people of the Northern Continent, free of hardship. ^10-16

May the hungry ghosts be satisfied
and always refreshed and bathed
by the stream of milk flowing
from the hand of noble Avalokiteshvara. ^10-17

May the blind see forms,
may the deaf always hear sounds,
and may pregnant women give birth without pain,
just as the goddess of illusion does. ^10-18

May the naked find clothing,
the hungry find food,
the thirsty find water
and other delicious drinks. ^10-19

May the poor find wealth,
the grief-stricken find joy,
and may the despairing find new heart
and perfect steadiness of mind. ^10-20

May all sick beings
be swiftly freed from their illness;
may every sickness that afflicts beings
never arise again. ^10-21

May the frightened become fearless,
may those bound be freed;
may the powerless gain strength,
and may beings' minds turn kindly toward one another. ^10-22

May all travelers, in every direction,
find their journeys pleasant and safe;
may whatever purpose brings them to travel
be accomplished without effort. ^10-23

May those who set out in boats and great ships
have their hopes fulfilled;
may they reach the shore in safety
and rejoice there with their loved ones. ^10-24

May those who wander lost on desolate roads
meet with fellow travelers,
and may they walk free of weariness and fear
of bandits, tigers, and other dangers, traveling in ease. ^10-25

May the guardian spirits watch over
children, the elderly, and those without protectors
who are helpless in wilderness and pathless places,
and over those asleep, intoxicated, or careless. ^10-26

May all beings be free from every hardship,
endowed with faith, wisdom, and love,
excellent in food and conduct,
and may they always remember their past lives. ^10-27

May all beings enjoy resources
as inexhaustible as the treasury of the sky,
and may they live freely,
without conflict and without harming one another. ^10-28

May beings of little splendor
gain great splendor;
may those who practice austerity and have unattractive bodies
gain bodies that are beautiful and complete. ^10-29

May however many women there are in the world
attain the state of a man, should they wish it;
may the lowly gain high standing,
yet may their pride also be overcome. ^10-30

Through this merit of mine,
may all beings without exception
abandon all wrongdoing
and always act virtuously. ^10-31

May they never be separated from the awakening mind,
may they remain devoted to the bodhisattva's way of life,
may they be embraced fully by the buddhas,
and may they abandon all the workings of harmful forces. ^10-32

May the lifespan of all these beings
be immeasurably long;
may they always live happily,
and may even the word "death" never be heard. ^10-33

May every direction be filled
with groves of wish-fulfilling trees,
resounding with the sweet teaching of the Dharma
spoken by buddhas and their bodhisattva heirs. ^10-34

Everywhere, may the ground
be free of gravel and the like,
and may it lie smooth as the palm of a hand,
with the nature of lapis lazuli. ^10-35

At the great gatherings of practitioners,
may many bodhisattvas
sit adorning the earth
with their own excellent qualities. ^10-36

May all living beings
constantly hear the sound of the Dharma
from birds, from trees, from rays of light,
and even from the sky itself, without interruption. ^10-37

May they always meet
the buddhas and their bodhisattva heirs,
and with endless clouds of offerings
may they worship the teachers of the world. ^10-38

May the gods send rain at the proper time,
so the harvests are abundant;
may kings rule according to what is right,
and may the world flourish and prosper. ^10-39

May medicines be effective,
and may prayers recited in solitude succeed;
may sky-faring beings and flesh-eating spirits alike
be filled with compassion. ^10-40

May no being ever suffer,
never commit wrong, never be sick,
never be scorned, and never be discouraged. ^10-41

May monasteries flourish,
adorned with recitation and study;
may the monastic community always be harmonious,
and may its aims be accomplished. ^10-42

May monks who wish to train
find the solitude they need;
may they abandon all distraction
and make their minds fit for meditation. ^10-43

May nuns receive what they need to live,
and be free from quarrels and harm;
likewise, may all who have gone forth into monastic life
never fall from their ethical discipline. ^10-44

May those who have broken their discipline, feeling regret,
work always to exhaust their wrongdoing;
may they attain a fortunate rebirth,
and there too may their commitment remain unbroken. ^10-45

May the learned be honored
and receive the offerings they need;
may their minds become entirely pure,
and may their good reputation spread in every direction. ^10-46

Without ever experiencing the suffering of the lower realms,
and without the need for difficult austerities,
may beings, with bodies surpassing even those of the gods,
swiftly attain buddhahood. ^10-47

May all beings, again and again,
make offerings to all the buddhas,
and, through the buddhas' inconceivable bliss,
may they always be happy. ^10-48

May the bodhisattvas
accomplish what they intend for the benefit of beings;
may whatever the protectors have in mind for beings
come to pass for them. ^10-49

Likewise, may the solitary realizers
and the hearers also find happiness. ^10-50

And may I too, through the kindness of Manjushri,
remember all my past lives
and attain full ordination as a monk
until I reach the joyous ground of a bodhisattva's realization. ^10-51

May I live simply, sustained by whatever food I find,
and may I be well in every life;
may I find perfect conditions for solitary retreat
in every one of my lives. ^10-52

Whenever I wish to see him,
or wish to ask him even some small question,
may I see the protector Manjushri himself,
without any obstacle. ^10-53

Just as Manjushri acts
to accomplish the good of all beings
throughout the ten directions to the very edge of space,
may my own conduct become just like his. ^10-54

For as long as space endures,
and for as long as the world endures,
may I too remain,
dispelling the sufferings of the world. ^10-55

Whatever suffering there is for beings,
may it all ripen upon me instead;
and through the community of bodhisattvas,
may the world enjoy happiness. ^10-56

May the Dharma, the one true medicine for the suffering of the world,
the source from which all happiness arises,
long endure, richly supported and honored. ^10-57

I bow to Manjushri,
through whose kindness virtuous understanding arises in me;
I also bow to my spiritual friend,
through whose kindness I have flourished. ^10-58

This completes the tenth chapter, called "Dedication," of *Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*. ^10-a
