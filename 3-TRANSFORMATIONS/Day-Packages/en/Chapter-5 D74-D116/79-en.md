## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the thirteenth, fourteenth, and fifteenth verses of the fifth chapter. Let us look together at a method for taming our own mind. It is like covering a shoe's sole with leather, instead of covering the whole thorny earth.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

To protect our feet from the thorns and sharp stones covering the earth everywhere, we could never cover the whole earth with leather. But by wearing just a small piece of leather on the sole of our shoe, our feet stay just as comfortable as if the whole earth were covered.

In the same way, we could never defeat or turn back every single harmful person and enemy in the world, one by one. But if we turn back and tame only the anger and hatred that arise in our own mind, every outer harm calms down naturally. So we do not need to tame even a single outer enemy. By taming only our own mind, we become completely free from every outer fear.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, in the course of one single action, I will generate clear loving-kindness from my heart, not just with words.

**Explanation:** *(Diligence Practice)* Even many weak and unclear actions of body and speech bring far less result than generating one single clear and pure mind of love and compassion. Long ago, a mother's child was swept away by a river. Through the power of the clear, strong love she felt for her child, she was reborn in the world of Brahmā. So today, whatever we do, we will not do it with mere words or empty formality. We will do it with a genuine, clear mind and pure love, from the heart.

For example, in order to remove harm such as thorns to one's own feet, one might wish to cover the whole surface of the earth entirely with leather, but there could never be enough leather to cover so much — it simply cannot be done. ##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Suppose a person of great aversion to unpleasant contact wished to cover with leather every surface of the earth over which he might travel, so that he would never encounter the rough sensation of stones, thorns, and the like. There could never be enough leather to cover the earth in that way — it is utterly impossible to cover it in every respect. But if he merely places a single layer of leather on the sole of his own shoe, this is the same as if every last surface of the earth had already been covered with leather.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Although one can never cover the whole surface of the earth with leather to guard against every thorn, covering merely the sole of one's own shoe accomplishes the very same purpose — an image, as the preceding verse teaches, for how conquering this single mind of anger accomplishes the same purpose as conquering every outer enemy.

**Key points.**

- **Covering the whole earth is impossible.** However much one might wish to guard the feet from thorns and the like by covering the entire earth with leather, there could never be enough leather to do so.
- **Covering merely the sole is sufficient.** Covering just the sole of one's own shoe with leather accomplishes the same protection from thorns as if the whole earth had been covered.
- **The point of the analogy.** This image illustrates that conquering this single mind of anger accomplishes the same purpose as conquering every outer enemy.

In just the same way, he explains, one cannot possibly turn back, one by one, every single outer thing — such as feelings of pleasure and pain, or favorable and unfavorable circumstances. It suffices to turn back one's own mind and, doing so, to know how to engage with those things as they come.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Just as the analogy shows, one cannot possibly turn back every single outer thing that causes harm. Rather, one must turn this mind of one's own back from generating anger when it focuses on those objects; through growing accustomed to this, the perfection of patience is completed. What use would it be for me to turn back the other objects of my anger? — for it is impossible to do so, and there is no need.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

One does not attain the ending of enmity or of the anger it provokes by eliminating all enemies, since unruly sentient beings are limitless like space, and there is no way to overcome them all. Therefore, if one conquers this single anger of one's own mind, it is as if all those enemies have been conquered — just as, for example, there could never be enough leather to cover the whole surface of the earth, yet the leather of a shoe-sole alone is the same as if the whole earth were covered. In just the same way, since one cannot possibly turn back every outer harm-doing thing, one should instead turn this mind of one's own back from anger; by this alone patience is accomplished, so what use is there in turning back all the others? <!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For example, though one might wish to cover the whole surface of the earth with soft leather in order to guard against the harm of thorns and the like, there could never be enough leather for that; but by covering merely one's own foot with the leather of a shoe-sole, the purpose of covering the whole surface of the earth is accomplished all the same. In just the same way, one cannot possibly turn back and eliminate every last unpleasant-appearing outer thing; rather, one should turn this mind of one's own back from anger and hatred, and abide in loving-kindness. What use, then, is there in turning back all the other outer harm-doers? Even without turning them back, once the mind is at peace the perfection of patience becomes utterly complete — just as the sutra says: "What is the perfection of patience? <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

In just the same way, one cannot possibly turn back or overcome every single outer harm-doing thing, such as enemies — there is no measure to how many there are to overcome. Rather, this mind of mine, when it gives rise to anger by focusing upon an object that causes harm, is itself what must be turned back or guarded against; and through growing accustomed to this, patience is brought to completion by that power alone. So what need is there to turn back the other outer harm-doing enemies in that way? Turning them back would be a great exertion, and there is no need for it. Even without turning them back, if this single mind of one's own is turned back by its antidote and one's anger is thereby calmed, that alone accomplishes the purpose and patience is completed — as the sutra says: "What is the perfection of patience? It is being free from a mind that is thoroughly tormented," and as the hero [Āryaśūra] also says: "By conquering this one anger, every hostility is conquered." Therefore patience, too, is held to consist precisely in the calming of anger in the mind, and its completion is established through bringing that habituation to its culmination.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-14]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If it were necessary to turn back every single outer harm-doing thing or enemy by eliminating them all utterly, as though rendering them empty and without root, then such a turning-back could never be accomplished. Rather, one should turn back this very intention of anger or harm within one's own mind; and once that is turned back, what need is there to turn back the outer, circumstantial harm-doing enemies? There is no need. It is the nature of things that if one does not oneself inflict harm upon others, no one else will inflict harm upon oneself in turn; therefore he teaches that one should turn back and abandon this angry mind of one's own.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** One cannot possibly turn back or eliminate every outer harm-doing enemy and thing; there is neither the ability nor the need to do so. Rather, by turning back this very anger in one's own mind and abiding in loving-kindness and patience, the perfection of patience is brought to complete fulfillment.

**Key points.**

- **Turning back every outer thing is impossible.** Although one cannot possibly turn back every single outer harm-doing thing, if one turns this mind back from anger, patience is completed, and there is no need to turn back the other harm-doers.
- **The essence of the perfection of patience.** By turning back from anger and abiding in loving-kindness, calming the mind, the perfection of patience is completed; the sutra likewise teaches that the perfection of patience is being free from a mind that is thoroughly tormented.
- **Turning back outer things is pointless.** Turning back outer harm-doers is a great exertion and pointless; Āryaśūra likewise teaches that by conquering this one anger, every hostility is conquered.
- **The nature of non-harming.** If one does not inflict harm on others, no one else will do harm to oneself, so there is no need to turn back every outer harm-doer or enemy.
- **Applying this to bodily conduct.** Since one cannot turn back each and every outer thing — such as feelings of pleasure and pain, or favorable and unfavorable circumstances — one must instead turn back one's own mind and thereby know how to engage skillfully with them.
- **The point of the analogy.** Just as covering the sole of one's shoe with leather is the same as covering the whole earth, so too, if one turns back this single mind of anger, there is no need to turn back everything else, and the perfection of patience is completed.

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-15`):*

> Just as a single clear mental state can produce a result like rebirth in the highest heavens, the results of body and speech do not follow that pattern when conduct is corrupt.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

This is taught in order to show that the perfection of diligence, whose nature is delighting in virtuous action, is likewise the mind. Just as the fruit of generating a single vivid engagement of mind in the meditative absorption of concentration is rebirth in a realm such as that of Brahmā, so too the fruit of the exertion made through body together with speech, being weaker conduct, does not achieve that kind of result.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-15]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that among efforts made in virtue through body, speech, and mind, the virtue of the mind has the greatest power in accomplishing virtue. For example, just as generating even a single instance of a vivid quality of mind, present as it is in cultivating meditative concentration, has the great power to bring about a fruit such as rebirth in the world of Brahmā, virtuous action of body and speech does not yield a fruit of such powerful force. He teaches that it is chiefly virtue exerted from the mind that has the greater power.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Diligence, too, resides within the mind: it is not a matter of a dull, unfeeling loving-kindness or compassion, but of a vivid mind — as when a mother, generating just such tenderness for her only child, thereby comes to be reborn in the form-realm heavens, such as the world of Brahmā, and attains an extraordinary happiness. For example, as the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, a mother generated great tenderness for her dying child, and, in another case, two women, a mother and daughter, generated a single vivid mind of mutual loving-kindness for one another as they were being swept away by a river, and, upon dying, the fruit of that was rebirth in the world of Brahmā. In just this way, however much virtue one accomplishes through the great exertion of body together with speech, since that exertion — body and speech — is a weaker form of conduct, its fruit does not achieve a result such as rebirth in the world of Brahmā, because the mind is what chiefly matters.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Diligence too — bringing habituation to its culmination — depends on the mind: just as generating even a slight vivid mind that delights in cultivating the first meditative absorption and the like, gathered within that absorption, has as its fruit rebirth in a realm such as that of Brahmā, so too, if it is not joined with a powerful mind, the fruit of a mind accompanied by body and speech, being conduct weakened by only slight delight, is unable to bring about rebirth in the first absorption and the like in that way. Hence it depends on a powerful mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

According to the detailed explanation, even a mere slight vivid mind gathered within the discipline of meditative concentration — one explains that a virtuous deity is a being without physical or verbal action — produces, from merely generating that much, a fruit such as rebirth in the world of Brahmā. Some say this is like generating a loving mind, as taught in the sutra, leading to rebirth in Brahmā's realm. If the mind is not joined with power, the fruit of an action accompanied by body and speech likewise fails to produce such a fruit, because the mind's conduct is weak.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For example, as the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, a woman generated a single vivid mind of loving-kindness for her child as he was being swept away by a river, and, plunging in to rescue him with eager delight, died — and the fruit of that was that she was reborn in the world of Brahmā. In just this way, if the mind is not vivid, the fruit of engaging in great exertion accompanied by body and speech does not attain such a vivid state of mind, because it clings to the mere conduct or engagement of the mind superficially. Hence diligence, too, is accomplished only through a vivid mind that delights in virtue.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Just as, without any accompanying action of body or speech, generating and cultivating even once a single vivid mind delighting in the cultivation of the first meditative absorption of equipoise — free from the faults of laziness and the like, exceedingly vivid — is taught to have as its fruit rebirth in a divine abode of the first absorption, such as the world of Brahmā, so too, if the delighted and vivid impulse of mind is not joined with power, then not only is there no action of body and speech at all, but even the virtuous action accomplished through the exertion of body together with speech has, as its fruit, no such rebirth in a realm such as Brahmā's, because the mind's conduct — or its delighted engagement — has become weak; such a rebirth is simply not possible for it. Some explain this by way of the example, given in a sutra, of generating the immeasurable minds of love and the rest leading to rebirth in Brahmā's world. Others explain it by way of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's account: that once, when a mother and a daughter were crossing a river, the mother thought, "If only my daughter is not swept away and killed by this river," and the daughter thought, "If only my mother is not swept away and killed by this river" — through generating in this way a single vivid mind of loving-kindness or compassion for one another, even though both were in fact swept away and died, by the power of that virtuous mind they were reborn in the world of Brahmā, as is generally explained. Therefore diligence, too, is held to consist in a vivid mind that delights outwardly, in every aspect, upon focusing on a virtuous object, and its completion is established through bringing that habituation to its culmination.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

If one generates just such a single vivid mind endowed with the concentration that is the actual state of meditative absorption, its fruit is to take rebirth in a form-realm abode — the world of Brahmā, namely the abodes of Mahābrahmā, the Brahma-retinue, and the Brahma-purohitas and the like — and thereby to attain the bliss of meditative concentration. In just this way, whatever merit is the fruit of striving, through great exertion of body and speech, in virtuous deeds such as prostration, circumambulation, and the recitation of prayers, is said to be "due to weaker conduct" — meaning that its nature, as conduct, is somewhat weaker even than a virtuous mind. Therefore, the root of virtue generated through the mind, when generated in that other way, does not produce such vast merit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-15]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC13_KTB -->
##### The Mother and Child Swept Away by the River

In a side-note drawn from the circle of Nirvāṇa Sūtra material, it is told how a mother and child were swept away by a river. Long ago, a woman was carrying her small child in her arms as she crossed the river Gaṅgā, and both were swept away by the current and died. As the mother and child were dying, great compassion arose in them; the mother thought, "Alas, my own death matters little — it is this child of mine that is so pitiable and moving," and, generating for just an instant a vivid mind of loving-kindness and compassion on the basis of that thought, upon dying she was reborn in the world of Brahmā, so it is told. Minyak Kunzang Sonam also relates that in another sutra it is said that when a mother and daughter were being swept away by a river, each thought of the other — "If only I die and my daughter does not," and "If only my mother does not die, even if I do" — and, through generating in this way a single vivid mind of loving-kindness and compassion for one another, even though both were in fact swept away and died, by the power of that virtuous mind they were reborn in the world of Brahmā, as is also explained.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree: Diligence, Too, Depends on the Mind

Generating, for just an instant, a single vivid and powerful mind has as its fruit something as great as rebirth in the world of Brahmā, whereas even great exertion through body and speech yields only a slight fruit if the mind's engagement is weak.

###### 2. The Story of the Mother's Compassion Illustrates the Power of a Vivid Mind

The story known from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra — of a mother reborn in the world of Brahmā through generating, for just an instant, a vivid mind of compassion — illustrates the power of a vivid mind.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Notes Two Ways of Explaining This Illustration

Some commentators explain this story as an illustration of generating the four immeasurables, while others illustrate it with the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's story of a mother and daughter swept away by a river.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Among the virtues of body, speech, and mind, the virtue of the mind has the greatest power. Generating even a single vivid mind endowed with loving-kindness, compassion, or concentration can produce the great fruit of rebirth in the world of Brahmā, whereas great exertion of body and speech does not produce such a great result if the mind's engagement is weak.

**Key points.**

- **The mind's virtue has the greatest power.** Among the virtues of body, speech, and mind, the mind's virtue has the greatest power, and the nature of diligence that delights in virtuous action, as well as bringing habituation to its culmination, depends on a vivid mind.
- **A vivid mind produces a great fruit.** Generating, for just an instant, a single vivid mind endowed with loving-kindness, compassion, and concentration has, as its fruit, rebirth in the world of Brahmā and the attainment of extraordinary happiness.
- **Illustrated by a story.** The Nirvāṇa Sūtra tells how a woman, upon generating for just an instant a vivid mind of loving-kindness for her child as he was swept away by a river, died and was reborn in the world of Brahmā — illustrating the power of a vivid mind.
- **Without a powerful mind, the fruit is slight.** If the mind is not joined with power and its engagement is weak, then even great exertion of body and speech in virtue cannot bring about rebirth in the first absorption, and does not produce vast merit or a great fruit.
