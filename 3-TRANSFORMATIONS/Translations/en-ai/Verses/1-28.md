## Combined Commentary Summaries
The three classical commentators—Gyaltsab Je, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme—express complete consensus on the philosophical and psychological import of Verse 1-28. They analyze this verse as a profound diagnostic of the human condition, detailing the tragic paradox of sentient beings who desire happiness and wish to avoid suffering, yet constantly act in ways that produce the exact opposite results.

### 1. Structural and Pedagogical Context
*   **Gyaltsab Je's Diagnostic Framework:** Gyaltsab Je frames this verse as an answer to a fundamental objection: If sentient beings already desire happiness and strive to avoid suffering, are they not already capable of managing their own welfare? Why must a Bodhisattva make great efforts on their behalf? Gyaltsab Je explains that despite their desires, beings are completely unskilled in the methods of adoption and avoidance, making the Bodhisattva's intervention absolutely necessary.
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen's Virtuous Superiority:** Sazang Mati Panchen organizes his commentary on this verse under the heading of "how the mind of enlightenment is supreme among all virtues," demonstrating that the Bodhisattva's motivation is the only force capable of correcting this universal confusion.
*   **Ngulchu Thokme's Absence of Practice:** Ngulchu Thokme examines this verse under the category of "showing that the exceptionally distinguished practice is absent in others," contrasting the Bodhisattva's wise, altruistic striving with the self-destructive ignorance of ordinary worldly beings.

### 2. The Universal Paradox: Desiring Happiness, Creating Suffering
All three commentators elaborate on the core tragedy of the verse: the absolute disconnect between what sentient beings want and what they actually do.
*   **Running Toward Suffering:** 
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** explains that under the powerful influence of mental afflictions (*kleśas*), beings accomplish only the causes of suffering, thereby "running directly toward endless cycle of existence and the sufferings of bad migrations."
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** uses the vivid analogy of moths leaping into a flame: in their desperate attempt to pacify immediate, minor sufferings (such as hunger), they commit non-virtuous actions (such as killing) and thus "run directly toward the sufferings of hell."
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** succinctly notes that while beings desire to discard suffering, they run straight toward non-virtue, which is the very cause of that suffering.
*   **Destroying Happiness Like an Enemy:**
    *   All three commentators emphasize the striking Sanskrit and Tibetan image of beings destroying their own happiness "like an enemy" (*shatruvat* / *དགྲ་ལྟར་*). 
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** explains that because of their ignorance, beings abandon virtue—the sole cause of happiness—thereby actively destroying their own well-being.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** and **Gyaltsab Je** point out that due to deep-seated confusion and ignorance of spiritual methods, beings engage in non-virtues that systematically dismantle the happiness they so desperately seek.

### 3. The Core Cause: Ignorance of Adoption and Avoidance
The commentators trace this tragic paradox back to a single root cause: ignorance of what to adopt (virtue) and what to avoid (non-virtue).
*   **Gyaltsab Je** highlights that worldly beings are fundamentally "ignorant of the methods of happiness and suffering" and enter wrongly into these practices due to their "conjoining with delusion."
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** describes them as "foolish sentient beings" who are "ignorant of its methods and, due to confusion, engage in various non-virtues."
*   **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly attributes this self-defeating behavior to "ignorance of not knowing the causes of happiness and suffering."

### 4. The Mind of Enlightenment as the Ultimate Remedy and Friend
Because worldly beings are trapped in this cycle of self-destruction, the commentators argue that the Bodhisattva's mind of enlightenment (*bodhicitta*) is the supreme, incomparable solution.
*   **The Unmatched Virtue:** All three commentators raise a rhetorical question: What other virtue could possibly compare to this mind?
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** argue that the mind of enlightenment is completely unparalleled because its wisdom "dispels the ignorance of not knowing the causes of happiness and suffering," while its love and compassion satisfy destitute beings with all happiness and cut off all their sufferings.
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** adds that this motivation is uniquely powerful because it "satisfies them with all the happiness of higher rebirth and liberation through love, cuts off all the sufferings... through compassion, and clears even their confusion by showing them the way of adoption and avoidance through wisdom."
*   **The Ultimate Spiritual Friend:** The commentators conclude that the Bodhisattva, motivated by this mind, is the only true "trustworthy spiritual friend or companion" (*kalyāṇamitra*) and "benefactor" in the universe, as they are the only ones who unerringly teach the correct path of adoption and avoidance to those who are lost.
