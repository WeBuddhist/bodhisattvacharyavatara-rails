## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the thirty-ninth, fortieth, and forty-first verses of the fifth chapter. This teaching clearly shows that we must tie the wild elephant of the mind to the great pillar of the sacred teachings with the rope of mindfulness.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on this verse give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Our own mind, when it falls under the power of attachment and hatred, is like a wild elephant. Just as a wild elephant, if it slips free from its bindings, causes great harm, so a distracted mind causes the harm of the lower realms, such as hell and the hungry-ghost realm.

The sacred teachings are like a great pillar. Mindfulness is like the rope that binds the elephant of the mind to that great pillar of the teachings. Awareness is like the watchman who continually checks whether the rope has come loose or the mind has slipped away. So we must tie the mind with mindfulness, so it does not wander outward, and guard it with awareness.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will tie my wild mind with mindfulness to a good focus, and I will continually check it with awareness.

**Explanation:** *(Meditation Practice)* A mind maddened by the afflictions, like a wild elephant, wanders aimlessly toward the objects of desire, without any real direction. So we must tie it firmly with the rope of mindfulness to the pillar of reflecting on the sacred teachings, and watch over it again and again with the eye of awareness. By practicing this way, the restless mind becomes calm and does not slip away from its good focus, and we gain firm concentration.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Having carried out and undertaken whatever activity was preceded by the thought, "With my body I will remain in this way," one should then, from time to time, look: "How is this body of mine remaining — in a virtuous, a nonvirtuous, or a neutral way?" and should apply it to virtuous action.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

In all four situations of conduct — going, walking about, lying down, and sitting — one first sends forth the mind's intention, "With my body I will remain in this way," or "I will act in this way," and then, "having undertaken" — that is, having begun, or having started — whichever activity is connected with virtue, such as reading scripture or giving up wrongdoing. (In some editions the text appears as "having realized the activity"; the meaning of this is: having realized and made certain whatever the purpose of the activity is.) In any case, "then," from time to time or in the intervals, one should look, with the watchman of awareness, "In what mode of conduct is this body of mine remaining — in that very conduct of going, sitting, and the like, as I had intended before: remaining in it or not, and engaging in virtue or non-virtue?" And if one has strayed from that earlier conduct to something else, one should again apply oneself to remaining as before; in short, one should strive to remain in faultless, virtuous conduct.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-39]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

At a time such as resting the mind in equipoise, one should remain with the body in such-and-such a way — for example, sitting cross-legged and holding the body straight. "Having undertaken the activity" means that, from the initial stage of beginning, having examined properly in this way with the mind and settled on it, one should then, "from time to time, in what manner is this very body of mine remaining?" — look again and again with mindfulness and awareness.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Illustrate Resolving the Posture First and Then Checking It from Time to Time

As Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both set out by way of example, the practice of this verse is as follows: one first decides in the mind, "I will remain in this way" — for instance, sitting cross-legged in an assembly row, or holding the body straight at the time of meditation — and begins the activity; and afterward, from time to time, one must examine closely, with awareness, whether the body is still remaining as one first intended. In this way the practice is stated directly.

###### 2. His Holiness the Dalai Lama and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Show That Checking the Body Supports Actual Virtuous Practice

His Holiness the Dalai Lama and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both give examples drawn from occasions of virtuous practice such as reciting from memory, reading scripture, and giving up wrongdoing, and show that this very practice of examining the state of the body again and again with awareness is a means connected with the actual practice of virtuous action.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites a Variant Reading, "Having Realized the Activity," of the Root Text's "Having Undertaken the Activity"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites, as a variant translation in some editions, "having realized the activity" (བྱ་བ་རྟོགས་ནས) for the root text's phrase "having undertaken the activity" (བྱ་བ་བསྡོགས་ནས). Although there is no great difference in meaning, he states that the essential point is the same: one must begin by knowing for certain the purpose of the action.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches the general point of guarding all the body's modes of conduct by joining mindfulness and awareness as a pair. It shows a special method that is not limited to walking and looking: whatever one does, such as sitting or reciting from memory, one first sends forth the intention of how the body will remain, and then, once engaged in the activity, from time to time examines the state of one's own body.

**Key points.**

This text shows the crux of two stages in guarding the body's conduct. First, whatever one does, one engages in the activity after mindfully sending forth the intention, "The body will remain in this way"; and then, during the activity itself, one examines with awareness — not constantly, but from time to time — whether the body is still as before. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain this clearly with examples: sitting in an assembly row with legs crossed and hands resting in equipoise, in the seven-point posture of Vairocana; or, when resting in meditative equipoise, holding the spine straight. Further, His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama and Minyak Kunzang Sonam teach that when reciting from memory, or reading and giving up wrongdoing, on one's seat, one first sits with the body upright and engages in the activity, and then must look from time to time to see whether the conduct of the body has declined. Minyak Kunzang Sonam also states that in some editions the root text's "having undertaken the activity" (བྱ་བ་བསྡོགས་ནས) appears instead as "having realized the activity" (བྱ་བ་རྟོགས་ནས); this means that, having found certainty about the purpose of the action, one must examine the body from time to time, so there is no difference in the point. In short, this verse teaches a special practice of the paired union of sending forth the mindful intention beforehand and checking from time to time with awareness, in regard to any bodily activity.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that, just as proper conduct of the body is needed, so the mind — like a maddened elephant — which has been tied by the rope of mindfulness to the great pillar of thinking on the Dharma, must be watched, with awareness, for whether it has broken loose from that and is scattering off in every direction; and that, in whatever situation arises, it must be held with effort, one-pointedly, on a virtuous object of focus.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-40]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Just as a maddened elephant that slips its tether does great harm, and so must be tied firmly so that it does not break loose, so too this mind of one's own, fallen under the sway of attachment, aversion, and the like — like a maddened or crazed elephant — if it becomes distracted toward external objects of attachment and aversion, does the harm of the lower realms, such as hell and the hungry-ghost realm. Therefore, so that one's mind does not engage outward at all, one must, by means of mindfulness and awareness, tie it to the great pillar that is the one-pointed thinking on such matters as listening to, explaining, and meditating on the sacred Dharma, just as the elephant is tied so that it does not break loose elsewhere — in the same way, with all effort, that is, with earnest endeavor. One should not stir the body about or utter speech; and one must not let the mind be distracted outward for even an instant from thinking one-pointedly on the words and meaning of the sacred Dharma — its view, meditation, and conduct; tying it with mindfulness, one must guard it with awareness.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

In examining mental conduct, "tying the mind to a virtuous focus" is as follows. This mind of one's own — fallen under the sway of the afflictions such as attachment and aversion, maddened and untamed, like an untamed, maddened elephant — to the steady great pillar of thinking one-pointedly on the sacred Dharma, on virtue, which has become a means of benefit for oneself and others, such as the trainings one has undertaken: just as it was tied, so that it does not stray onto another, mistaken path, in that way one should, with every effort, examine and investigate it, hold it firmly with mindfulness, and thereby bring one's own mind under one's own control.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The mind that has come under the power of the afflictions such as attachment is the elephant of the mind — of a nature like one intoxicated by grape wine. To the great pillar of thinking on the sacred Dharma — such as meditating on the difficulty of finding the freedoms and endowments, on death and impermanence, on karma, cause and effect, and on the faults of samsara; or else to the great pillar of thinking on loving-kindness, compassion, bodhicitta, and so on — first, by means of mindfulness and awareness, just as it was tied, so that it does not stray elsewhere, in that way, through earnest effort in every way, one should examine and investigate it again and again.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. This Verse Opens the Section on Examining the Conduct of the Mind

This verse opens a new section, on examining the conduct of the mind: after the conduct of body and speech has been examined, it sets out as the chief key point of the chapter that one must examine whether the mind itself remains one-pointedly on the sacred Dharma.

###### 2. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Assigns Separate Functions to Mindfulness and Awareness in the Elephant Image

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains the parts of the image one by one: mindfulness is like the rope that ties the elephant, and awareness is set as the function of examining whether or not that rope has slipped — thus setting out clearly the separate work of mindfulness and awareness.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Widens the Range of the Object of Focus, the "Great Pillar of Thinking on the Dharma"

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk states that the image of the "great pillar of thinking on the Dharma," as an object of focus, can be applied on the occasion of both the common four mind-trainings (the difficulty of finding the freedoms and endowments, death and impermanence, karma, cause and effect, and the faults of samsara) and the uncommon loving-kindness, compassion, and bodhicitta — thereby setting out a wide range for the object of focus.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Having first examined the conduct of body and speech, this verse opens the text that shows how, with regard to the principal field of engagement — the conduct of the mind — one examines it and fixes it on a virtuous focus. Likening the mind intoxicated by the afflictions to a hard-to-tame great elephant, it instructs that, through the power of mindfulness and awareness, one must hold it so that it does not slip from the focus of the sacred Dharma. This is an important verse that is like the heart-essence of the fifth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, on guarding awareness.

**Key points.**

As Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain from the standpoint of the structure of the text, this verse opens the door from guarding the conduct of the body to the stage of examining the mind itself and binding it to a virtuous focus. That is: this mind, thoroughly maddened by afflictions such as desire and hatred, is, like a worldly maddened elephant, fierce and hard to tame; if it openly breaks loose, there is the danger of producing the great suffering of the lower realms. Therefore, as His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes clear, mindfulness is like the rope that binds the elephant of the mind to the great pillar of the sacred Dharma, and awareness is like the observer who constantly checks whether the rope has loosened or the mind has broken away. As Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk states, the pillar of focus, or thinking on the Dharma, can be applied to whichever virtuous object of focus is suitable — whether the common preliminary meditations such as the difficulty of finding the freedoms and endowments, impermanence and death, karma, cause and effect, and the faults of samsara; or else loving-kindness, compassion, bodhicitta, and the like. So that this mind of one's own does not stray even slightly from that virtuous focus, the essential point of this verse is that, with diligence and effort, one must constantly examine and hold the way one's mind is going.

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-41`):*

> "Where is my mind engaged?" — examining it in this way again and again, I should make sure that not even a single moment of meditative effort slips away.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that if one is especially a practitioner of the meditative practice of one-pointed concentration, then all the more, one must stay one-pointedly with the mind, not letting it go for even an instant under the power of the obstacles of laxity and excitement; it is therefore extremely important to examine, posting the guard of mindfulness and awareness.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For one who strives, by whatever means, to meditate on profound concentration, one should devote this mind of one's own to that alone, and not let it become distracted or slip away to another object, even for a single instant, from that meditation; and if it becomes distracted or slips away even slightly, immediately thinking, "Where is this mind of mine engaged and going, other than the concentration?" — thus one must examine one's own mind individually, take it back again, and rest in equipoise without distraction.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Having tied the mind to the virtuous focus, it must still be examined again and again whether it strays from that to something else, so one should examine as follows. In all four situations of one's conduct, by whatever means, having tied the mind in this way to the virtuous focuses, one strives for the one-pointed concentration that is undistracted from them: not letting that virtuous mind be distracted elsewhere and fall under the power of a wrong object even for a single instant, but, from time to time, thinking, "Where is this mind of mine engaged — in virtue, non-virtue, or the like?" — and, with the watchman of awareness, having examined one's own mind again and again, individually, in this way, if it has gone elsewhere one should turn it back from that, place it again on that same virtuous focus, and bring the mind under control; and if it engages by itself, as before, one should rest it in equanimity.

The way of taming the mind in this manner — fixing it on a virtuous focus, examining whether it remains as it was tied, bringing it under control and subduing it — is shown through the example of taming an elephant. Just as an unruly elephant is tied with an extremely strong noose-rope to a post or tree-trunk that is exceedingly firm, so that it does not break loose, and then, if it does as the elephant-keeper has trained it, that is good, but if not, it is struck again and again with a sharp iron hook, chastised, brought under control, and tamed — so too this mind of one's own, like an untamed elephant, is tied with the rope of mindfulness to the firm pillar of the virtuous focus, kept from slipping onto the objects of inverted views, and brought under control; and if it appears that it will not stay as tied but engages a wrong object, one should strike it with the iron hook of awareness, chastise it with sharp, repeated force, bring it under control, keep it from straying from that virtuous focus onto another path, and train it to be tied there. In this way one must tame it, as the text says, from the *Madhyamakahṛdaya*:

> The elephant of the mind that goes astray —
> to the firm pillar of the focus,
> with the rope of mindfulness bind it securely;
> with the hook of wisdom bring it gradually under control.
> — *Madhyamakahṛdaya* (the example of binding the mind's elephant with the rope of mindfulness and mastering it with the hook of wisdom)

So it is said; and the *Bhāvanākrama* too says, "With the rope of mindfulness and awareness, the elephant of the mind should be tied to that very stake of the focus." Here, "the hook of wisdom" speaks of awareness as like a hook, while "the rope of awareness" speaks of awareness as like a rope; these do not conflict. For the actual tying of the mind to its focus in a continuous way is mindfulness, and awareness too, by recognizing the mind's scattering away from the focus and so on, sets it back on the former focus, and so is indirectly what fixes the mind on the focus. And in the commentary on the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*:

> Mindfulness and awareness are what fix the mind close to its object: the one, because it keeps the mind from scattering away from the focus; the other, because it thoroughly knows the mind's scattering.
> — *Sūtrālaṃkāra* commentary (the source for both mindfulness and awareness being what fix the mind on the focus)

By these passages it is shown that relying on mindfulness and awareness to purify ethical discipline through all three doors is the sublime method for accomplishing concentration, as in the sūtra:

> Monks, relying on ethical discipline free of regret, a workable concentration of the mind will arise.
> — Sūtra (the source for how concentration arises from stainless discipline)

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Moreover, by whatever means, one should strive for the concentration in which the mind remains one-pointed, thinking on and practicing the meaning of the sacred Dharma, and should remain so, not letting it slip elsewhere even for a single instant. And if on some occasion the mind becomes slightly distracted elsewhere, one should think, "Where is this mind of mine engaged now?" — thus examining one's own mind by oneself, individually, again and again, and resting it once more in equipoise on the virtuous focus.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Continuing the Elephant Image of Verse 5-40, This Verse Shows That the Mind Must Be Examined Moment by Moment to Keep It from Straying

Continuing the image of tying the elephant from verse 5-40, this verse shows the key point of striving for the concentration of a one-pointed mind: not letting the mind slip from its focus even for a single instant, one must continuously examine one's own mind individually.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Separates the Functions of Mindfulness and Awareness and Supports Them with Many Scriptural Sources

Minyak Kunzang Sonam separates the functions of mindfulness and awareness: he shows mindfulness to be like the rope that ties the elephant, and awareness like the hook that checks whether the rope has slipped, and sets this out with citations from many scriptural traditions.

###### 3. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Presents This Examination as an Important Means of Abandoning Laxity and Excitement

His Holiness the Dalai Lama sets out this examination as an important means of abandoning the interruption of laxity and excitement, and shows that it can be applied directly to the practice of calm abiding (śamatha).

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* teaches that, when striving for the concentration of a one-pointed mind on its focus, one must not let the mind stray from the virtuous focus for even a single moment, and that, "Where is this mind of mine engaged — in virtue or non-virtue, in what is to be done or what is not to be done?" — one must examine it again and again, individually, with mindfulness and awareness; the commentaries are in full agreement on this.

**Key points.**

- **Not letting the mind slip from the focus.** When striving to meditate on profound concentration, one must not let the mind slip, for even a single instant, under the power of distraction or of a mistaken object; and should it become even slightly distracted, one must immediately take it back and rest in equipoise.
- **The example of taming the mind's elephant.** This untamed elephant of the mind is to be tied with the rope of mindfulness to the firm pillar of the virtuous focus so that it does not slip away; and if it engages a mistaken object, it must be struck with the iron hook of awareness, firmly subdued, and brought under control.
- **The functions of mindfulness and awareness.** The actual means of tying the mind to its focus in a continuous way is mindfulness; awareness, by recognizing when the mind scatters elsewhere, sets it back on the former focus, and so indirectly is what fixes the mind on the focus. ⚑ The *Madhyamakahṛdaya* speaks of awareness as like a hook, with "the hook of wisdom" (ཤེས་རབ་ལྕགས་ཀྱིས), whereas the *Bhāvanākrama* speaks of "the rope of awareness" (ཤེས་བཞིན་གྱི་ཐག་པ); he holds that these two statements do not conflict.
- **Abandoning the obstacles of laxity and excitement.** Especially for one who practices the meditation of one-pointed concentration, it is extremely important, without releasing the mind for even an instant under the power of the obstacles of laxity and excitement, to post the guard of mindfulness and awareness and examine.
- **Taming the mind and resting in equanimity.** Examining one's own mind with the watchman of awareness: if the mind has gone elsewhere, one turns it back, sets it again upon the focus, and brings it under control; and if it engages the focus by itself, as before, one should rest it in equanimity.
