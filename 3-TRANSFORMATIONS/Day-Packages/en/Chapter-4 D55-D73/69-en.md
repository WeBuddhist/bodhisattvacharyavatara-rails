## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 38 and 39 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Just as a soldier takes pride in the scars of a pointless battle and wears them like ornaments, we too must hold this same outlook toward the hardships of working for awakening.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a comparison that is easy to understand and encouraging.

For example, fire is hot by nature. We do not need to ask whether a particular fire is hot, since heat is the very nature of fire. In the same way, whoever's mind the afflictions arise in, they cause harm by their very nature. We do not need to look for another reason why the afflictions cause harm. They always give rise to suffering.

So, since the very nature of the afflictions is to cause harm, we work without hesitation to conquer them. Even if we meet a hundred hardships, we face them without losing heart. If an ordinary soldier wears the scars of a pointless battle like ornaments, then we should count the hardships met for the sake of great awakening as the finest ornament of all, and welcome them with joy.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will not resent the hardship of my practice. I will welcome it with joy.

**Explanation:** *(Welcoming hardship)* A soldier counts even the scars of a pointless battle as ornaments and is glad of them. So we should be even more glad of the hardships met while working for the great purpose of awakening. If we welcome hardship without resentment, our practice will grow greatly.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: this affliction-enemy, the constant cause of all suffering, is in every respect certainly, by its own nature, an enemy — a force that, entirely on its own, brings harm upon oneself. For me who strives to conquer that whole army of afflictions, today — that is, in the very time of practicing the Dharma, when I am cultivating and growing accustomed to a virtuous frame of mind in order to defeat the affliction-enemy, the time spoken of as the patience of accepting suffering — the *Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path* teaches that if one does not enter into that virtuous activity, the incidental suffering in question does not arise, whereas if one does enter into it, incidental suffering such as hunger, thirst, and fatigue does arise; and it says that if one dwells in austerity, one sets aside whatever worldly comforts are appropriate — for example, even wanting to drink alcohol, one may not drink it; even wanting to indulge in desire, one may not indulge — and in this way one takes a firm hand with the afflicted mind of attachment and aversion. Even though this brings on, as its condition, a certain immediate hardship, it says that one must definitely accept it. However much discomfort arises from relying on that kind of immediate hardship, since it serves a vastly important purpose, one must definitely not let such conditions turn one back.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-38]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

If I do not make the effort to abandon and conquer them, then, constantly, in this life and beyond, these afflictions — which, like the heat that is fire's very nature, need no reason at all to become the cause of every kind of suffering, and which, once they arise in the mindstream of anyone, high or low, are certain by their very nature to be the enemy that brings ruin — will destroy me. So, at this very time when I begin the diligence needed to conquer or abandon them, whatever harm comes my way for the sake of striving against them — going without food, without goods in hand, without a coat on my back, and other such hardships and adverse circumstances, themselves the cause of hundreds of sufferings — needless to say I will not lose heart or become despondent, thinking "I relied on the antidote to the afflictions before and still could not get rid of them, and I surely cannot get rid of them even now," and so grow unwilling and discouraged about relying on the antidote. I will not stop striving until this affliction-enemy is subdued — so it is simply unacceptable to become disheartened or discouraged.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-38]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

By nature — that is, even without being killed, being an object of compassion because it will die of its own accord in any case — the afflictions have become the cause of suffering.

Moreover, when opposing armies clash on the battlefield and, driven by fierce resolve, wish to defeat the enemy, even the pain of being struck by arrows, spears, and other weapons does not stop them from advancing; they do not turn back and flee, leaving the goal of vanquishing the enemy unaccomplished, until that is done.

So needless to say it is unfitting for me to abandon my effort — before this affliction-enemy, which is by nature certainly an enemy from the very moment it is born, and which is constantly the cause of all suffering — is subdued, even when the hardship of trying to conquer it brings me, at that time, any of the hundreds of sufferings such as cold, hunger, and thirst that might make my courage falter and leave me disheartened and discouraged.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-38]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Ngulchu Thokme transcludes verses 4-37 and 4-38 together as one quoted block and comments on both at once; the explanation below covers both. In the current segmentation of this source, the quoted verse-pair is stamped as this file's own block ^4-78, and the explanation as ^4-79.)*

This means: the afflictions — an object of compassion, since, without depending on any killer, they die of their own accord by nature — are enemies that have become the cause of suffering. When fierce resolve arises against enemies of that kind, and one confronts them, drawn up for battle, wishing to conquer them, one does not turn back and flee, leaving the goal unaccomplished, even when struck by the pain of being hit by arrows, spears, and other weapons. So, needless to say, for me who strives to conquer these enemies — certain to be enemies by nature merely through existing, constantly the cause of all suffering — it is right that today, whatever harm comes my way as the cause of many hundreds of sufferings, I should not become disheartened, that is, cowed, or discouraged, that is, joyless.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

If I do not conquer them, then, constantly, by becoming the cause of every kind of suffering, they never cease to cause harm — so these afflictions, certain by nature to be the enemy, are ones against which I now, here, begin the diligence needed to conquer, for the sake of striving against the antidote. Whatever harm comes my way as the cause of many hundreds of sufferings, needless to say I will not become disheartened and cowed, or discouraged and joyless, and I will not abandon the effort until the afflictions are subdued.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-38]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Without needing to depend on any killer, dying of its own accord by nature and unable to remain for long — even at the very end of one's life — this ordinary sort of enemy, the afflictions, is weak and pitiable, an object of compassion in that sense. Yet even such an enemy, when forced into violent confrontation on the battlefield for the sake of killing and the like — drawn up in ranks against each other, closing for combat, engaging in the fray — is met by someone who, summoning courage, resolves with fierce determination to defeat that enemy and take his life; and such a savage, ferocious fighter, even when struck by great and repeated suffering from sharp weapons such as arrows, spears, and swords, does not, whatever he may think, turn back or flee, having failed to accomplish the goal of killing that enemy.

So needless to say — for me, whose enemy is far, far greater: the afflictions, which are constantly the cause of every kind of unbearable suffering that must be experienced, which do nothing but inflict harm by their very nature from the moment they are born even without my having harmed them, and which will never, ever cease unless conquered by the weapon of the antidote — for me, who has generated the diligence to conquer these and set out upon it, whatever harm comes my way today, at this time of applying the antidote — heat, cold, hunger, thirst, exhaustion, and so on, the cause of many hundreds of sufferings — needless to say I must not become disheartened and cowed with courage failing, thinking "I can do no more" out of despair or lost hope, and so grow unwilling to apply the antidote and become discouraged; rather, without turning back until the affliction-enemy is subdued, I must strive on.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-38]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: constantly, from birth to birth throughout all of one's lives, the afflictions — root and secondary — that are the cause of every physical and mental suffering are, all of them, certain by their own essential nature to be nothing but the enemy that harms oneself. For example, just as heat is the nature of fire, so, since the harmful, self-injuring nature of the afflictions is certain regardless of whose mindstream, high or low, they arise in, at the point of beginning the diligence needed to abandon and conquer afflictions such as attachment, whatever hundreds of sufferings come my way at that time or on that occasion — that is, whatever suffering arises, whether from illness such as hunger, thirst, heat, or cold — even so, needless to say I must carry out, through fierce diligence, the training and practice of generating bodhicitta without becoming disheartened or discouraged on account of them.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^4-38]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Since the Afflictions Are, by Nature, an Enemy Constantly Causing All Suffering, It Is Wrong to Lose Heart Even Over Hundreds of Hardships

All the commentaries agree in explaining that, because the afflictions are certain, from the moment they arise, to be nothing but an enemy that harms oneself, it is not right to abandon one's diligence, however much harm arises while striving to conquer them.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Uses the Example of Fire to Establish That the Afflictions Are Certainly an Enemy by Nature

Just as fire's nature is to be hot, needing no further reason, so the afflictions, whoever's mindstream they arise in, high or low, are certainly nothing but an enemy that causes harm through their own essential nature.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Details the Specific Sufferings That May Arise While Applying the Antidote

Going without food, without goods in hand, without a coat on one's back, and other such adverse hardships — even hundreds of them — should not lead one to lose heart or become discouraged about relying on the antidote to the afflictions.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Distinguishes the Ordinary Enemy from the Affliction-Enemy

An ordinary enemy is weak and an object of compassion, yet even a fighter who confronts one, undeterred by the pain of weapons on the battlefield, does not turn back until the goal is accomplished — so, for the sake of conquering the affliction-enemy, which does nothing but harm and is never stopped except by the weapon of the antidote, one should be all the less willing to lose heart over hundreds of sufferings.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Cites the *Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path* to Show That Incidental Suffering Must Not Be Turned Back From

Even though entering into virtuous activity such as austerity brings on incidental suffering such as hunger, thirst, and fatigue, because this serves a vastly important purpose, one must not let such conditions turn one back, as the *Great Treatise* teaches.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** All the commentaries agree that the afflictions are, by their own nature, the enemy that is the constant cause of every suffering of cyclic existence, so that in striving to conquer them one must not lose heart or become discouraged even by hundreds of sufferings such as hunger, thirst, and exhaustion.

**Key points.**

- Just as fire's nature is to be hot, the afflictions are, by their own essential nature and without needing any further reason, an enemy that harms oneself — as both Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explain.
- Even when adverse circumstances such as going without food, goods, or a coat bring much suffering while one applies the antidote, one must not lose heart, thinking one could not get rid of the afflictions before and cannot now, nor grow unwilling to keep relying on the antidote.
- An ordinary enemy, though weak and an object of compassion since it dies of its own accord without being killed, differs from the affliction-enemy, which does nothing but harm from the moment it is born, even without provocation, and which never ceases unless conquered by the weapon of the antidote.
- As the *Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path* teaches, incidental sufferings such as hunger, thirst, and fatigue that arise while dwelling in austerity, since they serve a vastly important purpose, must not be turned back from because of their conditions.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: an enemy inflicts a pointless wound — as when, in fighting an ordinary enemy, one's head or hand gets wounded — and afterward one wears that scar on the body like an ornament, or even shows it off proudly, saying "I got this wound when I once stood my ground as a hero." If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely applied myself to accomplishing the ultimate great purpose of both self and other — the state of omniscience — however much suffering from hardship comes my way, rather than needing to count it as a harm, I ought to count it as splendor and as an ornament and welcome it. Why should it become a harm? It is something to be proud of.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-39]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Even when an enemy, in battle over some matter of no great importance, cuts a wound into one's own head or some other part of the body with a sword or the like, one still displays that scar on one's own body like an ornament, showing it off to others as a sign that one is no coward. If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely begun the diligence to apply myself to the bodhisattva's trainings for the sake of accomplishing the great purpose of buddhahood for both self and other, whatever suffering arises while striving to apply the antidote — why should it be considered a harm? It is not a harm at all; rather, since it purifies obscurations and so forth, it is nothing but a benefit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-39]]

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely applied myself to accomplishing the great purpose of complete buddhahood, why should the suffering of hardship be a harm? It is nothing but a benefit, and so one should welcome it.

If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely applied myself to the bodhisattva's trainings for the sake of accomplishing the great purpose of complete buddhahood, why should the suffering that comes from applying the antidote be a harm? <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(In this source, Minyak Kunzang Sonam's explanation of 4-39's own content opens the long passage given in full on 4-40's page, where his source's own transclusion is anchored; the opening portion pertaining to 4-39 is excerpted here.)*

Why should one not become disheartened and discouraged? In the world, when people quarrel and come to blows over something that produces nothing but strife and confusion, with no benefit at all to self or other — treating something worthless as though it mattered — and, in their rage, inflict wounds on each other's hands, arms, face, chest, and so on with the enemy's own weapon, they afterward, delighted, display the scars, saying "I got this wound then," showing them off with pride as a sign of their own bravery, and wear them on the body like ornaments, cherishing and treasuring them. If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely applied myself to the bodhisattva's conduct for the sake of accomplishing the great cause of complete buddhahood — the source of the great purpose of both self and other — why should the suffering of hardship that arises in accomplishing the sacred Dharma, the antidote to the afflictions, be considered a harm? It is not; rather, since it is nothing but an aid to accomplishing the awakening that is the cause of happiness in this life and beyond, one should rely on it with joy and enthusiasm, without becoming disheartened or discouraged.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-40]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: worldly people, wounded by an enemy who accomplishes no purpose of his own whatsoever — a fresh wound of the kind that leaves a scar, say, on the head, the face, or the front of the body — if they have such a mark, boast of it as a sign of their own bravery, showing it off, and display and wear it on the body like an ornament of gold, silver, or the like. If that is so, then for me, who has genuinely begun the diligence needed to apply myself to the trainings of generating bodhicitta — restraining wrongdoing and gathering virtuous qualities and so forth — for the sake of accomplishing the great purpose of both self and other through abandoning the afflictions, whatever harm or suffering comes my way at that time — such as pangs of cold, hunger, thirst, or illness, or being belittled or spoken ill of by others — why, for what reason, should that be considered a harm to me? It is not the nature of a harm at all; rightly understood, such things are a favorable condition for virtue, a broom that sweeps away suffering, and a spur to renunciation — one should regard them with confidence, welcome them, and cultivate patience with them.

- **Wearing an enemy's scar like an ornament** (དགྲ་ཡིས་བཏོད་པའི་རྨ་སྲོལ་རྒྱན་དང་འདྲར་སྲེལ་བའི་དཔེ) → Just as, in a pointless quarrel, people take even the mark of a wound an enemy inflicted on the body as a sign of bravery and wear it like an ornament, cherishing it — so it is right to welcome the suffering of hardship met while striving in the bodhisattva's conduct, rather than regarding it as a harm. All the commentaries agree on this.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. If Even the Scar of a Pointless Quarrel Is Worn Like an Ornament, How Could One Not Welcome the Hardship of Accomplishing the Great Purpose of Buddhahood?

All the commentaries agree in showing, from the outset, that the suffering met while striving for the great purpose of buddhahood, for self and other, is not a harm — since even the scar of a wound suffered in a fight with an ordinary enemy is proudly worn as a badge of bravery.

###### 2. Sabzang Mati Panchen Establishes That the Suffering of Hardship Is Not Only Not a Harm, but a Benefit

He explains that suffering arising while one strives at the trainings that serve as the antidote to the afflictions is not a harm, but is nothing other than a benefit.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Sets Out in Detail How People Delight in Displaying the Scars an Enemy Has Inflicted

He shows how, after a pointless quarrel, people delight in showing off their wounds as proof of bravery and wear them like cherished ornaments, cherishing them dearly.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Counsels That the Suffering of Hardship Be Regarded as a Favorable Condition and a Spur to Renunciation

He teaches that harm or suffering met while striving at the trainings of bodhicitta is, rightly understood, a favorable condition for virtue, a broom that sweeps away suffering, and a spur to renunciation — to be welcomed and made an object of patience.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Counsels That the Suffering of Hardship Be Counted as Splendor and Ornament and Welcomed

He explains that, just as a wound from an ordinary battle is worn like an ornament, the suffering met in accomplishing the ultimate great purpose of omniscience should be counted as splendor and ornament, and welcomed rather than resented.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows that, just as worldly people count even the scar of a wound suffered in a pointless fight as a badge of bravery and wear it like an ornament, so the suffering of hardship met while striving to accomplish the great purpose of complete buddhahood, for self and other, is not a harm — it should instead be regarded as a favorable condition and welcomed with joy.

**Key points.**

- Just as people count even the mark of a wound suffered in a pointless quarrel as proof of bravery, treasuring it like an ornament, so the suffering of hardship met while practicing the bodhisattva's conduct should be welcomed rather than resented — all the commentaries agree.
- Suffering that arises while striving at the trainings that serve as the antidote to the afflictions, for the sake of accomplishing buddhahood, is not a harm at all; rightly understood, it is nothing but a benefit.
- One should not become disheartened or discouraged about accomplishing the great purpose of all beings; rather, one should hold to the thought that it is only ever a benefit, and rely on it with joy and enthusiasm.
- Harm or suffering met while striving at the trainings of bodhicitta should be regarded with confidence as a favorable condition for virtue, a broom that sweeps away suffering, and a spur to renunciation, and made an object of patience.
- Whatever suffering of hardship arises while striving to accomplish the state of omniscience should be counted as splendor and ornament, and welcomed.
