## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice comes from verses 1 to 3 of the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. It teaches us that to grow and keep the precious awakening mind (bodhichitta) in our hearts, we need to gather a vast amount of positive energy, or merit. Even if we do not have money or expensive goods to offer, we can mentally present all the natural, unowned beauty of the world — lovely flowers, clear clean water, quiet forests, and gems — to the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha) without any stinginess. This practice invites us to cultivate a generous heart through the power of our mind.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These verses show that making offerings to build positive merit is the very foundation of the spiritual path. There is an ancient story about a young man in Shravasti who wasted all his money and became extremely poor. When he asked to become a monk, the Buddha saw that he needed more merit first and asked him to make an offering. The young man found a single blue lotus flower and offered it to the spiritual community with a pure heart. Because of this simple, sincere offering, he gathered enough merit to eventually reach complete spiritual freedom.

Ordinary people like us might not have rich material gifts to give either. However, master Shantideva explains that the universe is full of treasures that belong to no one — such as wild flowers and fruits, healing plants, sparkling jewels, pure drinking water, and peaceful forests. By imagining these beautiful things and mentally offering them to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas without any greed or attachment, we wash away the stain of selfishness from our minds. This mental generosity transforms our heart into a clean vessel, ready to hold the precious awakening mind.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, whenever I see beautiful flowers, clean water, or lovely nature, I will mentally offer them to the Three Jewels without any stinginess or attachment.

**Explanation:** *(Generosity Practice)* Usually, when we see a beautiful flower, a lovely landscape, or something nice, we naturally want to own it or keep it for ourselves. But today, just like the young man who offered the lotus flower, I will let go of my greed and attachment when I see beautiful things in nature. Instead, I will use my imagination to offer them joyfully to the Three Jewels. Through this simple practice of mental generosity, I will clear away selfishness and build the positive merit needed to grow a truly compassionate heart.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Having thus gained the hard-to-find, perfectly complete freedoms and advantages, and knowing the benefits of giving rise to the awakening mind, then — in order to **take hold of** that **precious** mind of awakening whose benefits have just been described — I **make excellent offering**, with a mind of deep, clear faith, **to** the **Thus-Gone Ones**, the perfectly complete Buddhas, the Blessed Ones; and to the **holy Dharma**, the **flawless Jewel** that is free of the **stains** of the two obscurations; and to the **Buddhas' children**, the bodhisattvas whose very nature is **an ocean of** boundless **good qualities**.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

At the moment when one has caught a first taste of that jewel-like awakening mind, in order to make it firm — and in order to take hold of it through a formal ritual — one makes excellent offering to the Buddhas, the Thus-Gone Ones; and to the holy Dharma, the flawless Jewel, which is the Dharma comprised of the two truths of cessation and path in the Great Vehicle. (Alternatively, "holy Dharma" may be applied to the Dharma of scripture and "flawless Jewel" to the Dharma of realization.) In any case, to such a Dharma-Jewel, and to the Buddhas' children — the bodhisattvas who have come into command of ocean-like qualities — one offers, and offers well.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-1]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The second chapter, the chapter on confessing wrongdoing, has two parts: the text itself and its title. The text has four branches — the branch of offering, the branch of paying homage, the branch of going for refuge, and the branch of confessing wrongdoing. Within the branch of offering there are five: offerings actually set out, offerings emanated by the mind, offering through the power of aspiration, unsurpassed offering, and the offering of praise in melody. Within "offerings actually set out" there are three: offering things that have an owner, offering things that have no owner, and the offering of one's own body. This verse is the first of these. Because that precious awakening mind, endowed with such distinguished qualities, does not arise without causes and conditions, its full set of causes must be gathered; and since offering is the highest of all the ways to gather merit — and since setting out pure offerings also clears and gladdens the mind — offering matters greatly.

That offering, too, should be made pure in three ways. Pure intention means offering in order to hold or give rise to that precious awakening mind in one's own being — offering it for that very purpose. Pure field means: the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, who have gone in accord with suchness, the true nature of reality; the flawless holy-Dharma Jewel — the truth of the path, which frees one from attachment, and the truth of cessation, which is that freedom from attachment; and the Buddhas' children, the bodhisattvas, who upon reaching the first ground come to possess an ocean of qualities, such as the twelve hundred qualities and the rest.

Pure substance means offering "well" — that is, with good, clean things, well arranged. "Good" means not gained through wrong livelihood or wrongdoing, not made into an occasion for pride or showing off, and not tainted by miserliness or grudging. "Clean" means that offering with whatever one has that is finest, most cherished, and most rare becomes an antidote to stinginess; whereas offering with wilted greens or the stingy scraps of leaves does not become a cause of merit, and so will not do. "Well arranged" means even, without high and low; upright, without crookedness; and properly filled — for if the vessels are not filled with water and the like, there is the fault of offering meagre enjoyments; if the offerings are thrown out afterward, there is the fault of broken discipline; and scattering the offering-water brings on illness such as tumours. One should set out the offering free of all such faults.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One should offer well — with excellent things, and with respectful intention and application. For what purpose? In order to take hold of that precious mind, the source from which all the good of every being arises. To whom does one offer? To the Thus-Gone Ones and the holy, exalted ones: the holy-Dharma Jewel — the Great Vehicle's truth of cessation and truth of the path, naturally pure and without the slightest adventitious stain — and the great oceans of qualities that are the Buddhas' children, such as the noble Avalokiteśvara and Mañjughoṣa.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-1]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

For the purpose of taking hold of that precious mind, one offers to the Three Jewels: to the Thus-Gone Ones; to the holy Dharma, free of the stains of conceiving things as existent or non-existent; and to the noble bodhisattvas, the Buddhas' children, who possess a boundless ocean of qualities. And the manner is "well" — that is, offering through six aspects: with the intention set on attaining buddhahood, for the sake of beings, with pleasing things, with the three spheres [giver, gift, recipient] completely pure, free of the stain of the discordant side, and dedicated to great enlightenment. So says the detailed explanation.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-1]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For what purpose does one thus completely take hold of that awakening mind, whose nature is measureless precious qualities? To that end, one offers — to the field: the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, who have gone within the state that possesses the two purities of suchness; the holy-Dharma Jewel, gathered under scripture and realization, flawless and utterly pure; and the Buddhas' children, the bodhisattvas, the noble Sangha — the three precious Jewels, endowed with an ocean of profound and vast qualities whose depths are hard to fathom. And the manner: with the substances of offering, both material and mind-made, with the three spheres completely pure and with deep clarity of faith, one offers well.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-1]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For the purpose of taking hold of — that is, giving rise to in one's own being — that precious awakening mind, whose nature is measureless benefit and which has become the source of all the good of oneself and others: one offers to distinguished fields. These are the Buddhas, who have gone within the state of the expanse of reality, suchness, endowed with the two purities; and the holy ones — hence "holy Dharma," which is both holy and Dharma: the flawless holy-Dharma Jewel, the truth of cessation (naturally pure and without even adventitious stains such as attachment) together with the truth of the path (which removes such stains), the Dharma of realization marked by the two truths, along with the Dharma of scripture included in the three baskets; and the Buddhas' children, such as Mañjuśrī and Avalokiteśvara — the noble bodhisattva Sangha, ocean-like in their profound and vast qualities. To these one offers well, through respectful intention and application. And the word "and" gathers in as well the other worthy recipients not explicitly named, such as the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-1]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The recipients to whom offering is made are the Three Jewels; and the purpose for which one offers is to take hold of — or give rise to, in one's own and others' being — that awakening mind of aspiration and engagement, which is like a priceless jewel. So one offers to the holy field: the Thus-Gone Ones, the perfectly complete Buddhas, the Blessed Ones; the flawless holy-Dharma Jewel, gathered under the truths of cessation and path (or under scripture and realization), free of the stains of the two obscurations; and the bodhisattva Sangha, the heart-children of the perfect Buddhas — in short, the Three Jewels, who possess boundless, ocean-like qualities. And the way one offers to that very field is "well": to the holy field of the Three Jewels, with a holy mind — the mind set on attaining buddhahood for the sake of others — and for the purpose of giving rise to that precious awakening mind in one's own and others' being, one offers well, free of any stinginess, with holy substances: things in accord with Dharma and unmixed with wrongdoing, excellent and fine, clean, well arranged, and good in nature.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-1]]

<!-- div:divergences -->
##### ⚑ Divergences (where the commentaries differ)

- **kunpal:** when dividing offering into three, he sets out directly the framework of "pure intention / pure field / pure substance," explaining by way of these three qualities of offering.
- **ngulchu-thogmed / minyak-kunzang-sonam:** they set out directly the six-fold way of offering given in the detailed explanation (the intention to attain buddhahood; for the sake of beings; with pleasing things; the three spheres pure; free of the stain of the discordant side; dedicated to great enlightenment).

The meaning does not conflict, but the fixed enumeration of the qualities of offering differs, so it is noted separately.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of Legs-'ongs

At the phrase "in order to take hold of that precious mind," the story of the boy Legs-'ongs ("Well-Come") is told as an example of the need to gather merit. In Shravasti, a wealthy householder had no son, and after requesting teachings from the teachers of the six [non-Buddhist] schools a son was born to him, whom he named Legs-'ongs. But his parents died, and being reckless he squandered all the family wealth, so his name was even changed to Nyes-'ongs ("Ill-Come"). A servant-woman took him in, but he grew angry and was driven off to the city of Shravasti. There he asked to go forth [as a monk] before the Omniscient One, but was not allowed to until he had first gathered merit. As a means of gathering merit he found a single utpala flower and offered it to the Sangha; his accumulation thereby completed, he confessed, went forth, and is said to have attained the fruit of an arhat.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-1]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of the Householder Palkye

The second story, told together with the first: a miserly householder named Palkye ("Wealth-Increase") fathered five sons in his old age and gave all his wealth to them. Later he went blind and, fallen to the lot of his daughters-in-law, was given poor food and left uncared for. In despair he again asked to go forth, but the Buddha, seeing that he had not gathered merit, did not permit it; so he cut off his food and stayed in the Jeta Grove. The Blessed One, looking on him with compassion, then allowed him to go forth, and he became an arhat. As to why this could happen even though, through the force of miserliness, there seemed to be no seed of merit — the Buddha declared that it was because a fine seed was seen within his afflictions: in a former life he had, with a wood-chip, uttered the word "Buddha," and by that tiny seed he finally attained arhatship. Both stories are told together to make the point of how important it is to gather merit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^2-1]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Chief Purpose of Offering Is to Take Hold of the Precious Awakening Mind

As all the commentaries explain in agreement, the purpose of the offering branch in Chapter 2 is to hold, or give rise to, that precious awakening mind in one's own being.

###### 2. The Field of Offering Is All Three Jewels

The Thus-Gone Ones (the Buddha-Jewel); the flawless holy Dharma (gathered under the truths of cessation and path, or under scripture and realization); and the Buddhas' children who possess an ocean of qualities (the bodhisattva-Sangha Jewel).

###### 3. The Manner of Offering — the Six-Fold Way Given in the Detailed Explanation

With the intention to attain buddhahood, for the sake of beings, with pleasing things, with the three spheres completely pure, free of the stain of the discordant side, and dedicated to great enlightenment.

###### 4. The Marks of Fine Offering-Substance — "Well," Meaning Clean and Well Arranged

A good source (not gained through wrong livelihood or wrongdoing), not made an occasion for pride or display, unbound by stinginess, even without high and low, and well filled.

###### 5. The Three Occasions of Offering

Offering things that have an owner, offering things that have no owner, and the offering of one's body; this verse is the first, the occasion of "offering things that have an owner."

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse opens the offering branch of Chapter 2, stating the commitment to offer well — for the purpose of holding or giving rise to the precious awakening mind in one's own being — to the Three Jewels: the Thus-Gone Ones, the flawless holy Dharma, and the Buddhas' children who possess an ocean of qualities.

**Key points.**

- The chief purpose of offering is to take hold of the precious awakening mind; and offering is the highest of all ways to gather merit, as Kunzang Pelden says.
- The field of offering is the Three Jewels: Buddha, holy Dharma, and Sangha (the Buddhas' children).
- For the manner of offering there is the six-fold framework of the detailed explanation; ⚑ Kunzang Pelden also explains it through the three — pure intention, pure field, and pure substance — so the fixed enumeration differs.
- The offering-substance must have the qualities of being clean and well arranged, unbound by stinginess and not produced through wrong livelihood, as Kunzang Pelden makes clear.
- This is the occasion, within the four branches of Chapter 2's offering, of "offering things that have an owner."

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the occasion of offering things that have no owner: throughout the world-realms of the ten directions, one offers whatever splendour and riches one sees or hears of. It is enough to think, "I offer these," by bringing their forms to mind, or even just their names, or simply the thought that such things exist. As for the things one offers: in the pure realms, and in the boundless realms of gods, nāgas, humans and the rest — whatever **flowers** there are, born of water or of dry land, such as white lotus, mandārava, and utpala; and whatever **fruits** there are, such as bilva, mango, barley, wheat, and grain; and whatever belongs to the class or kinds of **medicine**, such as the medicine-kings gi-wang and the "six excellent substances" — medicinal essences, mineral medicines, and herbal medicines; and, in the world, whatever **jewels** there are, such as the wish-fulfilling gem that grants every want and need, beryl, and sapphire; and, as the sūtra says, **clear, lovely water** — the water of Lake Anavatapta endowed with the eight qualities, the four great rivers flowing from its four sides (Gaṅgā to the east, Sindhu to the south, Pakṣu to the west, Sītā to the north), the seven play-lakes, and so on. The peerless Lord Atiśa said, too, that because the water in Tibet is so good, one may complete the accumulation with this alone.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

[The offerings here are of three sorts:] the offering of unowned substances, the offering of one's own body, and offerings emanated by the mind — [treated in terms of] the substances of offering, the way they are offered, and the reason for offering unowned substances. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Occasion of Offering Things That Have No Owner

Of the three kinds of offering (offering things with an owner, offering things without an owner, and the offering of one's body), this begins the second: taking up in mind all the splendour and riches of the world-realms of the ten directions that no one has claimed, and offering them.

###### 2. Bringing Them to Mind, or Even Their Names, Is Enough

It suffices to offer these substances by recalling the form of whatever one sees or hears of, or even just their names, or the mere thought that such things exist; one need not have them in hand.

###### 3. Four Classes of Offering-Substance

Flowers and fruit; the kinds of medicine; the world's jewels; and clear, lovely water — the substances are counted under these four.

###### 4. The Eight Qualities of Clear, Lovely Water

Cool, sweet, light, soft, clear, stainless, harmless to the stomach when drunk, and harmless to the throat — shown by the sūtra and by the prince's words.

###### 5. A Scriptural Source That Even Unowned Enjoyments May Be Offered

The *Śikṣāsamuccaya* teaches that all enjoyments such as medicines, water, and earth-butter may be offered even though no one owns them.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse opens the occasion of offering things that have no owner: taking up in mind all the flowers, fruits, kinds of medicine, jewels, and clear, lovely water that exist throughout the world-realms of the ten directions, and offering them to the Three Jewels.

**Key points.**

- This is the occasion of offering things that have no owner: one takes up in mind the unclaimed splendour of the world and offers it.
- These substances may be offered simply by recalling them or even their names — one need not have them in hand, as Kunzang Pelden says.
- The offering-substances are counted under four classes: flowers and fruit, medicine, jewels, and clear water.
- The mark of clear, lovely water is its eight qualities, established by the sūtra and the prince's words.
- ⚑ Many of the commentaries (Gyaltsab and the rest) explain this verse together with the following verses as a single cluster of offering-substances, whereas Kunzang Pelden explains this verse on its own — a difference only in segmentation.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Unowned Offering-Substances of Many Extraordinary Kinds

This verse sets out, as offering-substances presented to the Buddhas and their children, the perfect qualities of the outer and inner world: jewel mountains; forested places, secluded and delightful; trees adorned with blossoms; and trees whose branches bend under fine fruit.

###### 2. A Secluded, Delightful Place Is a Quality in Keeping With Meditative Concentration

For "forested places, secluded and delightful," Zhenga's annotations explain a place in keeping with meditative concentration, while Kunzang Pelden points to its being free of human traffic by day and the clamour of sound by night.

###### 3. Two Ways a Tree Is Beautiful: Adorned With Blossoms, and Bending Under Fruit

Trees "adorned and made beautiful with various blossoms" is the beauty of flowers; a tree whose "branches bend under fine fruit" is the beauty of perfect fruit — on which all the commentaries agree.

###### 4. The Example for the Jewel Mountain — Meru and the Seven Golden Mountains

For "jewel mountain," Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga give the example of the king of mountains, Meru, formed of the four kinds of jewels, and the seven golden mountains.
