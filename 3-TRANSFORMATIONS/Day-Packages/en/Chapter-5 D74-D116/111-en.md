## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the ninety-fourth, ninety-fifth, and ninety-sixth verses of the fifth chapter. Today, we learn the conduct of respectfully stretching out the right hand when we show the way to others.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable point, which is easy to understand and encouraging. We must lie down on our right side, like the Protector resting in nirvana. We must cover our own body with the Dharma robe and lie facing the direction we want. Then, with awareness, we must give rise to the thought, "I will rise quickly." We must definitely apply this thought at the very beginning of lying down.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, at night, I will lie on my right side and definitely hold the thought of rising quickly, with awareness.

**Explanation:** *(Diligence Practice)* If we lie on our right side, like the Buddha's posture when he passed into nirvana, our awareness does not wander. If we hold the thought of rising quickly at the very beginning of lying down, we will rise quickly on time.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

That is how it is. Further, on how to avoid giving rise to a lack of faith: when one must signal to others — to point out some other object, for example — one should not signal by merely extending a single finger of the left hand, because that becomes disrespectful, a contemptuous way of putting others down. Therefore, with the body bowed and the mind respectful, one should signal with the fingers of the right hand, raised and stretched out, all of them together; and when another asks one about the road he is travelling or the like, one should show the way likewise. Although it has been said that the word "too" (ཡང) teaches that one should not show even other objects, let alone the way, in a disrespectful manner or with the left hand, I think it would be easier to say instead: not only when pointing out other objects with the right hand stretched out in this way, but the way too should be shown in that manner. When one must signal about a small matter, or to someone close by, one should not signal by moving the hand a great deal, forcefully, and stretching it out, because that becomes the fault of boisterousness (རྒོད་བག).

Therefore one should move the right hand only slightly, and make a sound neither too loud nor too faint — and even that only the sound of snapping the fingers, or calling in a voice that is neither loud nor soft, and so on — and in these ways calmly make whatever signals are needed. Otherwise, it is enough to move the hand slightly and make a faint sound; if one moves the hand a great deal or makes loud sounds and so on, it becomes the fault of one's mind not being settled in equipoise and one's conduct of body and speech not being restrained.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** When showing others the way or an object and so on, one should entirely avoid conduct that leads to a lack of faith and to disrespect. To that end, one should not signal with the left hand or with a single finger, but, with respect, show the way with the whole right hand.

**Key points.**

- When asking the way and so on, or signalling to others, one should not signal with the left hand or with the fingers in a disrespectful manner.
- If one signals with a single finger alone, or by pointing the index finger, it becomes conduct that belittles others or is disrespectful.
- When one must show others the way or an object, one should show it with respect toward the one being shown, with the whole right hand.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If there is a need such as signalling someone far away, or signalling quickly, then, held by mindfulness and awareness, there is no fault in moving the hand a great deal and making a loud sound, or calling out in a high, loud voice. In that way, if one does not examine well and abandon even subtle conduct that causes others to lose faith, then, as others' loss of faith arises, it becomes a cause of accumulating great sin, of others' benefit not being accomplished, and so on; so abandoning it is very important.

On that account, the way to guard against what could harm others: the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* says:

> Never boisterous, but gentle, / and speaking in a fitting and mild way, / fortunate people become keen [toward one], / and one becomes worthy of being heeded.

As this says, by restraining bodily and verbal misconduct, faith arises and one's words become worthy of being heeded; and, moreover, making beings well-disposed is itself the work of a bodhisattva, which is established by the scriptural passage from the *Dharmasaṃgīti* cited above. If one wonders what fault there is if one does not act in this way:

> If he is not fit to be taken up, / the world scorns the sprout of the victors; / like a fire covered with ashes, / it burns beings in the hells and so on.

That is: through misconduct of body and speech, the bodhisattva himself comes to be scorned, and as a result [others] go to the lower realms; and because he himself has made the cause for that, it becomes a fault for himself as well. That is the meaning. As for the need to abandon this, the same text says:

> For this reason, in the victor's sutra on commitments: / whatever makes beings lose faith, / that should be wholly abandoned with care, / so it was taught in the *Ratnamegha* (དཀོན་མཆོག་སྤྲིན).
> As it says:
> What is the right conduct of a bodhisattva? Because of which beings would lose faith in a bodhisattva, he does not act in an unsuitable situation, does not act toward an unsuitable object, and does not speak at an unsuitable time, and so on; and
> From the *Dharmasaṃgīti* too: "Whoever protects beings protects ethical discipline," it teaches; by this method, he does not stay in a secluded place or the like together with a woman about whom suspicion could arise — thus he guarded the world. In the same way, urine, excrement, spittle, pus, and other loathsome things, whether in water that is used or on open ground, whether secluded or not, are not discarded there, in order to guard the minds of gods and humans — and so on; and from the Individual Liberation (Prātimokṣa) too: "To abandon lack of faith, one should not chew toothwood in front [of others] or spit." So it says. This practice of respect and propriety is not to be observed only toward one's near ones (ཉི་ཚེ) among fellow practitioners of pure conduct, but should be regarded as something to be observed everywhere. This sutra does not teach it for the sake of fellow practitioners of pure conduct, but to show the dread of a very grave fault if these things are done in front of them; just as, in this same text, the rule "one should not make a loud noise" is not [meant] only for near ones (ཉི་ཚེ་བ), and so on; and from the Individual Liberation: "What is taught as making the world lose faith should be examined and removed. Of these, first: with a full mouth, and with noise," and so on; and likewise: "One should guard against what, by one's own examination, or by seeing or hearing, causes the world to lose faith." So it says, and

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** For a small need, if one does not restrain one's bodily and verbal conduct — such as moving the hands greatly — others will lose faith, so one should avoid it. When one needs to signal, one should move the hand slightly and signal discreetly, by finger-snaps and the like.

**Key points.**

- For no need or a small need, one should not stretch out and lift the hand and move it greatly and forcefully.
- When signalling for a small need, one should move the hand slightly and signal by snapping the fingers or by the sound of a pen.
- If there is a need to signal someone far away or quickly and so on, one should, held by mindfulness and awareness, guard against harming others.
- If one acts wrongly through unrestrained conduct of body and speech, the world loses faith, and, by scorning the bodhisattva, [beings] are burned in the lower realms, the hells and so on.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus: one must lie down having placed the right side down. And when going to sleep, it should not be with a thought like "I must sleep until midday tomorrow"; rather, one must strive in yoga, not sleeping in the first and last parts of the night, and, he said, one must sleep in the middle part of the night; so, with awareness, one must sleep in connection with a resolve of the mind: "I will rise quickly at such-and-such a time tomorrow." If one does so, one will wake at the very time one resolved. Those who cannot fall asleep because of conditions such as wind agitation (རླུང་འཕར་སྟངས) should take a pill and then sleep; otherwise, one must rise early. These matters, down to how one carries oneself in everyday conduct, are of great root importance for the teachings. As for how we ourselves practice the Dharma, we back away, saying, "We are not able to practice in the way of the high path." Moreover, even those present-day practices of this kind that can be put into practice are not put into practice: we make light of them and sit around doing nothing at all — this is a very great mistake.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

When sleeping, in the middle watch of the night: if one thinks, "I will lie down just as the Protector of all beings, the buddha, the Blessed One, lay down when he showed the manner of passing into nirvana — in the lion's sleeping posture, with the right side laid on the ground, his head to the north, facing west," it is also a recollection of the buddha, and so it is harmless in this way. And, with the head turned toward whatever direction one wishes in one's mind — east, north, and so on — one lays the right side down, presses one foot upon the other, stretches the right hand beneath the cheek and the left along the thigh, covers the body with the Dharma robe, and lies down; and, with mindfulness and awareness, one should apply the mind to — or entrust one's intention to — the perception of light, the perception of death, and the mind or perception of waking and rising quickly, definitely at the very beginning of lying down, and lie down. If one does so, sleep too becomes virtuous.

In this, one must practice by correlating the activities of one's own lifetime with those of a single day. That is: the very rising of the sun (ད་ནང་, unclear) corresponds to the time of being born; the arrival of midday, to the prime of life; the aging evening, to old age; lying down, to the time of being struck by the illness of death; sleep, to dying; dreams, to the intermediate state (bardo); and waking in the morning, to joining the link with the next life's rebirth. So, in each of those situations, one should set the recognition that it is this and this, and not forget practices such as the precious bodhicitta — so he said.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Just as the Protector of beings, the Buddha, the Blessed One, when he showed the manner of passing into nirvana, his final act, lay down in the lion's sleeping posture with his right side laid down, so too, when the middle watch of the night arrives and one turns toward sleep: one goes out of the dwelling, washes the feet, goes back inside, turns the head toward whatever direction one wishes, such as the north, lays the right side down, places the left foot upon the right, stretches both out, places the right hand beneath the right cheek as a pillow, stretches the left hand out and rests it upon the left thigh, covers the body well with the Dharma robe, and lies down like a lion.

Lying down like a lion is, by its very nature, such that the body does not become slack, mindfulness is not forgotten even during sleep, sleep does not become heavy, and one does not have bad dreams; whereas lying down otherwise brings the faults that are the reverse of those four. It is also said: the lion, among animals, has a powerful body and a lofty mind and overpowers others; so too one who strives in virtue without [idly] sleeping is also highly capable and so on, and overpowers others; therefore one lies down like a lion. The way gods, hungry ghosts, and sensualists lie down is not like that, because they are weak in diligence and lazy, and so have little power to overpower others.

Of the four [factors] in lying down with whatever intention: mindfulness is that one must rely on the mindfulness of virtuous Dharma until one falls asleep, for this enables one to maintain virtuous practice even during sleep as though one were not asleep. Awareness is, when relying on mindfulness, knowing whatever affliction arises and not accepting it but abandoning it. The perception of light is that if one lies down with the mind fixed on the radiance of light, no darkness arises in the mind when one falls asleep. The perception of rising is: in every way, not inclining the mind toward being overwhelmed by thick sleep, and not remaining [in that state] ruined by inactivity (བྱ་བསྙོངས) and laziness and so on, but, having set the aim "I will rise quickly," not savoring the taste of sleep and of the pleasure of lying, and sleeping lightly curled up like a deer — by this, sleep does not become heavy, and one is able to wake without letting the time of rising pass. Therefore, to bring such minds forth, one should definitely apply them at the beginning of falling asleep, at the very first moment. These are [taught] in the sutra:

> When he lies down, he lies down on his right side, places one foot upon the other, covers the body well with the Dharma robes, and lies down with mindfulness and awareness, with the perception of light and the perception of rising; he does not become attached to the pleasure of sleep. Nor is he attached to the pleasure of "སྙེས་པ"; otherwise, he lies down only to the extent needed for the great elements to remain and to sustain life, and

and so on; and

> In the first watch of the night, he purifies the mind of the things that become obscurations, by walking and sitting. Having purified it, in the middle watch he washes his feet outside the monastery, enters the monastery, lays the right thigh down, places one foot beneath the other, and lies down in the lion's posture, with mindfulness and awareness. In the last watch, he rises quickly and purifies the mind of the things that become obscurations, by walking and sitting.

This is based on what was thus taught. "Things that become obscurations" are taught to be such as the five obscurations. Likewise, from the *Letter to a Friend* (བཤེས་སྤྲིང) too:

> You whose nature is reason, spend the whole day / and the first and last parts of the night in repeated practice, / and, so that even sleep is not fruitless, / sleep in between, endowed with mindfulness.

Thus, it is explained that one spends the whole day and the first and last parts of the night passing the time in virtuous activity through walking and sitting, having thoroughly purified the mind of the five obscurations; when the middle part of the night arrives, one sleeps — that is, lies down — and in order that even then it not be fruitless, one sleeps a little, restores body and mind, and, with mindfulness and awareness holding the thought "I will rise quickly and engage in virtue," one must sleep while meditating on the four immeasurables and recollecting the Three Jewels — this is the meaning, as the commentary teaches. The *Spyod bsdus* (སྤྱོད་བསྡུས) also says the same, and the *Pungzang* (དཔུང་བཟང) too:

> Endowed with love, compassion, joy, and equanimity, / recollecting the Sugata together with the Dharma and Sangha, / sleep with palms joined, bowing to Śāriputra and to me. / In this way the mantra-holder exhausts all misdeeds.

So it says; and "to me" means to the noble Vajrapāṇi.

By sleeping in this way, the elements that are to be nourished by sleep are nourished by sleep, and so, the body's elements being restored, it becomes very helpful for making effort in what is virtuous; and not only that, because of motivation it becomes virtuous, so that sleep too becomes meaningful. As the Levels (ས་སྡེ) says: "A monk who is intent upon virtue makes the passing of time meaningful — namely, by sleeping at the proper time"; and from the *Precious Garland* (རིན་ཆེན་ཕྲེང་བ) too:

> Through Dharma one sleeps in comfort / and comes to wake in comfort; / since one's inner self is free of fault, / even one's dreams bring happiness.

So it says. Furthermore, it is taught in the *Bsdu ba* (བསྡུ་བ) that striving in this way, without [idly] sleeping, brings perfection through abandoning the eight things to be abandoned; and again, it is taught in the *Sutra of the Ten Wheels of Kṣitigarbha* (སའི་སྙིང་པོ་འཁོར་ལོ་བཅུ་པའི་མདོ) that such striving is a quality in accord with meditative concentration and so brings about many samādhis.

As for the nature and function of sleep: the *Compendium* (ཀུན་བཏུས) says, "What is sleep? Relying on a cause of sleep, it is the gathering of the mind toward what is virtuous, nonvirtuous, or neutral, toward what is timely or untimely, reasonable or unreasonable; it belongs to the category of delusion; its function is to serve as the basis for letting what should be done slip away." That is: relying on causes such as bodily heaviness, weakness, fatigue, and attending to the sign of darkness, a mind that gathers in the sense consciousnesses, which engage their objects, without independence, is called sleep. Of this, sleeping at the time of the middle watch of the night is timely, whereas sleeping in the first and last parts of the night and so on is untimely; lying down wishing to engage in virtue after restoring the body's elements is reasonable, whereas lying down stirred up by afflictions is unreasonable. Of this, there are two, virtuous and nonvirtuous, depending on motivation; and the function is that sleep of the afflicted kind ruins virtuous activity.

The conduct concerning food explained above and the conduct concerning sleeping taught here: when they are practiced blamelessly (ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་མེད) and meaningfully, it is said that many ways of passing one's life meaninglessly are prevented; since this is of very great importance, I have explained it somewhat at length.
