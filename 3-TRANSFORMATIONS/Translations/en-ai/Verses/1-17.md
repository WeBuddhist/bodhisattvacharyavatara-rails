## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three commentators maintain an absolute consensus on the meaning and doctrinal implications of Verse 1-17, which contrasts the immense but finite fruits of the aspiring mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-praṇidhi-citta*) with the continuous, inexhaustible stream of merit generated by the engaging mind of enlightenment (*bodhi-prasthāna-citta*).

### 1. The Analogy of the Broken Diamond
To illustrate the immense value of the aspiring mind—even when it is completely separated from actual practice—both **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** cite a famous analogy from the *Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra* (also referred to as the *Biography of Noble Maitreya*):
*   **The Diamond's Qualities:** Even a cracked or broken diamond outshines all exceptional, exquisite golden ornaments, never loses the name of a "diamond," and dispels all poverty and distress.
*   **The Application to Bodhicitta:** In the same way, the precious diamond of generating the mind of perfect enlightenment for the sake of omniscience—even when it is separated from the actual practice of the perfections—still completely outshines all the golden ornaments of the qualities of Hearers (*Śrāvakas*) and Solitary Realizers (*Pratyekabuddhas*), never loses the name of a Bodhisattva ("Hero of Enlightenment"), and dispels all the poverty of cyclic existence.

### 2. The Limitation of the Aspiring Mind
Despite its supreme nobility and vast benefits, the commentators emphasize that the aspiring mind has a distinct limitation compared to the engaging mind:
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** explains that while merely generating the aspiring mind toward awakening (without actual practice) brings great fruits—such as obtaining the perfect splendor of gods and humans while wandering in the cycle of life—it does not produce a continuous, uninterrupted stream of merit or virtue.
*   **Ngulchu Thokme** and **Gyaltsab Je** align with this view, clarifying that although the aspiring mind of enlightenment brings great fruits while remaining in cyclic existence, it cannot produce a continuous, uninterrupted stream of merit (*puṇya-dhārā*) in the way that the engaging mind does.

### 3. The Functional Difference: Cause vs. Fruit
The commentators trace this difference in benefits back to the relationship between the mind and the actual practice of the perfections:
*   **Conjoining with Practice:** **Sazang Mati Panchen** explains that the continuous, uninterrupted stream of merit from the engaging mind arises because it is directly associated with the diligent, active practice of generosity and the other perfections.
*   **Seeking the Fruit vs. Accomplishing the Causes:** **Gyaltsab Je** (in his subsequent block, ^1-6-7) provides the philosophical reasoning for this distinction: the aspiring mind is merely an attitude that seeks the fruit (Enlightenment), whereas the engaging mind is actively conjoined with accomplishing the complete, actual causes of Buddhahood.
