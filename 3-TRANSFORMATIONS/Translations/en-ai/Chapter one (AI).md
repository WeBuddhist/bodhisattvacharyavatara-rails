## Chapter One: The Benefits of Bodhicitta

_From Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra (Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)_

---

Paying homage with reverence to the Sugatas — the Dharmakāya and their heirs — and to all who are worthy of veneration, I shall now briefly describe, in accordance with scripture, the practice of entering the discipline of the Sugatas' heirs. ^1-1

What is written here has not been said before, nor do I possess any skill in composition. Therefore I have no thought of benefiting others — I composed this solely to cultivate it in my own mind. ^1-2

This precious human life of leisure and endowment is extremely difficult to obtain. Now that one has the chance to accomplish the purpose of a being, if one does not make use of it for benefit, how will such a perfect opportunity arise again? ^1-3

Through these writings, my faith in virtue may grow stronger for a while, and should another of equal fortune encounter this, perhaps it may also be of benefit to them. ^1-4

Just as a flash of lightning on a dark, cloud-covered night illuminates all for one brilliant instant, so too, by the power of the Buddhas, the mind of virtue and wisdom arises but rarely in the world. ^1-5

Therefore, virtue is always meager, while the power of negativity is immense and unbearable. Except for perfect bodhicitta, what other virtue can overcome it? ^1-6

The mighty sages — who contemplated this over many eons — saw that bodhicitta alone is truly beneficial. Through it, the immeasurable multitudes of beings easily and effortlessly attain the highest bliss. ^1-7

Those who wish to overcome the hundreds of sufferings of existence, who wish to remove the misery of sentient beings, and who wish to experience the many hundreds of joys — let them never abandon bodhicitta. ^1-8

The moment bodhicitta is born, even those wretched ones bound in the prison of samsara are called sons and daughters of the Sugatas, and become worthy of veneration by gods and humans alike. ^1-9

Like the supreme alchemical elixir that transforms base matter into gold, this very body — impure as it is — is taken up and transformed into the priceless body of a Victor. Therefore, hold firmly to what is called bodhicitta! ^1-10

The sole guide of wandering beings, examined with a mind of immeasurable wisdom, is seen to be more precious than jewels. Those who wish to be free from the realm of wandering beings — hold firmly to this precious bodhicitta! ^1-11

All other virtues are like a plantain tree: they yield their fruit and are exhausted. But the tree of bodhicitta continuously bears fruit and grows without end. ^1-12

Even if one has committed the most dreadful misdeeds, one who relies upon it — as upon a hero in great danger — is swiftly liberated. Why then do the conscientious not take refuge in it? ^1-13

Like the fire at the end of time, it burns even the greatest sins in an instant. Its immeasurable benefits were wisely explained by Maitreya to Sudhana. ^1-14

In brief, bodhicitta should be understood to have two aspects: the aspiring mind of enlightenment, and the engaging mind of enlightenment. ^1-15

Just as the distinction between wishing to go and actually going is understood, so too the wise should recognize the distinction between these two in proper order. ^1-16

Although great fruits arise in samsara from the aspiring mind of enlightenment, it does not produce a continuous flow of merit in the way the engaging mind does. ^1-17

From the moment one takes up, with an irreversible mind, this intention to liberate the boundless realm of sentient beings, ^1-18

from that moment forward, even while asleep or distracted by carelessness, a vast and unceasing stream of merit arises, equal to the expanse of the sky. ^1-19

This was spoken by the Tathāgata himself with reasoned justification, as requested by Subāhu, for the sake of beings of lesser aspiration. ^1-20

Even if one merely thinks to relieve a headache of sentient beings, that intention, laden with the wish to benefit, yields immeasurable merit. ^1-21

What need is there to speak of wishing to dispel the immeasurable suffering of each and every being, and to establish each and every one in immeasurable qualities? ^1-22

Who — whether father or mother — has such a wish to benefit others? Do even the gods and sages possess it? Does even Brahmā have it? ^1-23

Beings who, before this, never even dreamed of such a mind for their own sake — how could it arise in them for the sake of others? ^1-24

This extraordinary jewel of the mind — the intention to benefit others — which arises not even for one's own sake in others — this is a wondrous birth unprecedented. ^1-25

The merit of this jewel of the mind — the cause of happiness for all beings, the antidote to the suffering of sentient beings — how can it even be measured? ^1-26

If merely thinking of benefiting others surpasses making offerings to the Buddhas, what need is there to speak of striving for the happiness of all sentient beings without exception? ^1-27

Though they wish to abandon suffering, they run straight into suffering itself; though they desire happiness, in their delusion they destroy their own happiness like an enemy. ^1-28

For those who are deprived of happiness and laden with suffering — one who satisfies them with every joy and cuts away all their suffering, ^1-29

and also dispels their ignorance — where is there a friend like that? Where is there such beneficence? Where is there such merit? ^1-30

If one who merely repays a kindness is praised for that, what need is there to speak of the bodhisattva who does good unsolicited? ^1-31

One who feeds a few people a single meal for just a moment — though offered with contempt, enough for half a day — people regard this as a virtuous act and honor it. ^1-32

But one who gives unceasingly to innumerable beings throughout time the supreme bliss of the Sugatas, fulfilling every wish in their mind — what need is there to describe such giving? ^1-33

If one were to generate an evil mind toward such a generous bodhisattva, the Sage has said that one will dwell in hell for as many eons as there were moments of that evil mind. ^1-34

But if one were to generate a joyful mind toward them, the fruit of that grows even greater. Even when great adversity befalls bodhisattvas, no negativity arises — only virtue naturally increases. ^1-35

To the one in whom this precious jewel of the mind has arisen, I bow down in reverence. To the source of happiness — even those who have harmed it find connection with bliss — I go for refuge. ^1-36

---

_Thus ends the first chapter of the Bodhicaryāvatāra: "The Benefits of Bodhicitta."_
