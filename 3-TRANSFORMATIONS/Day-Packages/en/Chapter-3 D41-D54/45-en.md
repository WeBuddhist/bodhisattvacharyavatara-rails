## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 10 and 11 from the third chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. At some point death will take our body and all our wealth away from us. We will have no say in it. So today, while we still have a say, build up the courage to give everything away to beings completely.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses tell us a story that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Long ago a bodhisattva king called Candraprabha ruled over five continents. Like a parent, he brought benefit and happiness to all beings. Out of jealousy, King Bhimasena sent a brahmin called Ledeva to ask Candraprabha for his head.

The minister offered many heads of gold, silver and jewels in its place. The brahmin would not take them. He wanted nothing but the king's own head.

The king's attendants tried to stop him, but he did not listen. He tied his head to the branch of a tree spirit's tree and gave it to the brahmin. And he made the prayer that, having reached unsurpassed awakening, he would free many beings from suffering.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today I will give one of my own things, or some food, to someone else without holding back.

**Explanation:** *(Generosity Practice)* The tenth verse of the third chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* says this. We should give our body, our enjoyments and all our good to beings without holding back. If today we give what we have to others without craving, our own clinging grows smaller. The ground of mind training that benefits others can then be laid. This brings great happiness to us and to others alike.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Not only one's own body and possessions, but all the roots of virtue gathered in the three times as well, are to be given away from the depths of the heart, holding nothing back, for the sake of accomplishing the aims of all beings.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

The training of the mind here has four parts: the actual training of the mind; setting out the reasons for it; giving the body in particular; and dedicating the fruit of that training to the welfare of others. This verse is the occasion of the first of them, the actual training of the mind. Since this very training is the cause of bodhicitta arising in one's own stream of being, he explains that if the bonds of self-interest are not cut, no intention to benefit others will arise.

As for the thing itself: this body that I hold so dear, and likewise all my possessions — wealth, food, clothing and the rest — and all the roots of virtue gathered in the three times as well, are to be given for the sake of accomplishing the benefit and happiness of all beings without exception. The manner of giving is this: with no miserly begrudging, and with no hope of return or of a ripening result, one gives from the depths of the heart. This one should say aloud, and its meaning should be brought clearly to mind and reflected on again and again.

This, he says, is the key point of the pith instructions. It drives out from its own place the demon of self-grasping, which is the root of samsaric existence; and, never parted from selflessness and emptiness moistened with the sap of objectless compassion, it becomes the supreme path of never abandoning beings, who are like illusions. And as the saying goes — "Roaming haunted places and mountain retreats is outer cutting through (chöd); casting the aggregates away as food is inner cutting through; cutting self-grasping out at the root is cutting through in its real sense" — he identifies three kinds of cutting through: outer, inner, and in its real sense.

He also cites the *Compendium of Trainings*: "The essential points among essential points, by which one does not fall, are of this kind: my body and my possessions, and the virtue produced in the three times — giving them to all beings, and guarding, purifying and increasing them." Accordingly he explains that the four — giving the three of body, possessions and virtue; guarding them against what is adverse; purifying the stains of one's own portion; and increasing them further and further by skilful means — when each is applied in turn, are accomplished as the very nature of the six perfections.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

My body, and my possessions such as food and clothing, and all the virtue as well that is included in the three bases and connected with the three times, are to be given away — with an intention that neither begrudges them nor looks for anything in return — for the sake of accomplishing the aims of all beings, both for now and in the end.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

The body, and likewise the possessions, and all the virtue of the three times as well — what has been done, what is being done and what will be done — are to be given away with an intention that holds nothing back, for the purpose of accomplishing the aims of all beings.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Having given rise to the mind of awakening, then for the sake of working for the welfare of beings: this body of mine — the appropriated aggregates that I hold so dear — and likewise all my possessions, my retinue, my wealth, my food and the rest, and all the roots of virtue gathered in the three times as well, are to be given away completely as generosity, with no miserly begrudging and with no hope of return or of a ripening result, in order to accomplish the aims of all beings without exception: their high status in the higher realms and their definite goodness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

This body of mine, which I hold dear, and likewise whatever possessions and wealth I have — food, clothing, house, horses, cattle and the rest — and whatever virtue there is that is included in the three bases and connected with the three times: all of it is to be given away completely, for the sake of beings, in order to accomplish every aim of all beings, their immediate high status in the higher realms and their ultimate definite goodness. It is given with no begrudging mind that thinks, out of miserliness and attachment, "I will not give this up," and with no expectation whatever of return or of a ripening result. In this way one trains from the very depths of the heart in the intention to give everything away; and this is the way of giving to others all that one has held as one's own.

As for body and possessions: past ones have already perished and so are not there, and future ones are not there now either, so they are not spoken of here. But in the case of virtue, even past virtue has not yet brought forth its ripened fruit, so it is precisely that which one gives rise to the intention to give. And although future roots of virtue do not exist now, one resolves to accomplish vast roots of virtue in time to come as far as one is able, and one trains from now on in the intention: "That too I will cut my clinging to, and give to beings." This is as it is taught in the *Sūtra of Akṣayamati*.

Miserliness belongs to the class of desire, and abandoning it entirely, together with its seed, is found in the arhats of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas too. So merely destroying every trace of miserliness with regard to body, possessions and the rest does not amount to the perfection of generosity. For it to become that, it is not enough merely to clear away the obstacle to giving, the grasping attitude of miserliness: one must also give rise, from the depths of the heart, to the intention to give away to others everything one has — body, possessions and the rest — and train the mind in this again and again.

Further, he explains that to train the intention by emanating in the mind, with heartfelt clear faith, measureless articles to be given, and aspiring to give them to beings, is a means of increasing measureless merit with a small accumulation, and is the generosity of a bodhisattva who has wisdom — as is said in the *Bodhisattva Grounds*.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This very body of mine, which is dear and precious to me, which I need and cannot do without; and likewise whatever possessions I have to enjoy — food, clothing, bedding and whatever articles and things there may be, however many; and all the virtue included in the three times — what was accomplished in the past, what is being accomplished now, and what may be accomplished in the future: all of it is to be given away with the mind, holding nothing back, that is, without even the slightest attachment, clinging or grasping for one's own sake, for the sake of accomplishing the aims of all beings, who have been my parents.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-10]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:kunkhyen-tsona -->
##### The Story of King Candraprabha

As an illustration of the words quoted here — "the body, and likewise the possessions" — for how one gives everything away to others once the mind of awakening has been generated, this story is told. Long ago there was a bodhisattva called King Candraprabha, who ruled Jambudvīpa and held sway over five continents, and who, like a parent, brought benefit and happiness to all beings. Out of jealousy of him, a more powerful king, Bhīmasena, sent a brahmin named Ledeva to ask King Candraprabha for his head. Although the minister offered many heads of gold, silver and precious jewels in its place, the brahmin would not accept them and wanted nothing but the king's own head. The king's attendants tried to stop him, but he did not listen; he tied his head to the branch of a tree spirit's tree and gave his head to the brahmin as a gift, and he made the prayer that, having attained unsurpassed awakening, he would free many beings from suffering.

The goddess of the tree struck the brahmin with the flat of her hand and he fainted. But the king said that in his former lives too he had already given many heads at the foot of that same tree, and he told her not to make an obstacle now to completing the perfection of generosity. His head was cut off; the earth quaked, and the gods sang his praise.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC13_KTB_bo.md#^3-10]]

<!-- story:wangchuk-rinchen -->
##### The Story of Viśvantara

Viśvantara (ཐམས་ཅད་སྒྲོལ་) was so bold in his generosity that he gave the finest of elephants to a brahmin. At this the people grew angry and banished him to the mountains; and even then he gave his chariot, his two young children and his queen to brahmins as well. In the end the people rejoiced, and brought him back into the kingdom.

In short, he says that the ten birth stories that take generosity as their theme — as the *Jātakamālā* has it, "the two merchants, and the hare, and Viśvantara, and Yajña (མཆོད་སྦྱིན་)," and so on — are to be told here, on the occasion of generosity, at whatever length is fitting.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^3-10]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Three — Body, Possessions and the Virtue of the Three Times — Are to Be Given Away Without Holding Anything Back, for the Sake of All Beings

All the commentaries agree in the end that the body, possessions such as food and clothing, and all the roots of virtue included in the three times are to be given away holding nothing back, in order to accomplish the aims of all beings.

###### 2. The Purpose of Giving Is to Accomplish Both Aims of All Beings — the High Status of the Higher Realms (for Now) and Definite Goodness (in the End)

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen speaks of the aims of all beings both for now and in the end, and Sabzang Mati Panchen speaks of the aims of high status and definite goodness; the two come to the same thing.

###### 3. The Manner of Giving Must Be From the Depths of the Heart, With No Miserly Begrudging and No Hope of Return or of a Ripening Result

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam all three agree in explaining that the giving must be done from the depths of the heart, with no hope of return or of a ripening result.

###### 4. Past and Future Body, Possessions and Virtue Cannot Actually Be Handed Over, but It Is Important to Grow Familiar With the Heartfelt Intention to Give Them

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, citing the *Sūtra of Akṣayamati*, that since past body and possessions have perished and future ones do not exist now, "giving" here is not an actual handing over: it is generating the intention to give, and training from now on in the thought of giving.

###### 5. Merely Abandoning Miserliness Does Not Accomplish the Perfection of Generosity; the Intention to Give Everything Must Be Cultivated Again and Again

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that merely abandoning the seed of miserliness is found even in the arhats of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas, so one must give rise again and again, from the depths of the heart, to the intention to give away to others everything one has — body, possessions and the rest.

###### 6. This Training of the Mind Is Itself the Cause of Bodhicitta Arising in One's Own Stream of Being

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that if the bonds of self-interest are not cut, no intention to benefit others will arise; so this training of the mind in giving away body, possessions and virtue is of great importance.

###### 7. The *Compendium of Trainings* Sets Out Four Stages — Giving Body, Possessions and Virtue, and Then Guarding, Purifying and Increasing Them

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden cites the *Compendium of Trainings* and explains that the essential point by which one does not fall is these four — giving the three of body, possessions and virtue, and guarding them, purifying them and increasing them — which, when each is applied in turn, are accomplished as the very nature of the six perfections.

###### 8. The Stories — King Candraprabha Giving Even His Own Head Without Holding Back, and the Ten Birth Stories Illustrating the Giving of Body and Possessions

Kunkhyen Tsonawa and Wangchuk Rinchen both cite stories from the birth stories, and so show the giving taught in this verse carried through to the very end.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches the first stage of the actual training of the mind: one's own body, one's possessions, and all the roots of virtue gathered in the three times are all to be given away from the depths of the heart — with no miserly begrudging and no hope of return or of a ripening result — in order to accomplish the aims of all beings, both for now and in the end.

**Key points.**

- All three — body, possessions and the virtue of the three times — are to be given away, holding nothing back, for the sake of the aims of all beings.
- The purpose of the giving is to accomplish both aims of all beings, the aims of the present and the final aim.
- Even for past and future body, possessions and virtue, which cannot actually be handed over, it is important to train from the heart in the intention to give; and merely abandoning miserliness does not accomplish the perfection of generosity.
- This is the beginning of the actual training of the mind, and it is the cause of bodhicitta arising in one's own stream of being; and according to the *Compendium of Trainings*, by way of giving, guarding, purifying and increasing, it is accomplished as the very nature of the six perfections.
- The birth stories — King Candraprabha giving away even his head, and the ten birth stories — are given as illustrations of the giving taught in this verse carried through to the very end.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

If it is asked: how can something that has no future existence be given away? True enough; and yet it is so, both in order to turn back one's clinging to it while it does exist, and so that by giving it away completely now the pure intention may increase. If it is then thought: surely it is right to keep back at least a little for my own sake? — **by giving away all** of the body and the rest **one attains nirvana, and my own mind is set on accomplishing nirvana;** and at the time of death **everything**, body and possessions alike, **is given up all at once anyway.** Therefore **giving them to beings** is itself **supreme**.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^3-11]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

If one trains to the very end in the habit of a mind of giving, in which the miserly grasping that clings to body and possessions has been uprooted altogether, one can attain the state of omniscience. So by giving everything away one can attain nirvana, that is, the non-abiding nirvana; and since my own mind is one that seeks nirvana, everything must be given away. So he explains.

And even if in one's own mind one has not managed to develop the intention to give, and remains bound by the grasping of miserliness, still one will be parted from these things sooner or later; whether one wants to give them or not, give them one must. So, he explains, one should give them all at one stroke, and give them somewhere great and generous.

At the moment of passing on to the next life too it is certain that everything must be given up. So, since giving everything away comes to the same thing either way, giving to beings is supreme.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Here he explains that there are two reasons why everything must be given away.

The first is this. If, by giving away all three of the bases of self-grasping, one passes beyond sorrow — that is, attains nirvana — then, since this mind of mine, which follows the Buddha and has gone forth, also wishes to accomplish the state of nirvana, everything must be given away. If it is asked whether nirvana is not accomplished by virtue itself: the accumulation of merit and virtue brings the high rebirth that supports the path, and is a mere condition for the realization of the selflessness of persons and of phenomena to arise in one's stream of being. When by abandoning the afflictive obscurations the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas attain the nirvana without remainder, that is held to become like a fire whose fuel is spent, or a lamp gone out. And it is said that apart from the nirvana of the Great Vehicle — the fruit of abandoning the two obscurations together with their habitual tendencies — there is nothing else to be accomplished. Therefore, for accomplishing liberation, worldly virtue bound by self-grasping is of no use; and in particular, for attaining final and perfect awakening one must be free of all compounded virtue. So that too must certainly be given away.

The second reason is this. If it is thought, "Even if nirvana is not to be accomplished, why must one still give?" — then consider that even if one does not give now, at the time of death the body and all one's possessions will go; and one's virtue too, whether it has already been consumed by anger and the like or not, will be exhausted once its ripening has been experienced a single time. So it will all be given up with no purpose and no fruit — it comes to the same thing, and one will certainly have to let it go. Whereas now, at this time when one still has some say in the matter, giving it for the benefit and happiness of beings is supreme: for then it is not squandered, and the supreme fruit is gained.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

To the doubt, "How can a thing that has no future existence be given away?" he answers that although this is true, it is nonetheless so — both in order to turn back one's clinging to it while it does exist, and so that by giving it away completely now the pure intention may increase. And to the doubt, "Is it not right to keep back a little for my own sake?" he answers by explaining that if one gives away everything, body and the rest, one attains nirvana; that my own mind is set on accomplishing nirvana; and that since at the time of death body and possessions are given up all at once anyway, giving them to beings is itself supreme.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

It is right to give away body, possessions and all the roots of virtue for the sake of others; for by giving everything away in this way one is able to attain nirvana, and my own mind, too, wishes to accomplish the non-abiding nirvana. Since giving everything away comes to the same thing either way — since it is given up in any case — giving it to beings is supreme, because from that one attains buddhahood.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

By giving everything away one attains nirvana, and my own mind is set on accomplishing nirvana for the sake of both myself and others. At the time of death body and possessions are all given up — it comes to the same thing either way — so giving them to beings now is supreme, because it turns into a great benefit for both myself and others.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

By giving away all one's body and possessions and gathering the accumulations, one comes to attain the non-abiding nirvana; and my own mind wishes to accomplish the great bliss of nirvana. Moreover, in the end all contaminated things must be given up, and are a cause of fear — it comes to the same thing either way. Therefore giving now to beings whatever each of them may wish, for the sake of benefiting them, becomes the supreme great benefit for both myself and others.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If one thinks, "Surely it is right to keep back at least a little — the body and so on — for my own sake," that is not right. For by cutting the bonds of attachment and clinging to body, possessions and all the roots of virtue, and giving them for the common welfare of beings, one is able to attain nirvana; and my own mind, too, seeks and wishes to accomplish that bliss of the non-abiding nirvana. As the Great Commentary says: "The passing beyond sorrow of all contaminated things, the body and the rest, is liberation, and my intention seeks after that."

In the end, at the time of death, even though one does not wish to give them up, body and possessions are all snatched away at a stroke, with no say in the matter — so it comes to the same thing: they are all given up alike. And virtue that has not been dedicated to the welfare of others is like a plantain tree: its fruit ripens once and then it is exhausted. So if all of it is given up for nothing, it simply runs out. But to cut the begrudging mind from now on and give whatever one likes for the sake of benefiting beings — who are the supreme field of generosity — is the supreme means of accomplishing the great benefit of both myself and others. What, then, is the use of holding on to these things out of miserliness? For from giving everything away comes the merit by which buddhahood is attained, whereas by not giving and keeping it all to oneself one cannot attain nirvana.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

By giving one's own body, possessions and all roots of virtue to beings, one will in time attain, for the sake of other beings, that holy state of the non-abiding nirvana; and since the ultimate goal my mind is set on is to accomplish, for the sake of other beings, that non-abiding nirvana, beings are the holy object of giving. So he explains.

When I myself come to die, everything I have — body, possessions and the rest — will be given up all at once, even if I have not given it away. Therefore, now, at this time when I still have some say in the matter, if I give away and offer to beings my body, my possessions and my roots of virtue, that is supreme: for it will benefit those beings who receive the gift, both for now and in the end, and I myself will in time, for the sake of others, attain nirvana.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^3-11]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **The plantain tree** (ཆུ་ཤིང་གི་དཔེ) → what becomes of virtue that has not been dedicated to the welfare of others. Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that virtue not dedicated to others is like a plantain tree, which dies once it has borne its fruit: its result ripens a single time and then it is exhausted. So he shows that all one's virtue too must be given to beings.
- **A fire gone out, or a lamp** (མེ་ཤི་བའམ་མར་མེའི་དཔེ) → the nirvana without remainder of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that when shravakas and pratyekabuddhas abandon the afflictive obscurations and attain the nirvana without remainder, it becomes like a fire whose fuel is spent, or a lamp gone out; and by this he shows that the mind here, which wishes to attain the non-abiding nirvana of the Great Vehicle, is something different from that.
- **The Tibetans' flight into exile** (བོད་མི་བྲོས་བྱོལ་གྱི་དཔེ) → the meaning of "giving everything away comes to the same thing either way." His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives the example of the flight from Tibet: whether one had a mind of giving to others or not, when the Chinese came in pursuit there was no way not to give things up, and one was left with nothing to carry under one's arm but a single cup. Taking this as the illustration, he explains that at the moment of passing on to the next life too it is certain that everything must be given up; and so, whether one wants to give or not, since it comes to the same thing, one should give it all at one stroke, somewhere great and generous.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. By Giving Away Body, Possessions and All Roots of Virtue One Attains Nirvana, and My Own Mind Wishes to Accomplish Exactly That

All the commentaries agree in the end that it is right to give body, possessions and all the roots of virtue away for the sake of others, and that by giving them one is able to attain nirvana.

###### 2. Even If One Does Not Give Now, at the Time of Death Body and Possessions Are Certainly Exhausted and Must Be Given Up — So Giving Everything Away Comes to the Same Thing Either Way

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that since the ripening is experienced a single time and is then exhausted, it will all be given up with no purpose and no fruit — it comes to the same thing. Zhenphen Jampé Gocha, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama all agree in explaining that in the end, whether one gives or not, at the time of death everything must certainly be given up.

###### 3. Because It Comes to the Same Thing Either Way, Giving to Beings Now, While One Still Has Some Say in the Matter, Is Itself Supreme

Many commentaries explain that giving to beings is supreme because it accomplishes the great benefit of both oneself and others and becomes the cause of attaining the state of a buddha.

###### 4. The Non-Abiding Nirvana of the Great Vehicle Is to Be Accomplished as Something Distinct From the Nirvana Without Remainder of the Shravakas and Pratyekabuddhas

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that the mere accumulation of virtue brings the high rebirth that supports the path; that the nirvana without remainder of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas, which is like a fire gone out, is not what is wanted here; and that in order to accomplish the non-abiding nirvana of the Great Vehicle, free of the two obscurations, all compounded virtue too must certainly be given away.

###### 5. Although Future Things Do Not Exist Now, Giving Them Away From Now On Is Spoken Of in Order to Turn Back Clinging and to Increase the Pure Intention

Zhenphen Jampé Gocha answers that although future things have no existence of their own, one must still give — both to turn back one's clinging to them while they do exist, and so that the pure intention may increase.

###### 6. Virtue Not Dedicated to the Welfare of Others Ripens Once, Like a Plantain Tree, and Is Then Exhausted — So It Too Must Be Given to Beings

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that virtue that has not been dedicated ripens a single time, like a plantain tree, and is then exhausted; so one should not hold on to it out of miserliness, but give it to beings.

###### 7. If One Uproots the Grasping of Miserliness Altogether and Trains the Mind of Giving to Its Furthest Point, One Can Attain the State of Omniscience

His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that if one trains to the very end in the habit of a mind of giving, in which the miserly grasping that clings to body and possessions has been uprooted altogether, one can attain the state of omniscience.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows that by giving away body, possessions and all roots of virtue one attains nirvana, and that my own mind wishes to accomplish exactly that. Even if one does not give now, at the time of death everything must certainly be given up, so it comes to the same thing either way; and therefore all the commentaries agree in the end that giving to beings now, while one still has some say in the matter, is supreme, since it accomplishes the great benefit of both oneself and others.

**Key points.**

- By giving away body, possessions and all roots of virtue one attains nirvana.
- Even if one does not give, at the time of death everything is certainly exhausted and must be given up, so giving everything away comes to the same thing either way.
- Because it comes to the same thing, giving to beings now, while one still has some say in the matter, is supreme, since it accomplishes the great benefit of both oneself and others.
- The non-abiding nirvana of the Great Vehicle is distinct from the nirvana without remainder of the shravakas and pratyekabuddhas; and since virtue not dedicated to others ripens once, like a plantain tree, and is then exhausted, it too must be given to beings.
- If the grasping of miserliness is uprooted altogether and the mind of giving is trained to its furthest point, the state of omniscience can be attained; and the example of the Tibetans' flight into exile shows that, give or not, everything must certainly be given up in the end.
