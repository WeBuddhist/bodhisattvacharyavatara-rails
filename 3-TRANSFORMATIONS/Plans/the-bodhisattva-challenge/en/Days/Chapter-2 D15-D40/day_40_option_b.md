# Day 40 — The half we usually skip

> **Notification**
> **Title:** For one regret, add the missing half: "not again"
> **Detail:** The confession chapter closes. Regret alone is half; the turn completes it.

## Opening

The chapter ends with the piece that actually completes a confession. It is easy to feel bad about something and stop there. Shantideva does not stop there. He names the deed plainly as not good, and then makes the turn: from now on, I will not do it again. Feeling sorry looks back; this looks forward. Both together are what let something finally be set down.

## From the Tradition

Sazang Mati Panchen, a fourteenth-century teacher, gives this last step its technical name: the power of restraint. In his reading of the whole confession, regret is essential but not enough on its own; what seals it is the sincere resolve not to repeat the deed, held, in the verse's bold words, even at the cost of one's life. That phrase can sound severe, so it helps to hear its spirit rather than only its letter. It is not asking you to become perfect or to punish yourself when you slip. It is describing wholeheartedness, a resolve that genuinely means "never again," and keeps facing that direction even after a stumble. A promise made half-heartedly, with one eye on doing it once more, never really clears anything. The turning that Sazang points to is what makes confession more than remorse: you name the thing honestly, and then you actually aim your life away from it.

## Today's Practice

Take one thing you already regret and give it the second half. First name it simply and without excuses: that was not good. Then make the turn, in plain words: from today, I aim not to do it again. Keep the resolve sincere but kind; if you slip later, you renew it rather than abandon it. Naming plus turning, together, is how the weight actually lifts.
