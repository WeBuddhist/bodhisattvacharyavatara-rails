## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:notification -->
### Notification

**Title:** Take one kind wish further than usual  
**Detail:** This wish is rarer than any ordinary love. Today, take one wish all the way.

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's verses look around the whole world for a wish like this one and cannot find it. Not in parents, who love their children. Not in the wise, not in the gods, not even in their highest god. No one, the verses say, has ever wished this for others. Most have never even wished it for themselves, not even in a dream. That is how rare it is.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These three verses clearly show that the awakening mind is a wonderful thing, with nothing else like it in the whole world. In this world, not even parents who love their child, not even gods and sages with special powers, not even Brahma who rests in the four boundless states, have this wish to lead all beings to buddhahood. Why not? Because they have never even dreamed of reaching buddhahood for their own sake — so how could such a wish arise for the sake of others?

This mind, which thinks only of the good of others — a mind that has never even arisen for one's own sake — is like a heavenly wish-granting tree growing out of a heap of filth. When it appears in our own mind, caught as we are in the trap of the afflictions, it is a wonderful, precious jewel of a mind, never seen before, like a wish-fulfilling gem.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice**: Today, I will be kind to someone I find difficult or someone I usually do not like.

**Explanation**: Today's verse asks a beautiful question: who else has a heart this kind? Not even loving parents, wise teachers, or powerful gods have a wish as great and wide as Bodhicitta. By choosing to be warm to a difficult person today, you are practising this special quality—a true kindness for everyone, without any exceptions.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, holding others dearer than oneself from the depths of the heart, one gives rise to the intention that wishes to set all beings equal to endless space in lasting happiness, free from all suffering — to set others in the ultimate, unsurpassed happiness, complete awakening. As for the means of accomplishing this, however much hardship it takes, and however long it may require, if such a good-hearted courage arises that does not have even a hair's tip of discouragement, benefits like these come about — but such a good mind is exceedingly rare to arise, he says. In the world, though a parent's love for their own child is among the greatest kinds of love, even such a good mind is hard to give rise to; and in the gods, sages, Brahmā, and the rest who are renowned as great in the world, there is no way for such a mind to arise at all. This is why the words "does even Brahmā have this?" are framed as a rhetorical question that negates [it].

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-23]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Even the intention held to be good in the world — that of a **father or mother** — extends only to wishing that their own child be long-lived, free of illness, wealthy, powerful, and so on. But apart from a bodhisattva, in **whom** is there a bodhicitta, **a kind wish like this** — thinking, "How good it would be if this being attained the state of buddhahood"? There is none. If one thinks, "They lack it only because they do not know," then even the **gods** endowed with the five higher perceptions, and the **sages** who know the eighteen great fields of knowledge, lack such an intention. And if one thinks, "They lack it because they have no wish to benefit others," then even **Brahmā** — who abides in the four Brahmā-abodes, wishing his subjects to have happiness and be free of suffering — does he have this bodhicitta that wishes [beings] to attain buddhahood? He does not.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Where is there a kind wish like this in the world? In a father or a mother who wishes to benefit their child, or in any other person, where is there such a kind wish — one that accomplishes the supreme happiness [of buddhahood] for them? Even in the gods, and in the sages who speak the truth, and even in Brahmā who abides in the four boundless states, there is no kind wish like this.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-23, 1-24, and 1-25 are explained together; the prose follows all three verse transclusions. As 1-23 is the first verse in the group, the full grouped prose is given here. See Verse 1-24 and Verse 1-25 for cross-references.]*

Whether father or mother, or [any] kinsman and friend — in whom is there a kind wish like this? There is none. Even in the gods and sages, and even in Brahmā, is this present? It is not. As the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* (Ornament of the Mahāyāna Sūtras) says: "Just as the buddhas' heirs abide here with a mind bent on benefit, ripening all beings — so father and mother and kin do not abide [so] well-disposed toward their own children and relations." Since such a mind never arose in those very beings even for their own sake, even in a dream, how could it directly arise for the sake of others? Therefore, this special jewel of a mind — a mind that thinks only of the welfare of beings, one that does not arise even for one's own sake in others — is born as an unsurpassed, wondrous marvel.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whether the father, mother, sibling, or friend who wishes to benefit each worldly [relation] — in what person is there a kind wish like this, one that wishes to set all beings in the great awakening? There is none. And in those who wish benefit in a general way — the world-guardian gods, and the sages who speak the truth, and even Brahmā, who has trained in the four boundless states and is held to be the forefather of all — is there a kind wish like this, or not? There is not.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*[Grouping note: in this commentary verses 1-23 and 1-24 are explained together; 1-23 is the first verse, so the full grouped prose is given here (and includes the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* quotation, which covers both verses). See Verse 1-24 for cross-reference.]*

A father or mother who is well-disposed toward some few beings, such as a son or daughter, or [any] sibling and friend — indeed, any person other than a bodhisattva — in whom is there a kind wish like this, an intention that wishes to attain buddhahood for the sake of all beings? There is none. Moreover, the world-guardian gods and the truth-speaking sages who are well-disposed toward other beings, and likewise a great god such as Brahmā, who abides in the four boundless states of love and the rest — in them too, is there such a special kind wish? There is not. (The great commentary explains that "gods" [here] means the moon-god, water-god, and so on; "sages" means Vasiṣṭha ⚑ and Gautama ⚑; and "even Brahmā" refers to the Vedic [Brahmā].)

Why does such a kind wish not arise in them? Because — never mind [wishing it] for others' sake — in ordinary beings like parents and the rest, even for one's own sake, a special kind wish like this ("May I attain the state of buddhahood") has never arisen, not through daytime reflection, nor even in the fleeting scope of a nighttime dream. If it has never even been an object of a dream, how could it directly arise, in the wakeful state, for the sake of all other beings? It could not; its arising would be a sheer contradiction. Thus the *Ornament* says: "Just as the buddhas' heirs abide here with a mind bent on benefit, ripening all beings — so father and mother and kin do not abide [so] well-disposed toward their children and relations."

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Whether it be a father who loves one dearly and holds one close, or a mother — that is, parents and siblings, endowed with a good heart that holds one dear — in whom is there a beneficial intention, a mind like this generation of bodhicitta, that wishes to set [beings] in, or attain, such a state of unsurpassed, perfect awakening? There is none. In one's innate or accompanying gods, and in the truth-speaking sages, and likewise in a great Brahmā who has trained his mind in the boundless states — even in them, is there such a good intention, wishing to set [beings] in the state of perfect awakening, the fruit? [No] — such a thing they cannot so much as call to mind.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Bodhisattva's Kind Wish Surpasses Anything in the World

This verse shows that the bodhisattva's kind wish is not found even among worldly relations, nor in the gods and sages, nor in a Brahmā who abides in the four boundless states — it surpasses them all. The pivotal point of the examination is the aspiration to set each and every being in complete awakening; that measure and depth is utterly absent in any aspirant who is not a bodhisattva.

###### 2. The Meaning of the *Ornament* Verse

The *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* verse quoted in Ngulchu Thogme and Minyak Kunzang Sonam makes the point vivid: just as the bodhisattva, abiding with a mind bent on benefit, ripens all beings, so parents and kin are not [so] well-disposed even toward their own children and relations. That the bodhisattva's aspiration ripens absolutely all beings to the ultimate is vastly different from the partial, self-cherishing benevolence of the world.

###### 3. How the Position Is Set Out

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden raises and dispels two objections: (a) if one thinks the gods and sages lack the kind wish merely because they do not know [how], [the answer is that] they are endowed with the five higher perceptions; (b) if one thinks Brahmā lacks it merely because he has no wish to benefit others, [the answer is that] Brahmā abides in the four boundless states (love, compassion, joy, equanimity). Yet none of them possesses the bodhicitta-aspiration to bring all beings to the state of buddhahood.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Setting Out the Reason Clearly

Verse 1-24 gives the reason for the incomparability stated in verse 1-23: in those very beings — parents, gods, sages, Brahmā — a mind wishing for buddhahood even for their own sake has never even been dreamed. On that basis, the bodhisattva's aspiration to buddhahood for the sake of others is unprecedented in two respects: both its object (the state of buddhahood) and its scope (for the sake of others) have never before dawned in their minds.

###### 2. The Measure Set by "a Dream"

The measure "never even dreamed" sets the bar as high as it can go: Khenpo Kunga makes it explicit — "not recalled by day, nor even dreamed of by night" — showing that in the mind's most natural, unaltered moments (even in dream) no such thought arises. He seals the point with, "how could such a good intention arise for the sake of other beings?"

###### 3. Shown by the *Ornament* Citation

Sabzang places the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* verse here, at verse 1-24, showing that although the buddhas' heir ripens all beings by his benevolent intent, parents and kin do not abide [so] well-disposed even toward their children. This completes the reasoning — grounded in the fact that ordinary beings have never even dreamed of it — that the bodhisattva's altruistic mind is incomparable.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse gives the reason for the incomparability established in the previous verse (1-23): since ordinary worldly beings have never had the wish for awakening even for their own sake — not even in a dream — it cannot arise in them for the sake of others.

**Key points.**

- The very clean reason — that it does not arise even in a mere dream — establishes the previous verse's claim.
- The *Ornament* shows that the means by which the bodhisattva's power ripens all beings is impossible for other benevolent minds.
- This verse leads into the following verse (1-25) as the fruit that draws out the wondrous instruction.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, [as for] others such as Brahmā and Indra — a mind that wishes to remove immeasurable faults and accomplish immeasurable qualities cannot arise even when aimed at their own sake — that this unsurpassed, special, precious mind should be born in one's own continuum is [what he means by] "born as an unprecedented marvel": it is a way of being amazed at oneself, he says.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-25]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In **others,** such as the gods — never mind [wishing it] for others' sake, since it does **not arise even for their own sake** — this bodhicitta, whatever mind **thinks of the aim** of all **beings, is** a **jewel of a mind:** among [all] thoughts, this **special,** exalted thought, like a jewel — that it **is born** in one's own continuum as **an unprecedented marvel,** something never before arisen — comes about now through hearing the Dharma of the Great Vehicle and through the power of a spiritual friend, and its arising in one's own continuum is like a heavenly wish-granting tree springing up in the human world.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

In bodhisattvas — in whom, [a mind that] does not arise for even an instant in others even for their own sake [now arises] — whatever mind wishes to attain buddhahood for the sake of accomplishing the happiness and removing the suffering of absolutely all beings, this special, exalted jewel of a mind, is born as an unprecedented, wondrous marvel; and others too should hold it as a wonder and strive [for it].

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Whatever mind wishes to attain buddhahood for the sake of all beings — a mind that, in any other person, does not arise even for their own sake, let alone for the sake of others — whoever's continuum it is born in, that [mind] becomes like a special, exalted wish-fulfilling jewel from which all one's needs and wishes arise. This is a wondrous distinction never before present in other beings, born in a bodhisattva; so [such a one] will certainly attain great benefit.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Thus, if in other ordinary beings it does not arise even for a moment — never mind for the sake of others, even for the sake of oneself and one's own kin and friends — then whatever mind thinks solely of the aim of beings, wishing, "May I attain buddhahood in order to remove the suffering of all beings and accomplish their happiness," this special, exalted jewel of a mind, like a wish-fulfilling gem that is the source of all one's needs and wishes, is an unprecedented marvel: since it has never before arisen, it is a wondrous thing. Thinking, "Why has such a special mind been born [in me]?", one should, if it has arisen in one's own continuum, meditate on joy; and, holding its arising in others as a wonder, one should strive to increase that very [mind] in one's own and others' continuums. [Minyak also says that] without bodhicitta, even the direct realization of emptiness does not become the practice of the path of the Great Vehicle.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

As explained above, in others — one's own parents, or gods, sages, Brahmā, and the rest — such an intention, [wishing] to attain the state of perfect awakening even for their own sake, does not arise. Yet in one's own continuum, now, through the power of dependent conditions, this mind — this good intention wishing to attain the state of a perfect buddha for the sake of other beings, called "generating bodhicitta" — arises as, among [all] minds, a mind like a wish-fulfilling jewel, the source of all one's needs and wishes, a special and exalted one. Its arising now, when such a good intention never before arose in my continuum, suddenly, through the power of the compassionate blessing of the divine Three Jewels and the guru — this is the birth of an unprecedented, wondrous thing. For example, if a heavenly wish-granting tree, which grows in the realm of the gods, were suddenly to spring up in a filthy heap of refuse in our own town, everyone would treat it as a marvel; likewise, in the continua of all of us, which since beginningless lifetimes have become like a filthy heap — a tangle of the afflictions: desire, hatred, jealousy, rivalry, pride, and the rest — that the precious jewel of generating bodhicitta, like a wish-fulfilling gem or a wish-granting tree, is born: reflecting on this, one meditates on joy.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Point of Wonder and Marvel

Verse 1-25 concludes the sequence of establishing [bodhicitta's excellence] from verse 1-21 through 1-25: measured against the fact that ordinary beings — never mind the gods, sages, and Brahmā — have never once, even for a moment, wished for buddhahood even for their own sake, the arising of bodhicitta in one's own continuum, wishing for buddhahood for the sake of all beings, is shown to be an unprecedented marvel. Gyaltsab also frames it as an instruction: others too should hold it as a wonder and strive for it.

###### 2. The Point That It Is Born of Compassion and Blessing

Khenpo Kunga especially emphasizes that the arising of bodhicitta in one's own continuum is not one's own effort alone but comes "through the power of the compassionate blessing of the divine Three Jewels and the guru." This shows that this unprecedented birth is not a mere self-produced fruit of practice, but arises through the compassionate blessing of guru and the Three Jewels.

###### 3. The Point of the Instruction

Minyak Kunzang Sonam goes beyond exposition into instruction: if bodhicitta arises in one's own continuum, meditate on joy; hold its arising in others as a wonder too; and strive to increase it in one's own and others' continua. He also says that without bodhicitta, even the direct realization of emptiness does not become the practice of the Great Vehicle path.
