## Today's Verse

> Protectors, I have carelessly Committed numerous misdeeds For the sake of this ephemeral life, Oblivious there is such a danger. ^2-42

> If people who are being led To have their limbs chopped off today Look different than they did before With their parched mouths and bloodshot eyes, ^2-43

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 42 and 43 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. We spend most of our time on this life's living, profit, reputation, and comfort, and along the way we gather all kinds of harmful actions, some deliberately and much of it without noticing. Today's verses remind us how short and unstable this life is, and how much fear the results of bad actions bring at the time of death. They give us the resolve to confess what we have done and to stop trading it away for this life's small returns.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

Khenpo Zhenga, Gyaltsab, and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk all notice the same painful thing in verse 42. Shantideva is addressing these beings as "protectors" — he already knows who they are and what they offer — and he went the way of carelessness anyway. The confession is not that he lacked information. It is that having it changed nothing.

What is worth slowing down for is the phrase "such a danger," because Kunzang Palden does not leave it vague. He separates it into three, in order. First the terror of the cutting-off at the time of death itself. Second, after death, the terror of the bardo. Third, in the next life, the terror of the lower realms. Kunga Wangchuk and Minyak Kunsö set out the same sequence. So this is not one undifferentiated dread; it is a series, and each stage has its own character. His Holiness the Dalai Lama, in the teaching this plan draws on, traces the carelessness itself back to the thought that we are somebody — not taking our own measure honestly.

Kunzang Palden is equally specific about what these misdeeds were for: subduing enemies, looking after our own people, food, wealth, things to enjoy. Sazang reduces it further, to securing a little livelihood for this one life. Set that against the three-stage price and the trade is visibly bad.

Verse 43 then stops arguing and shows. Kunzang Palden describes the man being led out to be maimed, and he does it in detail: the mouth open and dry, the eyes bloodshot and fixed, the head hanging. The Dalai Lama adds the skin going pale and dry. Anyone can recognize the state, and none of it is under the man's control. Kunga Wangchuk supplies the comparison that makes the picture work: if a merely human punishment produces that much terror, what should the ripening of our own actions produce. Gyaltsab gives the conclusion in one line — therefore, feel regret for the misdeeds. Not to freeze us, but because regret is the door the four powers open through, and it is available now.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today I will not lie or mislead anyone for my own small advantage. I will stay straight with people.

**Explanation:** _(Avoiding wrongdoing)_ Piling up wrongdoing for the sake of this life's profit and comfort is exactly the carelessness these verses confess, and it usually shows up in small forms rather than large ones. A convenient untruth to get out of something. Shading a fact so a deal goes our way. Letting someone believe what suits us. Today I will hold my ground in those moments instead. When the easy false answer is right there, I will give the true one, even if it costs me something. These small deceptions harm the other person and quietly harm us, and declining them once makes the next one easier to decline.
