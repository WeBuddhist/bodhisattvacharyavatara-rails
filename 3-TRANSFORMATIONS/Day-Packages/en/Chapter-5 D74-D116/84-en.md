## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the twenty-sixth, twenty-seventh, and twenty-eighth verses of the fifth chapter. Just as a thief watches for a chance to break into a storehouse and steal its treasures, the moment our guard of mindfulness and awareness relaxes, the thieves of the afflictions suddenly break in.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

For example, a thief who plans to steal wealth from a rich storehouse will scout around. They watch where its owner goes and where they stay, looking for the moment to break into that great storehouse. In the same way, the moment our antidote loses even a little of its strength, without our noticing, the afflictions immediately look for their chance.

When afflictions like attachment and hatred find their chance and enter our mind stream, they steal away the wealth of our good deeds. In particular, if even a single instance of anger arises, all the roots of goodness gathered over a long time can be completely destroyed.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, the moment I notice my mindfulness and awareness slipping, or the afflictions watching for a chance, I will restore mindfulness at once and guard my mind.

**Explanation:** *(Ethics Practice)* Because the thieves of the afflictions are always looking for a chance to harm us, even a small relaxing of the guard of mindfulness and awareness lets them steal away our roots of goodness. So the very moment we notice mindfulness and awareness slipping, we must pay close attention to our own mind. This blocks the afflictions' chance and protects the very life-root of our good deeds. This practice keeps the good deeds in our own mind stream from being lost, and it brings great benefit for eventually reaching happy realms and the path to liberation.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that if, at the moment one encounters whatever basis for a downfall, one is unable to catch it with mindfulness and awareness, then even if one knows, once already tainted by the fault, that it is indeed a fault, there is nothing left to be done about it. There is a real danger of ending up as the saying goes: "When one has the vow, there is no discipline; when there is discipline, one no longer has the vow." So, from the very moment there is a danger of encountering a basis for a fault, if one fails to catch it with mindfulness and awareness, then, just as we can see for ourselves in this very life, even many who are learned, who have faith, and who are diligent become entangled in the taint of downfall through the fault of lacking awareness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In the same way, ethical discipline too can be squandered. That is, many persons who have the learning that correctly knows what is to be adopted and rejected with regard to the training they themselves are undertaking, who have faith that trusts in cause and effect, and who diligently apply themselves with enthusiasm to what is to be adopted and rejected, may nonetheless, on some occasions, have the fault of lacking awareness take hold in their mind-stream, so that their ethical discipline degenerates and they become entangled in the taint or stain of downfall.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Many who are learned, who have faith in the Dharma, and who diligently apply themselves to virtuous conduct may nonetheless, through the fault of lacking awareness with regard to what is to be adopted and rejected taking hold, have their mind-stream become entangled in the taint of downfall; therefore one should make effort to guard awareness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Many who are learned, who have faith, and who diligently and respectfully apply themselves to the training may nonetheless, through the fault of lacking the awareness that individually examines one's own conduct taking hold, become entangled in the taint of downfall — so he says.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Many persons who have the wisdom arisen from hearing, who have faith trusting in cause and effect, and who diligently apply themselves to virtue with joy may nonetheless, on some occasions, have the fault of lacking the awareness that examines the actions of the three doors before engaging in them take hold and remain in their mind-stream, so that they become entangled in the taint or stain of root and branch downfalls — just as the Vinaya states: "Not knowing is the cause from which downfall arises."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Many conscientious persons who have the wisdom of extensive learning, who have faith trusting in the Three Jewels and in cause and effect, and who — respectfully and joyfully — diligently engage in what is to be adopted and rejected, both in virtuous conduct generally and in the training specifically, may nonetheless have the fault of lacking the awareness that guards against unfavorable conduct by examining and investigating, again and again, however one's conduct of the three doors turns out, take hold within their own mind-stream. This fault alone becomes tainted with the wrongdoing that is blameworthy in one's own eyes and with the stain of the taint of downfall, so that even excellent ethical discipline degenerates and is squandered. The word "even" in the verse gathers in the sense that not only does one lacking learning, faith, and the rest degenerate, but even one who has them may likewise degenerate. Thus, although a downfall does not arise, respectively, through each of the three kinds of unconscientiousness with regard to engaging in what is to be adopted and rejected — not knowing what is to be trained in, lacking respect for the training, and not engaging in adoption and rejection — even one who possesses all three forms of conscientiousness with regard to extensive learning, faith, and engaging in what is to be adopted and rejected may nonetheless have a downfall arise through the power of an affliction that has turned into either non-awareness or an afflicted form of wisdom, exactly as the Vinaya explains: "Not knowing is the cause from which downfall arises." ⚑ He himself makes clear that none of the other commentaries explicitly connects "diligently applying oneself with respect to the training" to conscientiousness — it is simply left there in the verse.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Learned" here means that, through study, one has extensive learning about what is to be adopted and rejected — the benefits of virtue, the faults of wrongdoing, and the like. Many persons, too, have faith that fits their circumstance — trust in the ripening of actions, or clear faith in the Three Jewels — and diligently apply themselves, by nature joyful in mind, both to accomplishing virtue and to the antidotes that abandon wrongdoing. Yet on some occasions the fault of lacking the very awareness that examines and investigates one's own bodily and mental continuum takes hold, so that, through the force of circumstance, whatever precepts of training one holds in one's continuum — all of them — come into conflict, giving rise to misconduct and becoming entangled in the taint of downfall. In brief, this teaches that a downfall can occur with respect to any of these: the prātimokṣa vows of a lay practitioner, novice, or fully ordained monastic; the bodhisattva vow of generating the awakening mind; or the vows of secret mantra.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-26]]

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. However Much Learning, Faith, and Diligence One Has, Lacking Awareness Degrades Discipline

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains, in terms shared by all the commentaries, that however much one has learning, faith, and diligence, if awareness is lacking, one's ethical discipline can degenerate on occasion and become entangled in the taint of downfall.

###### 2. This Fault Applies to All Three Sets of Vows, per the Vinaya's Own Statement

Sabzang Mati Panchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both teach that this applies to all three sets of vows — the prātimokṣa, the bodhisattva vow, and secret mantra — and cite the Vinaya's statement that "not knowing is the cause from which downfall arises."

###### 3. ⚑ Minyak Kunzang Sonam Distinguishes Three Causes of Downfall and Connects Them to the Three Forms of Conscientiousness

Minyak Kunzang Sonam distinguishes the three causes of downfall (not knowing, lack of respect, and unconscientiousness) and explains this verse in relation to the three forms of conscientiousness, while himself pointing out that the other commentaries do not explicitly connect "diligently applying oneself with respect to the training" to conscientiousness.

###### 4. Merely Taking a Vow Is Not Enough — It Must Be Guarded Well with Awareness

His Holiness the Dalai Lama first cites the saying, "When one has the vow, there is no discipline; when there is discipline, one no longer has the vow," to make clear that merely taking a vow is not enough — one must guard it well with awareness.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This root verse clearly teaches that a person of extensive learning, of great faith in the Buddha's teaching, and of constant diligence in virtuous conduct will nonetheless become tainted by the fault of downfall — a fault contrary to the vows of ethical discipline — if lacking the "awareness" that continually examines the conduct of one's own body, speech, and mind. Awareness is thus shown to be the indispensable guardian that protects one's own vows and ethical discipline.

**Key points.**

- **Applying to all three sets of vows.** Sabzang Mati Panchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and others explain that the fault taught here applies to all three sets of vows — the prātimokṣa, the bodhisattva vow, and secret mantra — as the Vinaya states, "not knowing is the cause from which downfall arises." Even one who has extensive learning, faith, and diligence will have their continuum divided by the taint of downfall if the mindfulness of awareness is absent.
- **The point about vows and discipline.** As His Holiness the Dalai Lama states, "When one has the vow, there is no discipline; when there is discipline, one no longer has the vow" — merely taking a vow is not enough; one must continually examine one's own continuum through awareness and join this with the practice of discipline.
- **⚑ Differing positions among the commentators.** Minyak Kunzang Sonam, taking as his basis the three causes of downfall taught in the Vinaya (not knowing, lack of respect, and unconscientiousness), explains the root verse's "diligently applying oneself" in direct connection with the practice of conscientiousness. However, the other commentators leave the direct connection between diligent application and conscientiousness unstated.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Following the structure of the fifth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, this verse teaches how the thieves of the afflictions — which arise for one lacking awareness — follow upon the decline of mindfulness, and thereby plunder the good and beautiful wealth of merit we have worked hard to accumulate. Just as a person who has amassed much wealth is nonetheless reduced to poverty when thieves plunder it, so too one who is diligent in virtuous action but lacks mindfulness and awareness has the essence of merit stolen by the thieves of the afflictions, and in the end faces the grave danger of a lower rebirth.

**Key points.**

There are two main points on which the commentators' treatment of this verse's essential meaning differs:

- **How the decline of mindfulness and awareness steals merit.** Following the decline of mindfulness, the thief of the afflictions lacking awareness follows close behind and plunders the root of virtue in one's own continuum. Here, Ngulchu Thogme cites the Great Commentary of Ācārya Prajñākaramati's exegesis, explaining the reason merit is stolen as ⚑ "because one lacks the protector of virtue" — that is, because the wealth of merit accumulated on the path of virtue is left unguarded, one goes to a lower rebirth.
- **A difference in how the outline is drawn.** Although most commentators gather this verse within the fault of lacking awareness, ⚑ as Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, some commentaries do not treat this verse (5-27) and the next (5-28) as merely belonging to the topic of lacking awareness, but instead set them apart specifically under "the fault of lost mindfulness."

In brief, this verse teaches the vital point that, in training in the bodhisattva's conduct, mindfulness and awareness must be guarded together, each relying on the other.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that if one is unable to sustain mindfulness and awareness continuously in this way, this multitude of nearby afflictions — such as the thief-like forgetfulness — seeks its chance. The moment the antidote's strength weakens even slightly, without being caught, they immediately seek their opportunity. And once they find that opportunity, what do they do? They cause the virtue previously accumulated in one's own continuum to decline, and, as its result, they also annihilate the very life of a good rebirth.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This multitude of afflictions, such as attachment and hatred, which are like thieves, first assess whether or not they can succeed against a traveler or the like, watch for their chance, and, if they can succeed, seek the moment — whether by day or night, or when the guard is inattentive — again and again, in just the same way, they constantly watch for their chance against our own continuum, seeking the moment to do harm; they are shown to be just like thieves. So whenever the afflictions such as attachment and hatred find an opening in one's own continuum, they steal the wealth of virtue previously accumulated, and, as its result, they also annihilate the very life of a good rebirth, so that one does not attain the state of a good rebirth.

If one fails to guard oneself with awareness, this multitude of thieves, the afflictions, seeks the opportunity to steal the wealth of virtue; and once that opportunity is found, they steal virtue, and, as its result, they also annihilate the very life of a good rebirth and of liberation. ##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

When awareness declines, mindfulness also declines; and once both have declined and there is no one to guard against the afflictions, this multitude of thieves of the afflictions — non-awareness and the like — like a flesh-eating predator, constantly and continuously watches for its chance and seeks the opportunity to cause harm, purely in order to steal the wealth of virtue. The moment any opportunity for entry is found, it steals whatever wealth of virtue has been accumulated as a cause, and, as its result, it also annihilates the very life of a good rebirth and of liberation. Therefore, having considered the many faults of lacking awareness, one should make diligent effort to guard awareness. ⚑ As for the phrase rendered "like a flesh-eating predator" in the Great Commentary: he states that in the Sanskrit, a single word is rendered in Tibetan in two ways, as both "thief" and "flesh-eating predator." As for non-awareness: the *Abhidharmasamuccaya* states, "What is non-awareness? It is the afflicted counterpart of wisdom — that is, wisdom conjoined with the afflictions — which engages, without awareness, in the conduct of body, speech, and mind, and thereby serves as the basis for downfalls."

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The afflictions — attachment, aversion, jealousy, competitiveness, pride, and the like — are just like thieves or bandits; this multitude of them seeks its opening and its chance. For example, just as a thief, intending to take the storehouse-wealth of a wealthy person, scouts and investigates wherever that person goes and wherever they stay, seeking the chance to enter that person's great storehouse whenever possible — in just the same way, once these afflictions such as attachment find their opening and enter one's own mind-stream, they steal away the wealth of virtue as well; and in particular, if even a single instance of anger arises, it is said that all the roots of virtue accumulated over a long span of time are exhausted and rendered non-existent. Thus all this wealth of virtue is stolen, and even the very life — which brings about the attainment of a support in a good rebirth as a god or human in the higher realms — is likewise annihilated: such a danger exists. Therefore, one must continuously rely upon that awareness which examines and investigates, again and again, the state of one's body and mind; and if one is unable to rely upon it, one incurs this fault.

#### Metaphors and Examples

**The Thief of the Afflictions — The Robber Watching for a Chance.** This extends the thief-image of verse 5-27 to a single further point: just as a thief scouts whether or not he can succeed in entering a wealthy person's storehouse, watches for his chance, and, the moment that chance is found, steals the wealth at once, so too this multitude of the thieves of the afflictions, the moment it finds an opening created by even a slight decline in the strength of the antidote of awareness, steals away all the wealth of virtue and also cuts off the very life of a good rebirth — as Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both make clear.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Thieves of the Afflictions Constantly Watch for Their Chance to Steal Virtue and Cut Off the Life of a Good Rebirth

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden lays out, in terms shared by all the commentaries, that this multitude of the thieves of the afflictions constantly watches for its chance and seeks its opportunity; the moment the strength of the antidote of awareness declines even slightly, it steals the wealth of virtue and also annihilates the very life of a good rebirth.

###### 2. A Single Instance of Anger Can Exhaust Roots of Virtue Gathered Over a Long Span of Time

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk makes clear the great gravity of the danger: even a single instance of an affliction such as anger arising can, in an instant, exhaust all the roots of virtue accumulated over a long span of time.

###### 3. ⚑ Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites the Abhidharmasamuccaya's Definition of Non-Awareness

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*'s definition of non-awareness, making clear that it is defined as "wisdom conjoined with the afflictions, which engages, without awareness, in the conduct of body, speech, and mind, and thereby serves as the basis for downfalls."

###### 4. ⚑ A Translation Note on the Word Rendered "Flesh-Eating Predator"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam also makes clear a point of translation regarding the word rendered "flesh-eating predator": he explains that a single Sanskrit word has been translated into Tibetan in two ways, as both "thief" and "flesh-eating predator."

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse explains how the multitude of the afflictions, like a band of thieves bent on stealing our wealth of virtue, constantly seeks the opening created whenever the guardian of mindfulness and awareness relaxes, so as to plunder the roots of virtue in our own continuum and, in the end, annihilate even the very life of a good rebirth. It thereby teaches the vital point that one must continually rely on conscientiousness, and on mindfulness and awareness.

**Key points.**

- **The harm done by the afflictions.** The afflictions constantly watch for their chance; as Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk's commentary explains, even a single instance of an affliction such as anger arising can annihilate, in a single instant, roots of virtue accumulated over a long span of time — so the harm done by the afflictions is even more to be feared than that of an ordinary thief who comes later.
- **The nature of lacking awareness.** As Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, citing the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*, lacking awareness is wisdom conjoined with the afflictions, which engages, without awareness, in the conduct of body, speech, and mind, and which serves as the basis for every downfall.
- **⚑ A first point of differing exegesis.** Citing the sub-commentary of Ācārya Prajñākaramati, Ngulchu Thogme explains the reason the afflictions steal virtue as "because one lacks the protector of virtue" (that is, one lacks a guardian) — making clear the reason why, without being caught by the guard of mindfulness and awareness, the accumulation of virtue is stolen away.
- **⚑ A second point of differing exegesis.** As Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, within Ācārya Prajñākaramati's commentary, the Sanskrit root word here carries a play on two senses — "thief" and "flesh-eating predator" — showing that the image conveyed is not merely that of an affliction as a thief stealing virtue, but also carries the more fearsome sense of a flesh-eating predator that destroys life itself.
