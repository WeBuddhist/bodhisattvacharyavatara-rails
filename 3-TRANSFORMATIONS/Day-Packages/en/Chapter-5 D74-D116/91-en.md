## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the forty-fourth, forty-fifth, and forty-sixth verses of the fifth chapter. As soon as we notice ourselves drawing lines on the ground for no reason, we must stop right there.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

> If we start the next task before the first is finished,
> neither is done, and this leads to tiredness.
> If we lift both feet without planting one,
> it is a cause of falling.

The meaning of this example is as follows. If we lift both feet without planting one on the ground, we will fall. In the same way, if we take up another task before the first one is finished, neither task will be done. We will only become tired.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, if attachment arises while I look at my phone or chat about nothing useful, I will let it go right away.

**Explanation:** *(Ethics Practice)* We do not need to stay away from idle talk and shows completely. But if we cling to them, our mind becomes distracted. So if we let go of that clinging when these things come up, we can keep our mind peaceful.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that, in carrying out tasks, for those kinds of tasks that must be done in sequence, whatever has already been begun should first be brought to completion. If that happens, what was begun can be properly brought to a close, and once it is finished, starting the next one afresh will go well. Otherwise, if we spread out many different jobs, there is a danger that in the end not one of them will have come to anything. If one begins a task and proceeds step by step — first settling one matter well, so that it is of good quality, and only then carrying out other tasks — then, as the verse says, "everything is done well": having properly finished the earlier one, one can gather up all the strength one has and put it into the later. Otherwise, without cutting through the thread of the earlier one, there is a danger that the later one too will not come together properly. So he teaches. In this way, if one relies on mindfulness and awareness in the proper manner, the secondary affliction of non-awareness will not increase.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-44]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

If one acts in this way, both the earlier and the later are done well — that is, both the earlier and the later are brought to completion. If one does not act in this way but instead, with the earlier unfinished, takes up the later or something else, neither the earlier nor the later will be accomplished, just as in the lines:

> Unless one foot is left on the ground,
> lifting both is a cause of falling.

In the same way, if one first takes hold of the earlier properly and then enters the later, in sequence, then, of the twenty secondary afflictions, the very secondary affliction called "non-awareness" will not increase if one acts like this.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-44]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Editorial note: the rail records that Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen treats verses 5-42 through 5-44 in one continuous prose run; the explanation specific to this verse reads as follows.)*

For whatever task must be learned in a fixed order: having first considered it and begun it, one should not think of anything else; with the thought of devoting the mind to the giving one began with first, one should for the time being accomplish that very giving. By training in the path in a fixed order in this way, everything will be done well; otherwise, if the order is reversed, neither the earlier nor the later will be brought to completion. The secondary afflictions of non-awareness that come from not knowing the sequence will not increase if one knows it in this way; therefore one should become skilled in the order of the path.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

If so, both the earlier and the later will be done well; otherwise, neither the earlier nor the later will be brought to completion. If one accomplishes virtue step by step in this way, the secondary affliction of non-awareness will not increase.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

If one acts earnestly in this way, everything one has undertaken will be done well; otherwise, if, with the earlier unaccomplished, one enters the later, neither of the two will be accomplished. The secondary affliction of non-awareness, which belongs to the category of delusion, likewise will not increase if one accomplishes things in sequence. Alternatively, as the Indian exemplar and the Great Commentary read — "Even the afflictions of non-awareness will increase if it is so" — if, without accomplishing the one, one engages in the other, then afflictions will arise and those already arisen will also increase, as in these lines:

> If one takes up another before the first is finished,
> neither is accomplished — a cause of weariness.
> If one lifts both feet without having planted one,
> it is a cause of falling.
> — Cāṇakya

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-44]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If one enters the later task before the earlier one first entered upon is finished, it becomes an obstacle to bringing the training to completion; therefore … with the very thought of devoting one's mind to, and being intent upon, whatever task was begun earlier, one must for the time being accomplish just what was begun first, through effort, and bring it to completion. The words "for the time being" show that, once that earlier task has been finished, one may also take up other tasks. If one becomes skilled in the sequence of tasks in this way, one will have trained well in all tasks, earlier and later; if one acts otherwise, they will not be accomplished — as in the lines:

> Whatever one has begun first,
> that very first thing should be accomplished.
> If so, everything is done well;
> otherwise, neither will be accomplished.
> — the *Ratnāvalī* (ནོར་བུའི་ཕྲེང་བ, "Garland of Jewels")

and:

> If one takes up another before the first is finished,
> neither is accomplished — a cause of weariness.
> If one lifts both feet without having planted one,
> it is a cause of falling.
> — Cāṇakya

Likewise, as for the secondary affliction of non-awareness, which belongs to the root affliction of delusion and arises from not knowing the order of tasks as it is: if one trains without error in the order of the path, it will not arise in one's continuum or increase; therefore one should become skilled in how to train in the path in sequence. The need to train in sequence applies to those kinds of trainings that must be learned in a fixed order; for virtues of equal strength, which need not be learned in a fixed order, whatever was begun first must be accomplished first.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-44]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

For example, suppose one first completes hearing and reflecting on the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* from beginning to end, and only then proceeds, in order, to hearing, reflecting, and putting into practice other texts. If one engages step by step in this way, both the earlier and the later will have been done well. If it is not like this — if one leaves the earlier unfinished and goes on to one thing after another — there is a danger that neither the earlier nor the later will be accomplished. But if one engages in the later only after the earlier has been accomplished, in sequence, then the secondary affliction through which one engages without awareness will likewise not increase. So he explains.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree That Engaging in the Later Only After the Earlier Is Well Accomplished Makes Both Well Done

This verse concludes the fixed-order instruction of verse 5-43. The commentaries agree that if one engages in the later after the earlier has been well accomplished, both the earlier and the later tasks are accomplished well, whereas if the order is reversed, neither is accomplished.

###### 2. The Two-Feet Simile and Cāṇakya's Verse Show the Fault of Reversing the Order

The simile of falling when one lifts both feet at once without planting one, together with the quotation from Cāṇakya, makes clear the fault of reversing the order.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Adds That Training in Fixed Order Applies Only to Certain Kinds of Training

Minyak Kunzang Sonam makes a side distinction: the need to train in a fixed order applies to those kinds of trainings that must be learned in a fixed order; as for virtues of equal strength, what was begun first must be accomplished first.

###### 4. ⚑ Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Note a Difference Between the Root Text's "Do Not Increase" and the Indian Exemplar's "Will Increase"

Both Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam note, as a side point, that there is a difference in what the root text's reading "do not increase" and the Indian exemplar's reading "will increase" point to.

<!-- sub:divergences -->
#### Divergences Among Commentators

- ⚑ **Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam, on a variant reading of the last line:** Most root-text witnesses read "do not increase," explaining that when virtue is accomplished step by step, the secondary affliction of non-awareness does not increase. The Indian exemplar and the Great Commentary, however, read "will increase," explaining that when one engages in a later task without having accomplished the earlier, the mind's engagement is unstable, and so the afflictions of non-awareness — those that enter, and those already entered — will increase. Both commentators flag the variant as fitting the argument at least as well as, or better than, the received reading.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** If tasks and trainings are accomplished step by step, in a fixed order, both the earlier and the later are brought to a good completion, and the secondary affliction of non-awareness does not increase. If the order is reversed and one engages in something else without having brought the earlier to completion, there is a danger that neither the earlier nor the later will be accomplished, and that the effort will be wasted.

**Key points.**

- **Skill in the order of tasks.** The commentaries are unanimous that, by becoming skilled in the order of tasks and accomplishing them in sequence, both earlier and later are done well; but if the order is reversed, neither is brought to completion.
- **The two-feet simile.** To show the fault of reversing the order, the commentaries give the simile — "unless one foot is left on the ground, lifting both is a cause of falling" — and Cāṇakya's verse — "taking up another before the first is finished, neither is accomplished, a cause of weariness."
- **Finish what was begun, then give the next everything.** If one begins a task and proceeds in sequence, one can then gather up all the strength one has for the later task; and for virtues of equal strength, whatever was begun first must be accomplished first.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree That Clinging to Idle Talk and Spectacles, Not Mere Engagement, Is What Is to Be Abandoned

The commentaries agree that what is to be abandoned is clinging and attachment to idle talk (many kinds of unconnected talk) and to the many kinds of amazing spectacles; merely engaging in them is not prohibited.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Specify an Exception: Protecting the Minds of Others

Both Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk specify, as a special exception, the situation in which one must engage in various kinds of talk and spectacles in order to protect the minds of others — the sick, small children, the elderly, and so on.

###### 3. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen Teaches Abandoning Attachment When One Engages from Need or for Others' Sake

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen teaches that attachment is to be abandoned when one comes to engage because there is a need or for the sake of others; he further sets out that one should not engage in such talk and the like from the very beginning.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Also Explains These Words Through Mind Training

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk also explains these words by way of mind training: seeing all phenomena as like an illusory spectacle.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* teaches that even when an occasion arises in which one must engage in unconnected idle talk and in various amazing spectacles, what must be abandoned is the clinging and attachment to them; merely engaging in them is not prohibited.

**Key points.**

- **Attachment, not engagement, is the target.** The commentaries are unanimous that the main thing to be abandoned is clinging and attachment to idle talk and amazing spectacles, not the bare fact of engaging in them.
- **An exception for protecting others' minds.** An exception is stated for engaging in idle talk and spectacles in order to protect the minds of others — the sick, small children, the elderly, and so on.
- **Talk of kings and thieves; song, dance, and conjuring.** In the case of unconnected talk such as talk of kings and thieves, and of song, dance, and conjuring tricks, if one comes to engage in them because there is a need or for the sake of others, attachment must be abandoned.
- **Seeing all phenomena as an illusory spectacle.** Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk also explains the key point of training the mind by seeing all phenomena as like an illusory spectacle.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

On occasions when one has to associate with people — for example, when one arrives, as a person of standing, at a banquet to which one has been invited by the host, or when one has gone to a place where a spectacle is being watched, or in other circumstances in which there is a reason one must go — when one meets the conditions of social bustle, one should take care that the mind of attachment or aversion does not arise. Apart from that, if actions such as digging the ground, cutting grass, and drawing lines on the ground, for which there is no special reason or need, come out of their own accord, then at once one must recall the Buddha's training and leave off.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-46]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Because bodhisattvas, lamas, tulkus, and the like must guard against giving others cause to lose faith, when, without any great purpose or need, one wishes to dig the ground with one's fingers or a stick; to cut grass and the like because one cannot keep one's hands still; or to draw lines on the ground — that is, to draw various different kinds of figures on the ground — one should recall the trainings laid down by the Blissful One that forbid these things, and, fearing that the fault of contradicting the training will lead to the lower realms, one should drop it "in that very moment" — that is, immediately.

If, without any great purpose or need, one comes to dig ground that has been compacted for three months and is undamaged; to cut a stretch of fresh green grass with one's fingernails or the like; to draw lines on the ground; to pull out stakes; and so on, one should recall the trainings laid down by the Blissful One in the manner of forbidding these things and, fearing the fault of a downfall, drop it in that very moment.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Likewise, if even a monastic bodhisattva — and how much more so a householder bodhisattva who is not a monk or the like — without any special purpose or need happens, without examining, to do certain kinds of pointless activity — digging the ground; cutting fresh grass with the fingernails and the like; drawing various lines on the ground ("ground-figures") with hands, feet, or wooden implements; driving stakes into the ground or pulling them out; treading or stamping on it (ཞལ་བ); working on stone, wood, and so on — then at once, recalling the trainings that forbid such things — "The Blissful One has said, in the Mahayana bodhisattva collection, that one should abandon whatever does not become for the sake of sentient beings" — and fearing the fault of contradicting them, one should "in that very moment" — that is, in that very instant — drop those pointless activities. If it is asked, "Why are digging the earth and so on taught?": it is because, in the Vinaya, the training bases such as killing and drinking alcohol are taught as common to householders and to the ordained, whereas digging the earth and so on are taught for monks and others and not taught for householders; and it is in order to show that, just as ordained bodhisattvas must guard these, so too householder bodhisattvas must guard them. So it is said in the Detailed Exposition.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-46]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Without any purpose or need in accordance with the Dharma — such as building a temple or a stupa — one digs the ground or turns over stones; one cuts grass, trees, and the like at one's whim, not for such purposes as spreading tufts of grass on muddy ground; likewise, for no need, one draws lines on the ground and the like; and, out of a frivolous state of mind, one damages by burning in fire or cutting with a blade the seeds that are certain to sprout — grains and so on, and the fruit of trees and so on. If one has done such things, then at that time, recalling the training that the Complete Buddha, the Blessed One, the Blissful One, laid down in the manner "such things must not be done," and with fear or dread that, because this contradicts the training laid down by the Buddha, one will be born in the lower realms in a future life, one should at that very moment drop it straight away and discard it.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC19_MKS_Sariputra-Maudgalyayana -->
##### Nyégyal and Pangnékyé (Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana) at the Festival

Minyak Kunzang Sonam, in connection with the instruction that where there is a need one should engage in conduct that is not pointless with a sense of disenchantment, having abandoned attachment — at the point where this is stated chiefly as "not stirred up by hatred and the like" — sets down directly a life story of the two supreme disciples from before they went forth, as it appears in the Vinaya scripture. It is told that Nyégyal, who became Śāriputra, and Pangnékyé, who became Maudgalyāyana, were famed from childhood for their mastery of the Vedas, and that each had five hundred students of his own. Once, when a great festival took place in that land, the two youths, out of regard for their parents, took their seats in the assembly. While the performers sang and the people beat out the rhythms of the dance, most of the crowd saw the two boys sitting with their senses turned back, and said, "Are these two fools, or are they two whose minds cannot be disturbed?" Pangnékyé asked Nyégyal, "Do you see the dance and the song and the music?" and Nyégyal answered:

> With dead hides and dead sinews,
> and with mortal laughter and coquetry —
> in a gathering of people who will die,
> what wise person would be heedless?

Likewise, when Nyégyal in turn asked Pangnékyé whether he saw the dance and the song, he replied:

> All adornments are burdens;
> all dances are mockery;
> all songs are idle foolery;
> all forms are impermanent.

*(Translator's note: the two middle lines of this second verse read གན and ཀླུ in the rail's Tibetan, with the words ཐོ་འཚོམ་པ and ཀྱལ་བ. The two opening words appear to be variant spellings of གར (dance) and གླུ (song), in keeping with the dance and song in the festival scene; the renderings "mockery" and "idle foolery" are provisional. Flagged for domain-specialist review.)*

It is shown that, although both saw the outward festivity of the gathering, they focused inwardly on the meaning of impermanence, and a sense of disenchantment arose in them. This very story from the Vinaya scripture was used by Minyak Kunzang Sonam, and set down directly, as an illustration of the point that "even where there is a need, one should engage not stirred up by attachment and clinging, but with a sense of disenchantment."

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree That Pointless Digging, Cutting, and Drawing Should Be Dropped in That Very Moment

The commentaries agree that if one comes to dig the ground, cut grass, draw lines on the ground, and so on, without any great need, one should recall the training laid down by the Buddha and quickly abandon it in that very moment.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Name Fear of the Fault and of the Lower Realms as the Reason

Both Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk state clearly that the reason for abandoning these activities is fear of the fault of a downfall and of rebirth in the lower realms in the future, and nothing else.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Explains Why These Trainings Apply to Householder Bodhisattvas Too

Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives a distinct reason why digging the ground and the like are to be abandoned: although in the Vinaya these are taught for monks and not for householders, the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* shows that householder bodhisattvas, too, must guard them just as ordained bodhisattvas do.

###### 4. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Shows, Through the Story of the Two Chief Disciples, How to Act When There Is a Need

The fear and the abandoning apply to activity that has no need; but where there is a need and one must act through any of the three doors, one should engage without attachment, with a sense of disenchantment — as Minyak Kunzang Sonam shows by way of the life story of the two supreme disciples.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Distracting activities such as pointlessly digging the ground, cutting grass, and drawing lines on the ground should be abandoned at once, recalling the Blissful One's training and out of fear of the fault and of the lower realms.

**Key points.**

- **Abandoning it at once.** When one comes to dig the ground, cut grass, draw lines on the ground, and so on, without purpose, one should recall the Blissful One's training and, from fear of the fault and of rebirth in the lower realms, abandon it in that very moment.
- **Examples of pointless activity.** Without any Dharma-related purpose (such as building stupas and temples), digging the ground; cutting trees and grass for no need; drawing lines on the ground; harming seeds and fruit-bearing trees — all such distracting activities are to be abandoned.
- **The same for householders and the ordained.** Although this training is taught in the Vinaya for the ordained, householder bodhisattvas, too, must guard it just as the ordained do.
- **Activity that does have a purpose.** When engaging in work that has a need, one must do so without attachment, with a sense of disenchantment — as Minyak Kunzang Sonam shows by way of the Vinaya narrative.
