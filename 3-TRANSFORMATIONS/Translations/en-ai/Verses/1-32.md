## Combined Commentary Summaries
The commentators present a unified, highly contrastive analysis of Verse 1-32, wherein they compare the limited and potentially flawed generosity of an ordinary worldly benefactor with the limitless, pure, and supreme generosity of a Bodhisattva (Hero of Enlightenment). They organize this comparison across four main categories: the recipient (object), the gift (substance), the manner of giving (application), and the temporal duration (benefit), concluding with an exhortation to single-minded devotion toward the Bodhisattvas.

### 1. The Worldly Benefactor: Limited and Flawed Generosity
All three commentators agree that worldly society often highly praises and respects ordinary benefactors who perform minor acts of charity, even though these acts are severely restricted and accompanied by negative behaviors.
- **Limited Recipients:** Sazang Mati Panchen points out that the worldly benefactor focuses only on a limited object, namely a few wandering beings of a specific area. Gyaltsab Je quantifies this limited group as "such as a hundred" individuals.
- **Mediocre Gift:** The gift consists of a minor, ordinary thing like plain food (described by Sazang Mati Panchen as "ordinary, mediocre food on an annual or monthly basis").
- **Disrespectful Application:** The manner in which the food is given is marked by contempt and disrespect. Gyaltsab Je characterizes this as a "low practice conjoined with disrespect and contempt." Sazang Mati Panchen provides the most vivid description, noting that the worldly benefactor gives "without making offerings, and with mediocre application, such as treating them with contempt, fighting, and beating them." Ngulchu Thokme similarly calls it a "disrespectful manner."
- **Short-lived Benefit:** The benefit is extremely minor and fleeting, merely satisfying the recipients' hunger for "half a day" (Gyaltsab Je, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme all use the exact phrase "for half a day").
- **Worldly Acclaim:** Despite these heavy limitations and disrespectful behaviors, society still praises and honors such a person as a "virtuous benefactor" (Ngulchu Thokme), "one who performs great virtue" (Sazang Mati Panchen), or a "great benefactor who performs virtue" (Gyaltsab Je).

### 2. The Bodhisattva: Limitless and Supreme Generosity
By contrast, the commentators demonstrate that the Bodhisattva's generosity is completely unexcelled, reversing every limitation and flaw of the worldly benefactor.
- **Infinite Recipients:** Sazang Mati Panchen explains that the Bodhisattva's focus is vast, encompassing the "infinite and countless realms of sentient beings." Gyaltsab Je calls this a "vast field of infinite sentient beings," and Ngulchu Thokme describes them as "infinite sentient beings."
- **Supreme Gift:** Rather than ordinary material food, the Bodhisattva bestows the "supreme, stainless bliss of the Blissful Ones (Sugatas)" (Gyaltsab Je and Ngulchu Thokme) or the "supreme object of the unexcelled happiness of the Blissful Ones" (Sazang Mati Panchen).
- **Respectful Application:** The Bodhisattva's giving is characterized by profound respect and care. Gyaltsab Je notes that they give "with a practice conjoined with respect, completely fulfilling all the thoughts of benefit." Sazang Mati Panchen describes this as a "peaceful, disciplined, and respectful application, in a manner that completely fulfills all the various desires in their minds." Ngulchu Thokme states they give "with deep respect, completely fulfilling all their desires."
- **Eternal Duration:** The Bodhisattva's giving is continuous and eternal. Gyaltsab Je notes it lasts "for a long duration throughout the cycle of existence." Sazang Mati Panchen explains that they constantly give "for a vast duration as long as space remains and the cycle of existence is not emptied." Ngulchu Thokme adds a valuable scholastic note, stating that the word "always" or "constantly" in the root text is explained by classical commentators as meaning "never exhausted as long as space and wandering beings remain," and in Sanskrit, it is phrased as "as long as space and wandering beings are not completely exhausted."

### 3. Conclusion and Exhortation
Based on this stark contrast, all three commentators argue that if society deems it proper to praise and honor a worldly benefactor for a tiny, disrespectful, half-day gift of food, then the Bodhisattvas are infinitely more worthy of praise, honor, and devotion.
- **Gyaltsab Je** concludes that because of this supreme, limitless benefit, an intelligent person should be "single-mindedly devoted to the Heroes of Enlightenment."
- **Sazang Mati Panchen** asks rhetorically "what need is there to mention how worthy of praise and respect are those endowed with the mind of enlightenment."
- **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly emphasizes that they are the "supreme benefactor" and are incomparably worthy of honor and veneration.
