## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth verses of the fifth chapter. This teaching clearly shows that the training in mindfulness and awareness is not a rigid set of rules. It must adapt to the needs of each situation.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a point that is easy to understand and encouraging.

When we walk somewhere, we must carefully check behind us for enemies, thieves, or dangerous animals. This shows that the training in mindfulness and awareness is not a rigid set of rules. It must be applied according to the need and reason of each situation.

We should give up pointless distraction and act only for reasons that benefit both ourselves and others. So we too should conduct ourselves properly, always knowing the actual need behind each action.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, whatever I do, I will first check whether it is truly needed for myself or others, and act with mindfulness and awareness.

**Explanation:** *(Wisdom Practice)* This practice is not simply a rigid set of rules. It is the best method for benefiting ourselves and others, according to the need and reason of each situation. If we act only once we clearly know the purpose of any action, no mistakes will arise, and our time will not be wasted on pointless activity.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The General Restraint on the Gaze Is Lifted in Situations of Danger

The general restraint of keeping the eyes lowered while walking is lifted precisely on occasions of danger: this verse establishes that one may look repeatedly in the four directions in order to check for dangers such as thieves, wild animals, or precipices on the road. It thereby shows that, even where the eyes are ordinarily kept restrained, a purposeful gaze is never prohibited.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and His Holiness the Dalai Lama Agree That Looking Behind Must Still Be Done Calmly

As Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both explain in agreement, when looking behind, one should not turn the head around abruptly or leap about in an unruly manner, but should turn to look with a conscientious, calm, and disciplined bearing — establishing clearly the point that, even where there is a genuine need to check for danger, one's overall conduct should never take on a coarse or unruly character.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse clarifies an exception, grounded in genuine need, to the earlier teaching of keeping the eyes lowered. When walking on the road and when resting, looking in the four directions and behind, in order to check for danger, is not pointless wandering of the gaze but a purposeful kind of looking, and is therefore permitted. It thereby shows that relying on mindfulness and awareness is never a rigid matter in every respect, but must be adapted to the genuine need of the occasion.

**Key points.**

- **Why the restraint has an exception.** In general, the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*'s teaching to keep the gaze lowered and unwandering is meant to stop a pointless, distracted wandering of the eyes; it does not prohibit looking in every direction for the sake of a genuine need or to avoid danger. Thus, while walking on the road, one must look repeatedly in the four directions to check for danger from thieves, wild animals, precipices, and the like, and, when resting, one should also turn the face around and look behind.
- **How this is carried out.** Even so, as Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both teach, when looking behind one should abandon any unruly or careless manner, never turning the head abruptly; rather, one should turn around slowly, with body and mind calm and disciplined.
- **The essential point.** Thus, even when engaged in a genuinely necessary action, never departing from a conduct that is mindful, calm, and disciplined is a most essential point of training for the bodhisattva.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse is the summarizing statement that concludes the section teaching how the eyes should look and how the body should move in coming and going. It shows not only that one must check ahead and behind when walking on the road, but that in every circumstance one must act, mindfully and consciously, by knowing the need and reason for the action. In this way, the verse does not merely address a single mode of conduct, but sets forth, in a comprehensive way, a principle of mindfulness and awareness relevant to every action of daily life.

**Key points.**

- **A concluding summary.** As Ngulchu Thogme explicitly states, presenting this verse as the summary of the meaning of the conduct concerning the eyes' gaze, it gathers together, in a single place, the points on conduct taught earlier. It states that in coming and going one must check whether there is danger ahead and whether anything remains behind.
- **A practical refinement.** Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen adds a practical refinement here: checking behind is not merely a matter of bodily danger, but extends also to checking whether one's own belongings and possessions remain with one. Likewise, Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that the phrase "in every circumstance" extends to cover every action of daily life — walking, sitting, eating, sleeping, and the like — whether in a town, a monastery, or an assembly.
- **A general principle for daily life.** His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama likewise teaches that this verse draws together two points into a single foundation: abandoning the pointlessly wandering conduct of the eyes, and, whatever action one undertakes, engaging in it only for a genuine reason and need. Thus, this verse makes clear a great general principle: that this training is not merely a rigid, mechanical set of rules, but must be applied, in every circumstance, mindfully and consciously, through knowing the need that will genuinely benefit oneself and others.
