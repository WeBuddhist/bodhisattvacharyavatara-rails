## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the sixty-fifth, sixty-sixth, and sixty-seventh verses of the fifth chapter. However dearly we care for this body, one day we will lose it. At that time, will we think, "What have we done with it?"

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following passage, which is easy to understand and encouraging. The *Smrityupasthana* sutra says that it is uncertain when death will come, and that the Lord of Death comes with no way to turn him back. The sutra says that for this reason we must use this body in a meaningful way.

> A great power that is hard to bear,
> a fierce mental force that moves everywhere,
> the many separate great fears
> are what is called the Lord of Death.

> The fire of time that cannot be turned back,
> which beings are sure to go through,
> which blocks people from living,
> is what is called here the Lord of Death.

So, since it is uncertain when death will come, we must quickly use this body for doing good.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will not cling to my own body. I will use it for the Dharma and for doing good.

**Explanation:** *(Doing good)* This body has no essence, yet it is a fine tool for doing good. If we stay attached to the body, our time passes for nothing. So if we use this body for doing good, it benefits us in both this life and the next.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Body's Impure Contents Cannot Be Eaten, Drunk, or Licked, So the Body Has No Practical Use

This verse shows that the body's impure substances (excrement and urine, blood, intestines) are fit neither to be eaten, nor drunk, nor licked, and so sets down that there is not even a practical need for the body.

###### 2. Ngulchu Thogme Links the Point to Verse 5-66: Fit Only to Be Guarded as Food for Jackals and Vultures

Ngulchu Thogme shows that, because the body itself cannot be enjoyed, it is fitting to guard it only as food for jackals and vultures, and so ties this point to the conclusion of verse 5-66.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama List the Impure Substances: Nothing Further Is Gained for the Mind Bound Up with Self-Grasping

Both Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama count off the body's impure substances one by one, and set down the conclusion that, relying on the body, nothing beneficial or further needed is gained by the mind bound up with self-grasping.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse puts a stop to the mistaken notion, "I should guard this body because it is needed and serves a purpose." Since the mind cannot eat the body's excrement, urine, and other impure matter; cannot drink blood, pus, and the like; and cannot lick up with the mouth the intestines, stomach, gut, and so on, this impure body can accomplish nothing helpful or needed for the mind bound up with self-grasping — and so it is not fitting to guard it.

**Key points.**

- Even if one thinks the body has something pleasing about it, it is said that, since excrement, urine, and the like cannot be eaten, the blood in the veins and trunk cannot be drunk, and the inner organs and intestines cannot be licked up with the mouth, the body is of absolutely no need.
- Even if one thinks, "I guard this body because it is close and needed by me," it is said that, since the mind cannot eat what is impure, cannot drink blood and pus, and cannot lick up the intestines and gut, it is not needed at all.
- It is said that, since one cannot enjoy it oneself, it is not right to guard this body for the sake of enjoyment; it is fitting to guard it for the sake of food for jackals and vultures.
- If one thinks, "Though the body has no essence, it is needed, and so I guard it," it is said that, since the impure matter, blood, and inner organs cannot be eaten, drunk, or licked, it cannot serve any special necessity whatever.
- It is said that, since the mind bound up with self-grasping cannot eat as food the blood, lymph, pus, feces, urine, and so on, and cannot lick up the intestines, stomach, and gut with the mouth, the body accomplishes nothing at all that would please that mind.
- It is said that even if one guards this body as utterly precious, it is of no benefit at all: since the impure matter, blood, and intestines cannot be eaten, drunk, or licked, no further purpose whatsoever is accomplished.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"At best, it is fitting that jackals and vultures / guard this for the sake of food" — this is said in a sarcastic manner: in the future this very body of yours will become food for jackals, vultures, wolves, and the like, so what you do in guarding it goes no further than guarding it for the sake of their food. "This human body, all of it, / is only to be put to use."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-66]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

For this reason, on the one hand there is no need at all to guard this body; and on the other hand, "if you, mind, guard this body for the sake of food for jackals, vultures, and the like, which have no concept of clean and filthy, that is fitting and reasonable" — these are words of disparagement. Thus, although when the body is examined outside and inside it has no essence at all, an essence must still be taken from it; and how is it taken? As it is said, "The mind, like a king, holds sway over all; the body is the servant of all virtue and vice" — if this body can be put to work as a servant to accomplish virtue, then among the bodies of the six realms of migrating beings, this human body is supreme for accomplishing the sacred Dharma. Therefore, in order to take an essence from it — whether lamas or monastics, high or low, powerful or weak, and so on — "all of this human body" (མི་འོ་ཅོག, that is, everyone who has become human) "is only to be put to use" as a servant for accomplishing virtue, and there is nothing else to be done with it.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If one says, "Still, it is fitting to have a little attachment to this body" — it is not fitting to cling to the body, because the body is a mass of impurity: you cannot eat the impure things that are in the body, you cannot drink the blood, and likewise, if you cannot lick the intestines, what useful thing can the body do for you to enjoy? If one does guard it, then "at best" (ཉིས་ན་) — that is, for one reason — it is fitting to guard this body for the sake of food for jackals and vultures.

The fifth has four parts: that this body, soon to be destroyed by death, should be put to virtuous use; an example showing why it is not fitting to nurture it through attachment while it does nothing at all; paying wages and so setting it to accomplish what one wants; and, taking up the idea of a boat, that it is fitting to accomplish the welfare of all beings. The first is: "This human body, all of it, is only to be put to use."

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

If one thinks, "Though there is no essence, it is fit to enjoy" — this body is an accumulation and heap of many impurities, and you, mind, cannot eat the flesh and other impure masses of the body, you cannot drink the body's blood, and you cannot lick or suck up the inner organs, the intestines, the stomach, and so on; so if nothing can be done with them, what can this body itself do for you, mind? There is not the slightest practical need for it that would make it fit to enjoy.

If this body, which has no other use, is to be guarded, then "nyis na" — in one respect, or for one reason — if it is guarded for the sake of food for jackals and vultures, and (since it is said that the Indian original has a word meaning "and so on") for the flesh-eaters of the charnel ground such as charnel-ground birds and crows, then, since there is nothing unfitting about guarding it for that much alone, it is fitting. That is the meaning.

Again: "On the one hand, since it cannot be used, it is not fitting to guard it; on the other hand, since jackals, vultures, and the like can use it, there is nothing unfitting about guarding it merely for the sake of their food" — so it is said in the Detailed Explanation (རྣམ་བཤད). There is also an explanation of "nyis na" in the sense of "if it is fitting"; and if it is explained in the sense of "if," I think that is acceptable.

Apart from that, there is no reason whatsoever for which it is fitting to guard this; hence the meaning is: do not guard it with an attached mind. Up to this point, the unfittingness of guarding the body out of attachment has been shown; and in that regard, to take an essence from the body and life, which have no essence, the obstacle is attachment to body and life; therefore the manner of meditating on the antidote to attachment to body and life has been shown in this way.

As for the fitness of putting the body to virtue and taking its essence, since it will soon be cast aside:

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"At best, it is fitting that jackals and vultures / guard this for the sake of food" — this means: for that reason, on the one hand there is no need to guard this body; and on the other hand (ཉིས་ན་, that is, གཉིས་སུ་ན་), if you now guard this body for the sake of food for jackals and vultures, which have neither clean nor filthy, then "that is fitting and proper" — in this way the author speaks words of displeasure, words of rebuke. That is: at the time when one has died, jackals, vultures, and the like will be sated by it. If you think, "Therefore I will guard this body of mine now," then "that is proper for you" — these are words with a sense of displeasure. "This human body, all of it, / is only to be put to use." If one asks, "Then what is to be done with this body?" — "mi 'o cog" (that is, as many as there are of humans): this body, properly speaking, is only to be put to work in the right way for accomplishing the sacred Dharma. There is no other function whatever that it has.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Turn: From "No Essence" to "Put the Body to Virtuous Use"

At this point the verse turns back from the conclusion of the investigation and sets down, as the key point to be expressed, that the body, although it has no essence, is not to be discarded but is firmly to be put to use as nothing more than a means for virtuous action.

###### 2. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen: It Is Not Fitting to Cling, and a Four-Part Outline for Putting the Body to Virtue

Beginning from the unfittingness of attachment to the body, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen sets down the point on the basis of the four-part outline of this passage concerning putting the body to virtuous use.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Gives Three Angles on Why Guarding the Body Is Not Fitting

Minyak Kunzang Sonam divides the reasons why it is not fitting to guard the body into three angles and sets them out in detail, from the body's not having the slightest practical need that would make it fit to enjoy, to the instruction not to guard it with an attached mind.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama: The Sarcasm Is a Rebuke, and the Body Must Be Put to Work for the Dharma

Both Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama explain that "it is fitting to guard it for jackals and vultures" is said by way of displeasure and rebuke, and they set down the point that the body must be put to work for the accomplishment of the sacred Dharma.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** When this body is examined outside and inside, it is a heap of impurity, so there is no need at all to guard it out of attachment. If one does guard it, it is said by way of sarcasm and rebuke that this would be fitting only if one guarded it as food for jackals, vultures, and the like. This human body, all of it, is only to be put to use properly for the sacred Dharma and for virtuous action.

**Key points.**

- It is said, sarcastically, that if one guards this body for the sake of food for jackals, vultures, and the like, that is fitting; and that this human body, all of it, is only to be put to use for virtuous action.
- Words of disparagement are spoken: if one guards this body for the sake of food for jackals and vultures, which have no concept of clean and filthy, that is fitting; and since among the bodies of the six realms this human body is supreme for accomplishing the sacred Dharma, in order to take an essence from it, it is only to be put to use as a servant for accomplishing virtue.
- The body is a mass of impurity, and its flesh, blood, and intestines cannot be enjoyed, so it is not fitting to cling to the body; if one does guard it, it would be fitting to guard it for the sake of food for jackals and vultures.
- It is said that this human body is only to be put to use for virtue.
- It is said that although guarding this body for the sake of food for jackals, vultures, and the like would be fitting, you, mind, are not like that, and so you should not guard it out of attachment.
- This body is an accumulation of many impurities, so that none of its flesh, blood, intestines, or stomach can be enjoyed; there is not the slightest practical need for it that would make it fit to enjoy.
- Words of displeasure and rebuke are spoken: if one guards this body for the sake of food for jackals and vultures, which have neither clean nor filthy, that is fitting and proper; and, of all there are of humans, this body is only to be put to work properly for accomplishing the sacred Dharma.
- It is said sarcastically that in the future this body will become food for jackals, vultures, wolves, and the like, so that guarding it goes no further than guarding it for the sake of their food.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Though you guard it in this way, / the pitiless Lord of Death / will seize it and give it to birds and dogs. / What can you do then?" Therefore, rather than doing such things as taking up pleasure, pain, and harsh words (བདེ་སྡུག་གཏམ་ངན) out of intense clinging and attachment to this body, one needs to look upon this body, by means of inner courage and a discernment unlike any other, as something to be employed as a basis for accomplishing virtue, and to know how to use it as a limb for accomplishing virtue, or as a contributing condition to be put to use. Otherwise, however much you guard it, when the pitiless Lord of Death seizes it and gives it to birds and dogs, there is nothing at all that you can do.

*(Editorial note: the phrase བདེ་སྡུག་གཏམ་ངན་དང་དུ་ལེན་པ་ལྟ་བུ is rendered literally; its exact sense is not clear in the rail.)*

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Moreover, those who are strongly attached to the body give their own body whatever food, clothing, ornaments, and the like they wish and please: "Though you, mind, thus guard this body with effort, the Lord of Death, Yama, without love or compassion, helplessly seizes it from you and gives it to vultures and the like and to dogs, and they eat it; then what can you, mind, do?" — there is no means of doing anything at all.

This body has no need at all from its own side; rather, this human body, complete with the freedoms and endowments, is only to be put to use, as is reasonable, for virtuous action. "Though you guard it in this way, without accomplishing what you wish, the pitiless Lord of Death will seize it from you and separate you from life, and when he gives it to birds and dogs, then what can you, mind, do?" — you have not even the slightest power to keep from being parted from it.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

"Mi 'o cog" — that is, this body of all who have become human — is only to be put to use for virtuous action while one is alive, and it is not fitting to guard it out of attachment. For although you now guard it with effort, in the end the pitiless Lord of Death will helplessly seize it and give it to birds, dogs, wolves, and the like; and at that time what can you do? There is nothing that can be done.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-67]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Of those endowed with yoga (རྣལ་འབྱོར་ལྡན་པ), this body — "mi 'o cog," glossed as "all of them" or "as many as are human" — can now, at this time while one is alive, be put to the work of merit and virtue; so it is fitting and proper only that it be put to that use, and there is no need whatever to guard it with any other, attached mind. Why? Because, not putting it to virtue from this very moment and not taking an essence from what by its own nature has no essence at all, to guard it with an attached mind is not only needless; even if one guards it thinking, "Later I will take its essence," then whenever the Lord of Death, utterly without compassion, helplessly and with great force parts you, mind, from life, seizes the body, and gives it to vultures, dogs, wolves, and the like — at that time what can you do? Nothing can be done; whatever means you use, you have no power to guard it so that you are not parted from it. This means: since it is not certain when death will come, it is fitting to put it quickly to virtue.

By the words "mi 'o cog," it is shown that the bodies of nearly all beings other than humans cannot at all be put to virtue, like the bodies of animals. Joining the two lines beginning "mi 'o cog" upward to the preceding two lines, "it is fitting to guard it for two purposes, because it is food for vultures and the like and because it is a limb for gathering merit" — so says Vibhūti (that is, Vibhūticandra). As for the meaning of "the pitiless Lord of Death": as is said in the *Smṛtyupasthāna* (དྲན་ཉེར):

> A great power that is hard to bear,
> a fierce mental force that moves everywhere,
> the many separate great fears
> are what is called the Lord of Death.
>
> The fire of time that cannot be turned back,
> which beings are sure to go through,
> which blocks people from living,
> is what is called here the Lord of Death.

and so on, as is said.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Though you guard it in this way, / the pitiless Lord of Death / will seize it and give it to birds and dogs. / What can you do then?" This means — the reason, as explained below, is this: you, mind, bound up with self-grasping, for the span of this one life now nurture this very body with food and clothing and guard it with bathing, massage, and so on; yet at the time of death, the Lord of Death, Yama, without love or compassion, seizes this very body from you, mind, and if he gives it as food into the mouths of birds, dogs, jackals, wolves, and the like, then at that time what is it proper for you, mind, to do? There is nothing that can be done.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. However Much One Strives to Guard the Body, the Pitiless Lord of Death Seizes It Helplessly

This verse shows that however much effort one makes to guard the body, when the pitiless Lord of Death helplessly seizes it and gives it to birds and dogs, the mind can do nothing at all.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Quotes the Smṛtyupasthāna on What "Lord of Death" Means

Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes two stanzas from the *Smṛtyupasthāna* on the meaning of "the pitiless Lord of Death" and sets out in detail the defining character of the Lord of Death.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama: However Well the Body Is Tended, It Cannot Stay, So Use It as a Support for Virtue

Both Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama show that, however much the body is nurtured with food, clothing, bathing, massage, and so on, it cannot remain there, and so they set down the point that the body must be used merely as a contributing condition for accomplishing virtue.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This human body, while one is alive, is only to be put to use for merit and virtuous action; to strive to guard it with attachment and clinging, through food, clothing, ornaments, and the like, is of no use at all. For when at some point the pitiless Lord of Death helplessly parts one from life, seizes this body, and gives it as food to birds, dogs, and the like, the mind has neither the power to avoid being separated from it nor any means to do anything at all.

**Key points.**

- It is said that though the mind strives to guard this body, when the pitiless Lord of Death forcibly seizes it and gives it to birds and dogs, there is nothing the mind can do, and no way to set it right again.
- It is said that though those strongly attached to the body guard it with effort, giving it whatever food, clothing, and ornaments they please, when Yama, the Lord of Death, without love, helplessly seizes it and gives it to vultures and dogs to eat, the mind has no means of doing anything at all.
- It is fitting to put this human body, complete with the freedoms and endowments, to virtuous use rather than guard it without accomplishing one's aim; when the pitiless Lord of Death seizes it, separates one from life, and gives it to birds and dogs, the mind has not the slightest power to avoid being parted from it.
- It is said that this body of all who have become human is only to be put to virtuous use while one is alive, and that guarding it with attachment is not fitting; in the end, when the pitiless Lord of Death helplessly seizes it and gives it to birds, dogs, wolves, and the like, nothing can be done.
- It is said that in this time of life it is fitting to put the body to the work of merit and virtue, and that there is no need to guard it with an attached mind; when at some point the Lord of Death, utterly without compassion, helplessly parts one from life, seizes the body, and gives it to vultures, dogs, wolves, and the like, there is no power to guard it so as not to be parted from it, whatever means one uses.
- It is said that though the mind bound up with self-grasping guards this body with food, clothing, bathing, massage, and so on, at death, when the pitiless Lord of Death, Yama, seizes it and gives it as food into the mouths of birds, dogs, jackals, wolves, and the like, the mind can do nothing at all.
- It is said that one needs to know how to use this body, through inner courage and discernment, as a limb or contributing condition for accomplishing virtue; otherwise, however much one guards it, when the pitiless Lord of Death seizes it and gives it to birds and dogs, there is nothing one can do.
