# Day 68 — Bound like wolf and sheep: the unyielding war against the afflictions

**Date:** Sep 11

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 35 to 37 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Like a brave warrior who does not retreat even when struck by a weapon on the battlefield, we must build the courage to never retreat until we have conquered the enemy of our own afflictions.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a comparison that is easy to understand and encouraging.

For example, if a wolf and a sheep are tied together with the same rope, the sheep will never find a moment of relaxed ease. As long as the wolf is not cut loose from it, the sheep will always feel fear and great worry.

In the same way, the afflictions living bound together with our mind are like a wolf and a sheep tied together. However good our outer circumstances may be, as long as the afflictions are present in our mind, we will not find real happiness. So freeing our own mind from the bondage of the afflictions becomes the most important thing of all.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when an affliction arises, I will apply its antidote again and again and work at it without giving up.

**Explanation:** *(Not giving up the effort)* People of this world work without rest to conquer even a small enemy who does them little harm. In the same way, I will apply the antidote until I have conquered the enemy of the afflictions. Applying the antidote again and again is the best way to conquer the afflictions.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

These beings who have become the guardians of the prison of cyclic existence, executioners in the hells and elsewhere — this is said because, through the power of the afflictions, one accumulates evil karma, and through the power of that karma these guardians, executioners, and the like arise as appearances from that same karma. And under the word "and so forth," the whole host of afflictions that lead one into rebirth as a hungry ghost or an animal reside in one's own mind without the least discomfort, entirely at ease and unconcerned — this is exactly as it says: "Bound to the prison of cyclic existence by the lasso of craving and desire." So because one remains caught in that net-like snare of attachment — that is, craving — from which one is unable to break free, how could I possibly have any opportunity for happiness? I have none at all.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-35]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

These afflictions — which act as the guards that, within the prison of the three realms of cyclic existence, do not allow one release, and which appear, in the realms such as the hells and beyond, as the executioners who take one's very life — if they abide, unrelinquished, in the net of attachment that resides in my own mind, holding me fast the way a hunter's snare grips its prey, then how could I have any chance of happiness, in this life or any life to come? I would have none — so I must strive to abandon this very thing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-35]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Since the afflictions do not permit escape from cyclic existence, they act as the guards of the prison of cyclic existence, casting one into the dungeon of the hells and the like, and become the executioners who kill in both the lower and higher rebirths alike. If these afflictions reside within the net of attachment that abides in my own mind — that is, within improper conceptual thought — then how could I have any opportunity for happiness? There is no occasion for either body or mind to be at ease.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-35]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Ngulchu Thogme transcludes verses 4-34 and 4-35 together as one quoted block and comments on both at once; the explanation below covers both. In the current segmentation of this source, the quoted verse-pair is stamped as this file's own block ^4-71, and the explanation as ^4-73.)*

In this way, if this affliction, the enemy that for such a long time — since beginningless time, continuously and without interruption — has caused harm, and is the sole cause behind the increase of every kind of harm, comes to reside with certainty in my own heart, then how could I be without fear in cyclic existence, or come to feel any joy? I could not. Furthermore, since it does not permit escape from the prison of cyclic existence, these afflictions — the guards of that prison, become executioners in hell and the like — if they reside in the net of attachment, that is, of clinging, that abides in my mind, then how could I have happiness? This is the point being made.

I would have none at all — so I must strive to abandon the afflictions.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam treats this verse as a summary verse — "this verse is also a summing-up of the meaning of the verses above" — of the reasoning built up across the preceding several verses on why the afflictions are the true enemy; the exposition below accordingly restates those earlier verses in condensed form before returning to this verse's own point.)*

As explained above, this affliction — the enemy that, since beginningless time and for such a long span, has never let up in inflicting grave harm upon me, and is the sole cause or seed from which every kind of harm arises and increases, whether the general sufferings of cyclic existence or, in particular, the sufferings, terrors, and injuries of the lower realms — if it resides with certainty in my heart, that is, in the continuum of my mind, and I remain inseparably joined to it, then how could I be free of fear in cyclic existence, or have any joy arise in my mind? I could not.

This verse is also a summing-up of the meaning of the verses above: the phrases "for such a long time" and so on, "the sole cause of every kind of harm" and so on, and "in my heart" and so on correspond, in order, to the earlier phrases about my own afflictions and so on, about the gods and demigods and so on, and about my own mind and so on.

Furthermore, these afflictions — malicious enemies that are the source of every fault without exception — act as the guards of the prison of cyclic existence, the abode that binds one with manifold sufferings and never, at any time, permits release from it, subjecting one to every kind of harm it is possible to inflict and applying one to many sufferings that are hard to bear; and they cast one down into the wretched dungeon of the hells and the like. Moreover, through the power of the evil karma that the afflictions themselves produce, they become the executioners who take life in the realms of the hells, the hungry ghosts, and the like — appearing there in the form of the messengers of the Lord of Death, whether as humans or as birds and dogs — and cut off the life of higher rebirth and liberation alike. If these afflictions — this malicious enemy, the source of every fault — dwell fixed within the net of attachment, that is, the improper mental engagement and conceptual thought, like an iron cage, within the abode of my own mind or the house of my thinking, always residing there, then how could I ever, at any time, have any opportunity for physical or mental happiness? I would have none — just as, when a wolf and a sheep are tied together, the sheep never has any occasion for ease — so said our late Guru in his oral teaching.

In this way, because the afflicted enemy inflicts extremely grave harm, it is the most malicious enemy even among enemies. As the Sūtra Requested by Udayana says: "Great king, this enemy is a minor enemy — there is another great enemy, previously unmentioned." "What enemy is that?" he asked. The reply came: "There is a great enemy called the self-grasping affliction." Because it is said in this way, these passages are teaching the faults of the afflictions; and since, having seen such faults, one must hold the afflictions to be an enemy and abandon them, knowing their faults is of great importance.

Furthermore, as for the faults of the afflictions: it is said that when an affliction arises, at the outset it thoroughly afflicts the mind; it distorts the object one focuses on; it strengthens the latent tendencies; it perpetuates an unbroken continuum of afflictions of the same kind; it inflicts great harm on both oneself and others; it produces great wrongdoing in both this life and the next; it causes one to experience suffering and unhappiness; it brings about the sufferings of conditioned existence such as birth; it removes one far from liberation; it causes one to decline from virtue and happiness; when one goes among an assembly, one has no joy or fearlessness and feels apprehensive; it increases one's ill repute in every direction; the deities who protect the teaching, the Teacher, and one's noble friends all reproach one for it; and, dying with regret, one is reborn afterward in a bad rebirth — these and many other faults are said to arise. The *Sūtrālaṃkāra* likewise says: "The afflictions destroy oneself, destroy other beings, and destroy ethical discipline; they bring one down through loss and through the very gains that should be a support and a protection; and likewise they bring reproach from the Teacher; entangled in dispute, of ill repute, one is reborn in another, unfree state; through the loss of what is gained and what is not yet gained, one comes to experience great suffering in one's mind."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-35]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This cyclic existence of the three realms is like a hard-labor prison, painful like a king's dungeon, and every one of these afflictions — attachment and the rest — is like a prison guard who does not let the prisoner go free. And these afflictions, moreover, become the executioners who put one to death in the lower realms — the hells, the hungry ghosts, and the animals. Since it is thereby certain that they are nothing but sources of harm, if these afflictions — attachment and the rest — reside in what is called "the net of attachment abiding in the mind," that is, in one's own place of mind — in short, if they abide, at every time and in every way, within the net or cage of attachment that clings openly to self and to what is mine — then how could I have any relaxed ease of mind, sitting at my own pleasure, or any happiness at all? I would have none, in any respect whatsoever — that is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^4-35]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. There Is No Occasion for Happiness at All While the Afflictions Reside in the Net of Attachment

All the commentaries agree in explaining that these afflictions — the guards that do not permit release from the prison of cyclic existence, and the executioners that kill in the lower realms and elsewhere — leave no possibility of happiness for me if they reside in the net, or cage, of attachment that abides in the mind.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Explains This Verse as a Summary of the Preceding Reasoning

He explains that if the affliction — the enemy that has caused uninterrupted harm since beginningless time — dwells in the continuum of the mind, all of the reasoning built up in the verses above, showing that one cannot be free of fear or find joy in cyclic existence, is summed up in this single verse.

###### 3. Scripture Establishes That the Afflictions Are the Greatest Enemy of All

Citing the *Sūtra Requested by Udayana*, where self-grasping affliction is named as the truly great enemy, and the *Sūtrālaṃkāra*, where the afflictions are said to harm both oneself and other beings and to destroy every virtue such as ethical discipline, Minyak Kunzang Sonam stresses the great importance of recognizing the afflictions' faults.

###### 4. The Wolf-and-Sheep Analogy Illustrates That There Is No Ease While One Cannot Escape the Net of Attachment

Just as a sheep tied together with a wolf never has an occasion for ease, so too, Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, citing an oral teaching of his own guru, one who is unable to break free from the net of attachment never has any opportunity for happiness.

###### 5. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Likens the Three Realms to a King's Prison

He explains that cyclic existence of the three realms is like a hard-labor prison, with the afflictions as the guards and executioners within it; as long as one clings openly to self and to what is mine within that net, there is no occasion whatsoever for happiness.

###### 6. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Explains That the Lasso of Craving Binds One to the Prison of Cyclic Existence

He explains that, through the power of the afflictions, one accumulates evil karma, whose root, in the guardians of hell and the executioners alike, is the lasso of craving and desire that binds one to the prison of cyclic existence.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

In this way, since this affliction is the cause of every fault, as long as I have not conquered it — that is, as long as I have not truly pulled it out by the root, made manifest before my very eyes — I will not give up my effort at this time when I am striving to do so. Even for a while, those puffed up with pride who become angry at even a minor harm will not sleep until they have conquered the one who harmed them — so what need is there to say how forcefully I must strive, wishing as I do to conquer the enemy of the afflictions?

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This teaches that as long as I have not, having actualized the noble wisdom, served as the antidote to the three fetters that are cut through by the path of seeing, together with the intellectually-adopted afflictions, and then, proceeding step by step, actually conquered both the intellectually-adopted and the innate afflictions and actualized the truth of cessation, I will not simply relax and remain settled. As for saying, "those puffed up with pride who become angry even at a minor harm will not sleep until they have conquered it" — for example, someone puffed up with pride, holding onto their conceit, who becomes angry at even an ordinary, minor harm over some trivial reason, thinking, "I must get rid of this enemy of mine," will find that as long as they have not conquered that enemy, their own mind — the mind of the very person seeking to defeat it — cannot settle at ease; they cannot even fall asleep, let alone find any relish for food. Given that someone remains in such a state over even this kind of matter, I too, this being said, should not give up my own effort here.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-36]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Second: having thus identified the afflictions, merely knowing their faults is not enough — they must be abandoned. And as long as I have not truly conquered — that is, abandoned — these afflicted enemies, manifestly and with certainty, rather than merely suppressing them on the surface, I must vow and strive not to give up, even at the cost of my life, the effort of generating and cultivating the antidotes to the afflictions in my own mind at this time. For example: even for a while, when someone steals a horse or cattle — an ordinary, minor harm — some who become angry over it and are puffed up with great pride, mustering great force, will not even fall asleep until they have conquered that enemy. If such effort is generated over that, then how much more must great effort be generated to conquer the greater harm-doer, the enemy of the afflictions — this needs no saying at all. This citing of an example is called the example of the counterpart in kind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-36]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Since every unwanted thing is produced by the afflictions, as long as I have not manifestly and certainly conquered this unbearable enemy, it is fitting that I, in this world, strive without ever giving up, even for an instant, the effort to apply the antidotes to the afflictions. For a while, if those puffed up with great pride, harboring a strong grudge, who become angry even at ordinary, minor harm — such as someone merely speaking a harsh word — strive until they cannot even sleep before conquering that enemy, then it is fitting that I too should strive to conquer the afflictions.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-36]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

In this way, as long as I have not manifestly and certainly conquered this affliction, the enemy, I must not give up my effort here. For a while, if beings puffed up with pride who become angry even at ordinary, minor harm-doers — that is, common enemies — cannot sleep until they have conquered that enemy, then how much more must I strive to conquer the enemy of the afflictions — this needs no saying.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^4-76]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Since it is the cause of every fault, as long as I have not manifestly and certainly conquered this enemy, the affliction, from its very root, I must strive here without giving up my effort in relying on a powerful antidote. For a while, if those puffed up with great and swelling pride, who harbor a grudge from becoming angry even at an ordinary, minor harm-doer, undergo the hardship of not sleeping until they have conquered that enemy, then it is extremely fitting to strive at conquering the great harm-doer, the affliction.

Since this is the root of every unwanted thing and every fault, therefore, as long as I have not certainly conquered these malicious enemies, the afflictions, together with their seeds of production, seeing them directly, I must strive here — at the time I have now entered the battlefield against the afflictions — not to give up, even for an instant, the burden of the vow to strive at relying on a powerful antidote that conquers them. As explained above, as long as I am unable — at all times and in every way — to have manifestly and certainly uprooted every one of these afflictions, attachment and the rest, that have become my enemies — that is, having pulled them out by the very root rather than merely suppressing them on the surface, having certainly conquered them together with their seeds, and abandoned them — then I myself, at this time, must apply diligence to those trainings that a bodhisattva's generation of the awakening mind requires me to practice, and must not give them up in any respect. ###### 1. One Must Never Give Up Effort Until the Afflicted Enemy Is Manifestly Conquered

All the commentaries agree in explaining that, just as those puffed up with pride who become angry even at a minor, ordinary harm will not sleep until that harm-doer is conquered, so too I must not give up, even for an instant, my effort at the antidote until this great enemy, the root of every fault, is manifestly and certainly conquered.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** As all the commentaries explain in agreement, this verse teaches, through the example of a soldier who disregards the suffering of being struck by weapons and does not turn back until victory is won, how one must persevere in conquering the ordinary, mortal, and pitiable enemy.

**Key points.**

- Ordinary enemies, even if not killed by oneself, will sooner or later die by nature at the hands of the Lord of Death, and so are weak and objects of compassion.
- In the heat of battle, one who suffers wounds and bloodshed from being struck by weapons such as arrows and spears does not turn back or flee before their purpose of conquering the enemy is accomplished.
- The verse's own word for the "enemy" does not refer to an inner affliction, but is a term of scorn applied to a weak or pitiable ordinary foe.
- If one does not turn back even when conquering a trifling, minor enemy, disregarding the suffering of weapons, then one must not be discouraged or dispirited by any harm at all in conquering the affliction, which is by nature the constant cause of all suffering.
