## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the eighty-second, eighty-third, and eighty-fourth verses of the fifth chapter. Without depending on others, we take up the path of doing good actions by ourselves.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable story, which is easy to understand and encouraging. Long ago, the caravan leader Great Compassion killed a bad man with a short spear. He did this to save that man from falling to hell for killing five hundred merchants. Also, to save a merchant leader's daughter from death, the young brahmin Star did not keep celibacy for twelve years. Though these two did forbidden acts out of great compassion, it is said that they completed the accumulation of many eons. Therefore, for those who have compassion and see far, even what is forbidden is permitted.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, without depending on others, I will begin doing one good action by myself, with joy.

**Explanation:** *(Diligence Practice)* This verse of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_ teaches that we must do good actions by ourselves, with joy, without depending on others. If we take up doing good ourselves, great happiness arises in the mind. This quickly completes the store of merit in our own mind.

### Verse 5-82

> **Rail source:** `2-RAILS/Verses/5-82-summary.md` &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Rail status:** `draft`

<!-- sub:root-verse -->
#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-82`):*

> Being capable and energetic, I should always do tasks myself rather than depending on anyone else for them.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Having become skilled in generating the strength of virtue and endowed with faith that rejoices exceedingly, I myself should always perform the actions that accomplish the aims of myself and others. In all virtuous actions, I am not to depend on, that is, to rely on, any person at all.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-82]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

So — through such things as the field, the time, the intention, and the circumstances of place and time, virtuous actions of great strength come to be accomplished. One must know these and become skilled, and, endowed with faith, one must always accomplish such virtuous actions. If one does not know the way of doing this well, there will come times when one has to ask others. But if one has first done genuine hearing and reflecting and knows well, then in all actions one need not depend on anyone at all; one will be able to put things into practice through one's own discernment that separates good from bad, and one will be able to make firm decisions.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-82]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In whatever virtue one practices, nothing comes of it if others merely go through the motions (ལད་མོ་བྱས་པ) in one's place. In particular, even in a bodhisattva's practice such as generosity, if one is unskilled in the distinctions such as pure and impure giving, and in the motivation, the manner of giving, the dedication, and so on, then merely giving in whatever way one pleases is something even animals do. So one must become skilled and get to the crux. Therefore, first, in whatever Dharma one practices, one should become thoroughly skilled in its meaning and endowed with faith, the faith of trust in its meaning. Then, "the actions" of a bodhisattva's conduct, such as the six perfections, "I myself should always perform." As in the life story of Jowo Je concerning the putting down of salt (ཕྱག་ཚྭ་གདབ་པ), in all actions such as offering and giving, "I must not depend on any other person" — whether someone who urges or someone who does it — in order to accomplish them: if it can be accomplished by oneself, one accomplishes it, and if it cannot be accomplished, it is not acceptable to depend on others.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-82]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Having become skilled in what to adopt and abandon and in what to engage in and avoid, and endowed with faith in virtue, I myself should always perform virtuous actions. In all virtuous actions, as in merely getting others alone to do them, I am not to depend on anyone at all, even as a companion.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-82]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Being skilled in virtue that has strength, and delighting in it, that is, endowed with enthusiasm, I myself should always perform the actions that serve the aims of myself and others, and in all actions I need not depend on anyone else at all. There is no fault of the absurd consequence that one would not engage others in virtue, for if a virtuous friend can be engaged in virtue, one engages them. The third (showing the adopting and abandoning of the higher and the lower) is:

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-129]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Thus, being skilled in the distinctions of virtue of great strength, and joyful, and endowed with diligence that rises lightly (ལྡང་བ་ཡང་བ), I myself should always accomplish all the actions that benefit myself and others. In all virtuous actions, I am not to depend on anyone at all, such as a teacher who is the one urging or a student who is the one carrying out.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-82]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Being skilled in the distinctions of great and small strength of virtue and non-virtue and in how to adopt and abandon them, and endowed either with faith in accumulating virtue or else with the joy of a delighted mind — and since some copies read "skilled and energetic (ལྡང་)" — endowed with the diligence that rises lightly, having abandoned laziness, which is the same in meaning as the former, "[garbled in the rail: དག འ་བ་] endowed with." In any case, having become endowed with such qualities, in performing the virtuous actions that become the causes of generating benefit and happiness for self and others, the bodhisattva needs no urging from others and needs no companions: "I myself," that is, by myself alone, not only at times but always, without forgetting, through diligence accompanied by the powerful strength of joyful enthusiasm, should accomplish the especially superior virtue. But in performing all virtuous actions, "on anyone at all" I am not to depend — such as an urger like a teacher, or a doer like a student, or others who do not do it themselves but only get other companions to do it. As the Sutra of Inexhaustible Intelligence says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] and so on. However, there is no fault of the absurd consequence that one would not engage others in virtue, for one engages friends who can be engaged in virtue; so it is also said.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-82]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

In whatever virtue one accomplishes, one should become skilled in the grounds of accomplishing it. That is: first, the preparation, the sublime generation of bodhicitta; the main part, the sublime non-referential state; and the conclusion, the sublime dedication. One should be skilled in the grounds of accomplishing virtue that is embraced by these three, preparation, main part, and conclusion. Further, one accomplishes the virtue to be accomplished by way of being endowed with the faith of thorough trust in it. So whichever of the bodhisattva's practices it may be, such as the six perfections and the four ways of gathering disciples, I myself should always accomplish these in the manner of free choice and one's own inclination. And whatever the virtuous action may be, such as hearing, reflecting, and meditating, in all of these I am not to depend on any other person, such as someone who commands or someone who drives. As the saying goes, "Do not take counsel with father; do not ask mother for advice" — one must decide for oneself and engage in accomplishing virtue.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-82]]

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** A bodhisattva, having become skilled in what to adopt and abandon and in the grounds of practice, and endowed with faith or joy, should always accomplish by his own effort the virtuous actions that accomplish the aims of self and others, without needing to depend on an urger on whom he relies or on any other companion.

**Key points.**

- First, having become skilled in the preparation, main part, and conclusion and in the grounds of what to adopt and abandon, one must become endowed with faith of trust in virtue, or with the enthusiasm of joy.
- Actions that benefit self and others, such as the six perfections and the three of hearing, reflecting, and meditating, one should accomplish by oneself, always and without forgetting.
- In virtuous actions, without needing to depend on anyone else, such as a teacher who urges or a student who carries out, one must engage having decided for oneself.
- If virtuous friends can be engaged in virtue, one engages them; there is no fault of the absurd consequence that one would not engage others in virtue.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Generosity and the other perfections too: one practices them with ever higher distinction, ethical discipline being more distinguished than generosity, and patience and the rest likewise than ethical discipline — this should be understood in the same way. For the sake of a smaller perfection one does not give up the greater, higher ones, and at all times one primarily considers whatever becomes of benefit to others.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-83]]

---

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Further, at the time of accomplishing virtuous actions, as it is said, "for the sake of the coarse, and for the sake of the subtle," so too in practices such as the six perfections: each earlier one is coarse and each later one subtle; each earlier one is easy and each later one difficult. So as soon as the practice of a lower one has taken hold, one must not think "that much is enough" but must train in the higher one. Whenever each lower one has taken hold, the higher ones are subtler and more profound, so one must seek out those higher practices, which are ever more distinguished. "For the sake of the lesser, the greater is not given up. Primarily, one thinks of the benefit of others."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-83]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

"Generosity" and "ethical discipline" "and so on": each higher one, "ever higher," is more "distinguished," that is, supreme or more noble, than the one below it, so one must practice the higher ones as primary. For example, as with the difference between the water in a cow's hoofprint and the great ocean, keeping ethical discipline for a single day brings greater benefit than giving gifts for a hundred years, and so on, ever more distinguished going upward. The Ornament of the Sutras, in dependence on the former, speaks of the latter in terms of the lower and the supreme, the coarse and the subtle. "For the sake of the lesser," that is, something inferior or small such as generosity, one does not give up "the greater," that is, something supreme or great such as ethical discipline — this is the general statement. In particular, bodhisattvas must think of and accomplish, "primarily," "whatever becomes the benefit of other sentient beings." This is as in the saying, "The general and what is excepted from it — most cherished in all the treatises."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-83]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

One should practice generosity and so on, up to wisdom, with ever higher distinction and increase. For the sake of the lesser need or lesser virtue of generosity, ethical discipline, and so on, one does not give up the greater but strives at the greater. By implication, this shows that one cannot do both at once, and that when it comes down to it (ནང་འདོམ་ན) one gives up the lesser in order to guard the greater. If asked, "By what are great and small determined?": if doing one of two things, such as one's own aim in this life and the aim of others, would damage the other, "primarily one thinks of the benefit of others."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-83]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Since one must choose among the six, generosity and the other perfections, they are "ever higher in distinction"; each later one is supreme, so one should practice that. Although the order of higher and lower is thus, as in the example given earlier, "for the sake of a small ethical discipline, one does not give up great generosity." If asked, "By what are great and small determined?": "primarily one thinks of whatever is for the benefit of others and undertakes whichever is greater" — so it is from the Detailed Explanation (རྣམ་བཤད).

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-131]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The six, generosity and the other perfections, are such that each later one is more distinguished and supreme than each earlier one, going ever higher; so one should practice primarily whichever is more distinguished, and "for the sake of the small," that is, the inferior, such as generosity, one does not give up "the great," that is, the supreme, such as ethical discipline — because the sutra says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] and so on. If one wonders, "Does this not contradict the teaching that at the time of generosity one sets ethical discipline in equanimity?" — even with great and small, supreme and inferior, one examines whether it will primarily become for the benefit of others. If great generosity can be accomplished, one sets ethical discipline in equanimity; and, for example, rising from meditative concentration to give the gift of Dharma to many people — whatever becomes for the benefit of others, "one thinks of that as great or supreme."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-83]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Generosity and the other six perfections: compared with each later one, each earlier one is lesser and inferior, and compared with each earlier one, each later one is supreme. In this way they are "ever higher in distinction," that is, more superior; therefore at the time of practice one should practice taking the higher ones as primary. "For the sake of the lesser," that is, the inferior, such as generosity, one does not give up, that is, does not discard, the supreme or greater, such as ethical discipline. As Vasubandhu says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] so it is said. By implication, it is shown that when the two cannot be accomplished at once, when it comes down to it (ནང་འདོམ་ན) the lesser must be given up in order to guard the greater; and that is said in terms of the general case.

If one asks, "Does this not contradict what was explained earlier — 'At the times of giving, ethical discipline is said to be set in equanimity'?" — then: as for the distinctions of great and small, supreme and inferior, if taken in terms of the greatness and smallness of virtue in general, then, as explained above, generosity and the earlier ones are small or inferior compared with the later ones, and the later ones are great or supreme compared with the earlier ones; so it is said that one must practice taking the higher as primary. But in the special case, if taken in terms of greater and lesser need, one examines whether it will primarily become for the aims of others. In such circumstances as when doing either one's own aim or the aim of others would damage the other, one makes the aim of others primary. And if great generosity that becomes for the aim of others can be accomplished, one sets one's own ethical discipline in equanimity, as explained above. And if many who seek the Dharma come from afar and one is able to give them the great gift of Dharma, one must set one's own meditation on concentration in equanimity, rise from concentration, and give the gift of Dharma, as the Sutra of Inexhaustible Intelligence teaches. This is what is meant by "whatever becomes for the aim of others, think of that as great or supreme": knowing it to be supreme, one must practice that as primary. The reason one must make giving the Dharma primary, setting aside one's own meditation, for those who seek the Dharma is this: it is taught as a root downfall of bodhisattvas not to give the Dharma to those who seek it when one has it. By this it should also be understood that it is implicitly shown that whatever does not become for the aim of others is small or inferior. Therefore, although in general ethical discipline is the supreme of the two, generosity and ethical discipline, in a special case, if giving brings about a great benefit for others, that very thing must be regarded as supreme, as explained through the example of gold and iron. That is: of these two, in general gold is supreme, but if the gold is very small and the iron very large, it is reasonable to regard the iron as supreme. In this way, "not discarding great generosity for the sake of small ethical discipline" is taught here as a special case.

Further, if one can accomplish both together — keeping the ethical discipline that purifies one's own mind-stream, and giving generosity for the aims of others — one must accomplish them so; and when one cannot, or when it comes down to it, it is taught that for the sake of guarding the great, the small must be set aside in equanimity; and one can extend this by analogy to other cases. However, the earlier teaching that at the time of generosity one sets ethical discipline in equanimity mainly shows the following: for someone who can train in generosity, which is coarse, but cannot train in ethical discipline, which is subtle, when training gradually, one makes generosity primary and for the time being sets ethical discipline in equanimity; so it does not contradict what is explained here.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-83]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

In virtue, for example: the perfections of generosity and so on, "ever higher" — as in "generosity is to be practiced by laypeople, and ethical discipline is to be kept by the ordained," and as in "Better than giving gifts for a hundred years / is keeping ethical discipline for a single day" — in the same way that these practices of the perfections enter with ever more distinction, each higher one above each lower one, so one should practice. As for "for the sake of the lesser, the greater is not given up": among the perfections, for example, giving gifts — above, making offerings to the Three Jewels, and below, giving gifts to lowly beggars — for the sake of such a small thing, the greater above, such as the training in ethical discipline, is not to be given up. This is the meaning. But since it is not definite in one direction only, in the special case it is said that the primary thing is: "a bodhisattva should think of whichever is greater, for the sake of other sentient beings."

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The six perfections are ever more distinguished going upward from the lower to the higher, so one must practice taking the higher as primary; and although, for the sake of something small such as generosity, one does not give up something great such as ethical discipline, the commentators agree that in special cases whatever primarily becomes for the benefit of others must be regarded as supreme and accomplished.

**Key points.**

- The six perfections are ever more distinguished going from earlier to later, so one must practice taking the higher as primary. The earlier ones are coarse and easy and the later ones subtle and difficult, so whenever the lower ones have taken hold, one must train in the higher ones.
- In general, one does not give up something supreme or great such as ethical discipline for the sake of something small or inferior such as generosity; and when the two cannot be done at once, when it comes down to it, the small must be given up in order to guard the great.
- The difference between generosity and ethical discipline is like the difference between the water in a cow's hoofprint and the great ocean, and keeping ethical discipline for a single day is more beneficial than giving gifts for a hundred years.
- In special circumstances one must primarily think of whatever becomes for the benefit of others and accomplish it; when doing either one's own aim in this life or the aim of others would damage the other, one must make the aim of others primary.
- If great generosity that becomes for the aim of others can be accomplished, or if one can give the gift of Dharma to those who seek the Dharma, which becomes a great benefit for others, one must set in equanimity one's own ethical discipline, meditative concentration, and so on, and regard that benefit of others as supreme.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Having understood thus, one should always strive and abide in the benefit of others. By the Buddha, the Blessed One, who is endowed with compassion and sees far, even what is forbidden to those of lesser intellect is permitted to this bodhisattva, the Victor's heir who works for the benefit of others. This should be understood from the sutra Skill in Means of the Great Secret (གསང་ཆེན་ཐབས་ལ་མཁས་པ) and other texts.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

So — when accomplishing any virtuous action, there is no reason to give up, in one's mind, a great need for the sake of a small need; but if, for the sake of accomplishing a great need, one lets go of a small one in one's mind, that is appropriate. One must take up and give up in accord with the occasions of greater and lesser importance of what is to be prevented and what is needed. If there is something needed that is of greater importance than what is to be prevented, one must accomplish it; and if what is to be prevented is of greater importance than the need, one must leave it — so it is taught. In all circumstances, what is of greater and lesser importance is this: placing the benefit of others as the root, and knowing the ways of what to adopt and what to abandon, one must accomplish the aims of others, with skill and wisdom in accomplishing the aims of other sentient beings. "The compassionate one who sees far / permits even what is forbidden in such cases." Thus, in accord with the particularities of place and time, the Teacher, the Blessed One, who is compassionate, directly sees the actual state of the far-distant future, and, in dependence on the disciples, sometimes permits what at another time he had forbidden.

There are two different ways of explaining this root text. If one applies the agentive particle (བྱེད་སྒྲ) in "by the compassionate one who sees far" to the Tathāgata: since the Tathāgata possesses the unsurpassed knowing that sees far into the long-term future, those things that were forbidden for a time to the disciples of his retinue are also permitted when place, time, and circumstances change. Or else: "For the compassionate one who sees the meaning, / even what was forbidden is permitted" — one applies "the compassionate one," that is, "to" great beings who hold in their mind-stream bodhicitta rooted in love and compassion, as the object of permission. Through the courage and the exceptional motivation of such beings, those things that were categorically forbidden to those who seek liberation with the thought of their own aims, such as shravakas and pratyekabuddhas, will be permitted to those special persons — this too is one understanding. And then,

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Third, regarding the ethical discipline of working for sentient beings: of its three — wholeheartedly taking up the aims of others, gathering [disciples] through material goods and the Dharma, and guarding against others' lack of faith — the first is as follows. "Having understood" in this way the ethical discipline of restraining misconduct and the ethical discipline of gathering virtue, and having abided [in them], the ethical discipline of working for sentient beings means this: in accomplishing "the benefit" of other sentient beings, with no thought at all of one's own benefit, one must "always" abide endowed with great "diligence." And for one who is thus diligent, with no thought of his own benefit, the Buddha, who "possesses" great "compassion," "sees" far — that is, sees the way in which, for the sake of attaining great bliss in the long run or ultimately, and of abandoning great suffering, one lets a small immediate happiness decline and brings about a small suffering — and "what is forbidden" to those of the lesser vehicle and to beginning bodhisattvas, the seven non-virtues of body and speech, "he permits" to that great bodhisattva who has no thought of his own benefit. For example, the caravan leader Great Compassion killed the bad man with a short spear in order to save him from falling into hell, and the young brahmin Star engaged in non-celibate conduct in order to save a merchant leader's daughter from the terror of death; and it is said that through these they completed the accumulation of many eons.

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

As explained before, a bodhisattva, knowing the grounds of what to adopt and abandon, should abide always diligent in the benefit of others. If one objects, "Since one must accomplish various activities for the benefit of others, would one not oneself [fall into] fault?" (the rail's wording here, ཉེས་པས་ པར་འགྱུར་རོ, is garbled) — the Buddha, who possesses compassion, sees directly even matters that are distant and hidden, and so those things such as the seven non-virtues of body and speech (the rail reads ལུས་ས་ངག་ངག་གི), which are forbidden to shravakas who mainly pursue their own aims, are permitted to that bodhisattva when the occasion arises; so not only does no fault come about, but a great accumulation comes about. As the sutra Skill in Means of the Great Secret (གསང་ཆེན་ཐབས་ལ་མཁས་པའི་མདོ) says, the caravan leader Great Compassion, by killing the bad man, cooled the samsara of many eons; and as is told in the account of the young brahmin Star. ##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Thus, knowing the distinctions of greater and lesser need, one should, out of compassion, abide always endowed with great diligence in the benefit of others alone. If one wonders, "Since it contradicts ethical discipline, would one not fall into downfalls through [acting for] the aims of others?" — the Buddha, the Blessed One, who has reached the very limit of great compassion for all sentient beings, for great bodhisattvas who look solely to the aims of others, with no regard for their own aims (explained in reference to what is found in the *Compendium of Training*), even what is forbidden in the lesser vehicle — since in that way of the Great Vehicle there are many [things] subsequently permitted — does not become a downfall. <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

As explained before, having understood or known as they are the grounds of adopting and abandoning in general, the distinctions of higher and lower such as generosity, and the distinctions of greater and lesser need that were taught, one should, taking as primary solely the accomplishing of the benefit and happiness of other sentient beings, always generate vast joy for that and abide endowed with especially superior diligence. If one asks: "If, thus making others' benefit primary, one strives to accomplish the aims of sentient beings through the various activities such as generosity, and, setting ethical discipline in equanimity, even engages in what is forbidden and so on, would one not be stained by the fault of contradicting ethical discipline and fall into a downfall?" —

Because the Buddha, the Blessed One himself, who possesses the supreme compassion, sees directly even the hidden matters that are hard to realize and hard to know, the seven non-virtues of body and speech, such as killing and non-celibate conduct, which were taught as forbidden in the sutras common to the vehicles to shravakas and others of lesser intellect who make their own aims primary, he permits to those great-souled bodhisattvas who are untainted by attachment and clinging to their own aims and who, through the force of great compassion, make solely the aims of others primary,

Further, according to the Indian commentaries (རྒྱ་འགྲེལ), "the compassionate one who sees far" must be applied to that very great bodhisattva who looks solely to the aims of others, who is the object to whom the seven non-virtues of body and speech are permitted. Therefore, in explaining how the seven, such as killing, are permitted to bodhisattvas, as for the special features of the basis [the person who engages]: in the old translation of the *Compendium of Training* it reads, "This is to be considered by one who has not attained a ground and who practices the six perfections; it is not for others." In the new translation it says "one who has attained a ground," but it is said that the former is reasonable. However, merely practicing the six perfections without having attained a ground is not enough: it is said that one needs a bodhisattva with the following special features — one in whom familiarity with the path over many eons has gone before; who has the two qualities of skill in means and great compassion; who, having taken the bodhisattva vows, trains properly in what is to be trained; who has a mind that cherishes others more than himself; and who has found no method other than killing and the like — and that it is not permitted to all Mahayanists.

In particular, for the basis [the person] who engages in sexual misconduct (ལོག་གཡེམ), a householder bodhisattva is needed, because for a bodhisattva who holds the ordained vows, engaging in non-celibate conduct and the like is forbidden in every way. Master Śāntarakṣita (ཞི་བ་འཚོ), in his commentary on the *Twenty Verses on the Vows*, says: "An ordained bodhisattva, in order to preserve the teaching of the shravakas from decline, must never in any way engage in non-celibate conduct." If an ordained bodhisattva sees that the benefit for sentient beings would become exceedingly great, he must give up the training, return to being a householder, and engage in it; as the *Compendium of Training* says, "If he sees that this will be of greater benefit to sentient beings, he gives up the training." In this case, the woman who is the object, too, if she is an ordained woman, may not be relied upon, because it says there: "Those who are truly celibate are, for the sake of what is beneficial (དོན་བྱས་པའི་ཕྱིར), shunned from afar and honored, like mothers, sisters, and so on."

Thus, while a householder bodhisattva is set as the basis for non-celibate conduct, for the other six, such as killing, bodhisattvas in general are said to be the basis; yet engaging in the grounds of defeat of killing and of lying (སྲོག་གཅོད་དང་བརྫུན་གྱི་ཕམ་པའི་གཞི) also cannot be done by an ordained person, just as with non-celibate conduct, so if one must engage in them, one must give up the training. As for what becomes a defeat in taking what is not given, taking for one's own aim is required as a component, so however much a bodhisattva steals for the sake of others, it does not become a defeat, and in engaging in this one need not give up the training.

As for the statement that non-celibate conduct is not permitted to ordained bodhisattvas whereas killing and the like are permitted: it is said with the following in mind. Even if the course of action (ལས་ལམ) of killing, taking what is not given, or lying occurs, it is not certain to become a defeat; but for one who is a basis of the precepts, if the course of action of non-celibate conduct occurs, it certainly becomes a defeat. It is said that it is solely with regard to non-celibate conduct that a distinction is made between ordained bodhisattvas and householders. Therefore, engaging in killing and the like of body and speech, apart from engaging in the grounds of defeat, is permitted to both householder and ordained bodhisattvas.

As for the particular ways in which engaging in the seven of body and speech is permitted: the way of engaging in killing is as follows. That bodhisattva, who has the special features of the basis explained above, has as his object a bandit or the like who has set out on an immediate-retribution deed (མཚམས་མེད་པའི་ལས) that is certain to kill many shravakas, pratyekabuddhas, bodhisattvas, and others for a trifling bit of wealth. With the thought, "If I kill this one, I will be born in hell; but it would not be right for this sentient being (the rail reads བླའི་སེམས་ཅན) to do such a deed and go to hell," motivated solely by a compassionate mind that is able to exchange self and others, as the application [of the act], having found no method other than killing to turn him back from that immediate-retribution deed, out of necessity (ཕོངས་ཆོས) he deprives him of life. And it is said that at the time of killing, one's own mind must be in a state of either virtue or the neutral, and one must know oneself that it is never mixed with afflictions and so on.

Engaging in taking what is not given: the object is a king or minister who is for the most part fierce, without compassion, and solely engaged in harming others. With the thought that if he stays in that position much non-virtue will increase, out of compassion, with a mind to benefit, the application is to remove him from that rank and seize it. Further, the object is a bandit who has seized the property of the Sangha or a stupa and wishes to use it himself. With the thought, "If this basis leads to lasting harm and no benefit for him, it is not acceptable," the application is to seize the goods back and offer them to whomever they belong. Further, the object is a steward (ཞལ་ཏ་བྱེད་པ) or a garden-keeper who squanders the property of a stupa or the Sangha, or uses it himself. With the same thought as before, the application is to remove those goods from him. One does any one of these three kinds of taking what is not given.

Engaging in sexual misconduct: the object is a woman who is tormented by desire and wishes to have non-celibate conduct with the bodhisattva himself, and who, if it is not done, would hold a grudge against him and, through the force of desire, would die. With the thought, solely out of compassion, "If, relying on this one's thorough ill-will (ཀུན་ནས་མནར་སེམས) toward me, her non-virtue increases greatly (the rail spells it མངདུ), that would not be acceptable," and by thinking, "If I satisfy her desire, I will lead her into virtue and she will gain freedom in abandoning non-virtue," the application is to engage in intercourse as she wishes.

Engaging in lying: the object is sentient beings who face things to be averted, such as being put to death, having the nose or limbs cut off, or having the eyes gouged out. With the thought of wishing to save them from those harms, out of compassion, the application is knowingly altering their perception and speaking falsely. In this case, if it were for one's own aim it would not be proper to lie even for the sake of one's life, so it is said that one must speak solely for the sake of sentient beings.

Engaging in divisive speech: the object is beings who are held by non-virtuous spiritual friends. With the thought, "If these people come to suffer for a long time by relying on bad companions, that is not acceptable," the application is speaking divisive words in order to separate them from those companions.

Engaging in harsh speech: the object is sentient beings who have entered a wrong path and do improper things. With the thought of wishing to turn them away from non-virtue by the method of speaking harshly and to set them in virtue (the rail spells it ལབསམ་པ), motivated by that intention, the application is speaking through harsh words.

Engaging in idle chatter: the object is beings who are devoted to song, dance, music, and so on, to tales of kings and bandits, to courtesans, to talk of food and drink, and so on. With the thought, out of compassion, of pleasing each one with words and means that he likes, bringing him under one's influence and making him listen, then drawing him away from non-virtue and setting him in virtue, the application is speaking frivolous words (ཀྱལ་བའི་ཚིག) in accord with their interests. Thus, by way of the complete special features of object, thought, application, and so on, whichever of the seven — killing and the rest — one engages in, there is no fault, and merit also increases greatly.

Incidentally, to state briefly the intention behind the statement that attachment (ཆགས་པ) is a minor fault: in the *Questions of Upāli* [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] and "All downfalls involving desire are explained as not being downfalls," and so on, are said. As for the intention of what is said there, the *Compendium of Training* says, "What is the intention of this? It is because the gathering of sentient beings was specified earlier." Its meaning is this: if there is a connection with a very great benefit for sentient beings, as in the story of the young brahmin Star, then such attachment has no fault; it does not teach that all attachment in a bodhisattva's mind-stream is faultless, because in that sutra, just before this, it was said with the specification of the desire that gathers sentient beings. The *Ornament of the Sutras* also says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] and so on. Hatred destroys compassion, so it is said that there is no occasion for permitting it; these points should be learned in detail from the *Compendium of Training* and other texts.

Thus, the permission for killing and the like is stated with regard to what is naturally blameworthy (རང་བཞིན་གྱི་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ); and even if one speaks with regard to what is blameworthy by prohibition (བཅས་པའི་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ), the Detailed Explanation (རྣམ་བཤད) says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below]; and the *Twenty Verses on the Vows*, in "For the sake of beings the action is minor," also shows this very meaning. Since there are many important points to be learned from the commentaries in that context, one should come to understand it from there. These are as in what Master Āryadeva says: [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] and, in the *Twenty Verses on the Vows* too, [the quotation is given under Scriptural Quotations below] — so it is said, and so on.

If one asks, "Then, when a bodhisattva who has the special features of the basis as explained earlier is confronted with such a thing that must be prevented, if he does not engage in the seven such as killing, would it become a fault of wrongdoing (ཉེས་བྱས)?" — Master Jangzang (བྱང་བཟང) holds that the line of the *Twenty Verses on the Vows*, "If done out of compassion there is no non-virtue," teaches a wrongdoing, so that on that view it would become a wrongdoing. But Master Śāntarakṣita (ཞི་འཚོ) and others do not hold that, apart from showing that the text provides occasions of permission for the seven of body and speech, it becomes a wrongdoing if one does not engage in them for the sake of others, and it is also said that to accept it thus is good. These points have been explained only briefly, following the commentaries on the *Compendium of Training* and the *Twenty Verses on the Vows*; for details, they should be learned from those texts and from the Detailed Explanation of the chapter on ethical discipline (ཚུལ་ཁྲིམས་ལེའུའི་རྣམ་བཤད) and so on.

Further, some explain "the compassionate one who sees far" in the sense that the Buddha, who has compassion and sees far, grants permission to the noble bodhisattva; and some also explain "self-seeing" (རང་གཟིགས) as a bodhisattva who, without regard to his own aims, looks solely to the aims of others. This should be examined.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Having understood — that is, having come to know — the ways of guarding the ethical discipline of restraining misconduct and the ethical discipline of gathering virtue, then, "the ethical discipline of working for sentient beings," that is, "abide always diligent in the benefit of others." Of the three ethical disciplines of the bodhisattva vows, beginners should train mainly in the ethical discipline of restraining misconduct; bodhisattvas abiding on the path of accumulation and the path of joining, the ground of devoted conduct (མོས་སྤྱོད་ཀྱི་ས), should, in addition to that, train mainly in the ethical discipline of gathering virtue; and further, bodhisattvas who have attained the first ground and above, in addition to those two, must be diligent in, or abide in, mainly the ethical discipline of working for sentient beings — this too is said. So then, with regard to that bodhisattva who, having utterly abandoned his own aims, abides always diligent in the benefit of others: the perfect Buddha, the Blessed One, who possesses great compassion without a reference point (ཐུགས་དམིགས་པ་མེད་པའི་ཐུགས་རྗེ་ཆེན་པོ), and who sees far into the actual state of affairs of slight immediate suffering and long-term happiness, and likewise of trifling immediate happiness and long-term suffering, permits to those bodhisattvas of very great strength of mind even the seven non-virtues of body and speech, such as killing, which appear as such in form but which he forbade to those of the shravaka and pratyekabuddha lineage and to beginner bodhisattvas. That is, as when the caravan leader Great Compassion, to save the bad man with a short spear — who, having killed five hundred merchants, was about to fall into hell — killed him; and the young brahmin named Star, in order to save a merchant leader's daughter from death, engaged in non-celibate conduct and, for twelve years, kept house (ཁྱིམ་ཐབ་བསྟེན་པ); it is said that through these, too, they completed the accumulation of many eons.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Knowing the distinctions of greater and lesser need, always abide diligent in the benefit of others

###### 2. To bodhisattvas with great compassion who look solely to the aims of others, what is forbidden — the seven non-virtues of body and speech such as killing — is permitted

###### 3. This requires a person with special features such as object and intention; it is not permitted to all Mahayanists

###### 4. On such occasions, letting go of the small for the sake of accomplishing the great, and adopting and abandoning in accord with the occasions of greater and lesser importance of what is to be prevented and what is needed

<!-- sub:divergences -->
#### Divergences Among Commentators

- ⚑ **First way of explaining the agentive particle in "the compassionate one who sees far":** The Buddha, the Tathāgata, who is endowed with compassion and sees far, permits to bodhisattvas of that kind what is forbidden.
- ⚑ **Second way of explaining it:** "Or else," and "according to the Indian commentaries (རྒྱ་འགྲེལ)," "the compassionate one who sees far" is applied to that very great bodhisattva who looks solely to the aims of others, and is explained as the object to whom what is forbidden is permitted.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Knowing the grounds of greater and lesser need and of what to adopt and abandon, one must always be diligent in the benefit of others; and for bodhisattvas who, with no thought for their own aims, devote themselves to the aims of others, it is said that even the seven non-virtues of body and speech that were forbidden to shravakas and beginner bodhisattvas are permitted. However, there are two differing explanations of the application of the agentive particle in the root text's "by the compassionate one who sees far": applying it to the Buddha, the Blessed One, and applying it to the bodhisattva himself.

**Key points.**

- Knowing the distinctions of greater and lesser need and of what to adopt and abandon, one must abide always, out of compassion, with great diligence in the benefit of others, with no thought at all of one's own aims.
- The seven non-virtues of body and speech, such as killing and non-celibate conduct, which are forbidden to shravakas of lesser intellect and to beginner bodhisattvas, are permitted to bodhisattvas endowed with great compassion; not only do they not become a fault, but it is said that a great accumulation is completed.
- The permission of the seven non-virtues is for a bodhisattva who fully has the special features of object, intention, application, and so on; it is said that it is not permitted to all Mahayanists.
- When accomplishing virtue, it is appropriate to let go of a small need for the sake of accomplishing a great need, and one must adopt and abandon in accord with the greater and lesser importance of what is to be prevented and of the need.
