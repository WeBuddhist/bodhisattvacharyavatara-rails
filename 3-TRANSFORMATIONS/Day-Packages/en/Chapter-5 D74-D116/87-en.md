## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the thirty-fourth, thirty-fifth, and thirty-sixth verses of the fifth chapter. This teaching clearly shows that when an affliction arises in the mind, we must remain as steady as a great tree unmoved by the wind.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on this verse give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Before engaging in any action, we must examine what thought has arisen in our own mind. If a harmful or flawed thought has arisen, we must recognize that this thought will cause harm in both this life and future lives.

At that time, we should not act with the body or speak with the voice. We must not let the mind follow after that harmful thought. Just as the wind cannot shake or move a great tree, we must remain steady, keeping our body, speech, and mind from falling under the power of a harmful thought.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, before any action, I will examine my own mind. When an affliction arises, I will remain unmoved, like a tree.

**Explanation:** *(Patience Practice)* It is very important to examine the intention behind our own mind before we do anything. If we see an affliction like anger or pride arising within us, we should not move the body or speak. We must remain steady, like a great tree unmoved by the wind. Taming the mind this way, without falling under the power of the afflictions, is the very heart of this verse of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. This brings the benefit of avoiding unwanted suffering for both ourselves and others, and of gaining great peace of mind.

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-34`):*

> At the outset, recognizing that this mind is full of faults, I should hold it steady, as one holds a piece of wood in place.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

For the time being, before engaging in any action at all, having examined one's own mind and found that mind to be afflicted, one should recognize: "This mind of mine is faulty." At that time, I should not act with the body, should not speak with speech, and, unmoved by discursive thought, should remain able to hold steady, just like a piece of wood.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that "for the time being" means: for instance, before entering upon whatever conduct one is engaged in — walking, sitting, eating, sleeping, and so on — one should first know in advance what is suitable and what is not suitable to that occasion. If some sudden circumstance threatens to lead one into an unsuitable situation, one must catch it at once with awareness and restrain it. At that time, thinking, "I must not enter into a situation like this," one should remain able to hold the mind steady, like a piece of wood, for that occasion. Likewise, having recognized through mindfulness that an afflicted state of mind is faulty, if while sitting one comes close to feeling pride arise on meeting another person, one must recognize this at once with awareness and restrain it. For example, when reflecting on the Chinese Communist Party's oppression of Tibet threatens to give rise to anger, one should also think, from another angle, how this very adversity has made the world at large take much greater notice of, and lend much greater support to, Tibet's religion and culture, and how the harsher the Chinese repression has been, the more people have come forward to support the Tibetan cause — reflecting in this way can itself help one remain like a piece of wood; at times, reflecting on the positive qualities involved can also help.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the third topic: the training in conduct that cultivates the mind through mindfulness and awareness, which has three parts — the ethical discipline of restraining wrongdoing, the ethical discipline of gathering virtuous qualities, and the ethical discipline of acting for the welfare of beings. The first of these has two parts: purifying all conduct of the three doors, and guarding the trainings without letting them decline. As for the first: relying on mindfulness and awareness at all times and in every way, one should, for the time being, first watch whatever discursive thought arises in one's own mind-stream, and examine whether this state of mind, or this thought, is virtuous or non-virtuous. If it is a non-virtuous thought, one should recognize, "This is faulty, for it will cause harm in both this life and the next." At that time, one should not act with the body, should not speak with speech, and should not let the mind follow after that bad thought; just as a great tree is unmoved by the wind, one must remain able to hold steady, not falling under the power of the bad thought into which one's mind has strayed toward its object. For example — just as one strikes a heated hide-scraper, or strikes a pig's snout — whatever bad thought arises must be crushed the very instant it arises.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

This is the third training in the conduct that guards the mind through mindfulness and awareness, which has three parts: training in the ethical discipline of restraint, in gathering virtuous qualities, and in the ethical discipline that acts for the welfare of beings. At the time of walking and so forth, one should, for the time being, first recognize: "This intention, this state of mind, is faulty," or, "this one is faultless." Then, once a faulty intention has arisen, one must apply the antidote and remain able to hold steady, unmoved by the fault, just like a piece of wood.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

This is the second topic, the method of guarding awareness, which has three parts: awareness sent ahead before an action; awareness engaged together with the action; and, in brief, the defining nature of guarding awareness. As for the first: for the time being, at the outset of engaging in an action, having examined the mind and found it afflicted, one should recognize, "This mind of mine is faulty." At that time, I should remain able to hold steady, like a piece of wood — that is, unmoved by that affliction.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

For the time being, at the outset, whatever action one engages in, having examined one's intention and found this mind afflicted with attachment, aversion, or the like, one should recognize, "This is faulty, for it produces suffering in samsara." At that time, I should apply the antidote and remain able to hold steady, unmoved, like a great tree in the wind — or, alternatively, as some commentaries explain, just as wood, being devoid of the sense-faculties, does not engage with objects of attachment and aversion, so too one should close the doors of the senses and remain able to hold firmly steady.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For the time being, whatever action one engages in — walking, sitting, and so on — one should rely on mindfulness and awareness, and, before entering into that action, first examine one's own mental intention well with awareness, recognizing, "This state of mind is faulty," or "this one is faultless," and accordingly engage in or refrain from that action. If the intention is found to be an afflicted state of mind such as attachment or aversion, one should recognize, "This afflicted state of mind is faulty, for it produces unwanted harm and suffering." At that time, I should not engage in any action prompted by that intention, and, just as a great tree cannot be shaken or moved by the wind, so too I should not fall under the power of that afflicted state of mind, but should remain unmoved, holding my own ground with the antidote, able to hold steady. Or else, in some Sanskrit texts and Indian commentaries it is said, "like wood devoid of the senses," meaning that just as wood, being devoid of the sense-faculties, does not engage with objects that give rise to attachment and the like, so too one should close the doors of the senses and remain able to hold steady.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"For the time being" means: at the outset, when one examines whatever discursive thought arises in one's own mind, if a non-virtuous thought is found to have arisen, one should recognize that this state of mind is faulty, causing harm in both this life and future lives. At that time, I should not act with the body, should not speak with speech, and should not let the mind follow after that bad thought; just as a great tree is unmoved by the wind, I must remain firm and able to hold steady.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-34]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

**A Great Tree Unmoved by the Wind.** This is the image most commonly given by the commentaries: when a faulty intention or discursive thought arises, one should remain unmoved by it — none of body, speech, or mind following after it — just as a great tree cannot be shaken or moved by the wind.

**Striking a Hot Hide-Scraper, Pounding a Pig's Snout.** Khenpo Kunzang Pelden offers this image to convey the speed with which any non-virtuous thought that arises must be crushed, the very instant it arises.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Together Open a New Section on the Ethical Discipline of Restraining Wrongdoing

As Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, and others explain in agreement, this verse marks the start, within the conduct that trains the mind through mindfulness and awareness, of the section on training in the ethical discipline of restraining wrongdoing — establishing the essential point that one must examine, before entering upon any action, whether one's own intention is faulty.

###### 2. ⚑ Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Sabzang Mati Panchen Note an Alternate, Sanskrit-Supported Reading of "Like Wood"

On the meaning of the image "like wood," some interpreters note that certain Sanskrit sources and Indian commentaries explain it instead as "wood devoid of the sense-faculties" — a reading that Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Sabzang Mati Panchen both present as an alternative: closing the five sense-doors so that they do not engage objects of attachment and aversion. This accords with the Sanskrit root text's own wording, *nirindriyeṇaiva…kāṣṭhavat* ("as if without senses… like wood").

###### 3. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Draws a Contemporary Example from His Own Practice

His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives a modern illustration of this practice, drawing on the occasion when reflecting on the Chinese Communist Party's oppression of Tibet threatens to give rise to anger: at such a time, reflecting from another angle on how this very adversity has led the world at large to take much greater notice of, and give much greater support to, Tibetan religion and culture — and how the harsher the repression, the more supporters have come forward for the Tibetan cause — itself helps to pacify the anger, offering another means, alongside remaining like a piece of wood, of practicing this verse.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse establishes the central basis for putting into practice the training in the ethical discipline of restraining wrongdoing: before engaging in any action of body or speech, one must examine one's own intention. If an afflicted or faulty intention is seen to have arisen in the mind, one must remain unmoved, like wood, without letting the conduct of body or speech follow after it. This is a crucial point of practice for guarding the mind through mindfulness and awareness.

**Key points.**

- **Matching the image to its meaning.** This verse opens, within the chapter's treatment of the three ethical disciplines, the section on the discipline of restraining wrongdoing. The essence of this training is to examine one's own intention continually, and, the moment an afflicted thought arises, to immediately halt the erratic conduct of body and speech that would follow it. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden illustrates the speed required with a vivid image: as fast as striking a heated hide-scraper, or striking a pig's snout, the very instant such a thought arises it must be crushed.
- **⚑ Different positions among the commentaries.** There is also a difference in how the commentaries explain this passage. As cited in the commentaries of Minyak Kunzang Sonam and Sabzang Mati Panchen, certain Indian commentaries render the root text's phrasing as "like wood devoid of the sense-faculties" (Sanskrit *nirindriyeṇa…kāṣṭhavat*) — meaning to close the doors of the senses so that they do not engage objects of attachment and aversion. This accords, in substance, with the more commonly known rendering, "remain able to hold steady, like wood," even if the two emphases differ slightly.
- **A contemporary illustration.** Likewise, His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama, applying this to present-day practice, teaches that when anger threatens to arise at the thought of the Chinese Communist Party's oppression of Tibet, one may not only remain unmoved like wood, but also reflect on how this same adversity has brought the world's attention and support to Tibet's religion and culture in far greater measure — a reflection that itself helps pacify anger, and offers a further skillful means for practicing this discipline of stilling the mind's harmful discursive thought.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that I must never look at objects toward which a pointless, unrestrained mind wanders off; rather, keeping the mind fixed at all times on virtuous objects of focus, I should not let my eyes roam unrestrained, but should keep them lowered — or, if I must look at something for some reason, I should do so while remaining conscientious.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-35]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

At all times and in every situation, looking about with wandering, distracted eyes — with no purpose connected to the Dharma or any real benefit — this I should never do. It is not merely a matter of putting on an appearance for others to see; rather, fixing my mind with certainty on what is to be adopted and discarded, or on suchness itself, I should always keep my eyes slightly open, lowered toward the tip of the nose or to about the length of a yoke. If, apart from this, some attachment, aversion, or the like arises in the mind, the eyes tend to follow after it, and one's state of mind becomes visibly apparent in the eyes; therefore, he teaches, one should not look about with distracted eyes.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-35]]

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Looking about with the mind pointlessly distracted in various directions — this I should never do, for it causes one's good qualities to decline. Fixing my mind with certainty on virtuous objects of focus, I should always look with my eyes lowered to about the length of a yoke.

Looking about with eyes distracted elsewhere, for no purpose or benefit — this I should never do. ##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Looking about at various objects and directions by letting the mind become distracted through the sense-door of the eyes, for no purpose or benefit — this I should never do at any time; for if I do, seeing all manner of pleasant and unpleasant objects gives rise to afflictions such as attachment and aversion, and the mind becomes thoroughly degraded. Therefore, when first setting out to look, fixing the mind with certainty on some purposeful object — such as a virtuous object of focus, or properly fixing it on what is to be adopted and discarded — I should always keep my eyes appropriately open or closed and lowered toward the tip of the nose, or to about the length of a yoke, looking with the gaze of concentration. This only prohibits looking about with purposeless distraction; it does not prohibit a purposeful gaze and the like. As the *Saṃcayagāthā* says: "Fully possessed of awareness while walking, standing, sleeping, and sitting, gazing no farther than a yoke's length while walking, with the mind unconfused."

Rather, fixing my mind with certainty on what is to be adopted and discarded, I should always keep my eyes lowered to about the length of a yoke, or about an arm-span, and look straight ahead.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Two Reasons Are Given for Why an Aimless, Distracted Gaze Must Be Abandoned

This verse teaches, at the very start of the discipline of bodily conduct, how one should look with the eyes; two different explanations are given for why a distracted, purposeless gaze must be abandoned. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that when attachment or aversion arises, this becomes visibly apparent in the movement of the eyes, causing embarrassment before others; Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen states more briefly that it is because one's good qualities decline.

###### 2. ⚑ Minyak Kunzang Sonam Notes a Difference in How the Commentaries Place This Verse Within Their Outline

Minyak Kunzang Sonam clearly notes a difference among interpreters as to the outline structure of the text at this point: most commentaries explain this verse, too, as chiefly concerned with abandoning unnecessary conduct on the basis of mindfulness (following the twelve kinds of conduct governed by mindfulness in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*), whereas the Great Commentary — Prajñākaramati's *Pañjikā* — explains that from here the text begins to teach how one engages in conduct by examining, with awareness, the state of the three doors. Though the final intent of the two need not conflict, their outline placement differs.

###### 3. This Prohibition Does Not Extend to a Purposeful Gaze

Minyak Kunzang Sonam clearly notes that this prohibition on a wandering gaze applies only to looking without purpose; it does not prohibit a purposeful gaze — such as looking to check for dangers on the road, as taught in verse 5-37.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse settles an important point within the branch of guarding bodily conduct through mindfulness and awareness: how to restrain the conduct of the eyes. Having abandoned a gaze that wanders and roams pointlessly, one should fix the mind with certainty and proceed with the eyes lowered to about the length of a yoke. This is not merely an artificial affectation, but among the very best of methods for firmly guarding the inner mindfulness and awareness of the mind through restraining the outer senses.

**Key points.**

- **Different outlines among the commentaries.** As Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains, there is a difference of opinion among the various commentarial traditions as to how this verse's subject matter is organized. Most Tibetan great commentaries include this verse, as he states, within the scope of guarding mindfulness through abandoning unnecessary conduct — a branch of the twelve kinds of mindfulness found in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* — while Prajñākaramati's *Pañjikā* holds that it is here that the text begins to teach directly how awareness examines the state of the three doors in engaging conduct. Though the two need not ultimately conflict in meaning, they differ in their outline structure.
- **The reason for this practice.** As for the actual practice: while walking, sitting, and so on, one's gaze should not be distracted to the left or right, but should be lowered toward a space about a yoke's length in front. The reason for this, as Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains, is that discursive thoughts of attachment or aversion arising in the mind become plainly visible in the change of one's eyes and manner of looking, causing one's own faults to be displayed to others; and as Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen states, it is because a wandering, pointless gaze causes one's own good qualities to decline.
- **The gaze this does not prohibit.** Nevertheless, as Minyak Kunzang Sonam and His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama both clarify, what is prohibited here is only a pointless, distracted gaze; a purposeful gaze — such as looking to check for dangers on the road — is not prohibited, and one should look at such times, too, with conscientiousness. This manner accords fully and without contradiction with the Buddha's own flawless word, as found in the *Saṃcayagāthā*: "While walking, standing, sleeping, or sitting, possessed of awareness, gazing to a yoke's length."

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentaries Agree That the Gaze May Rest When Body and Mind Become Overly Tired

Although mindfulness and awareness well restrain the conduct of the eyes, all the commentaries agree that when the body and mind become overly tired, one may occasionally look to the side for the sake of resting; this establishes the point that the discipline of mindfulness and awareness must be applied appropriately, without falling to either extreme of fault.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, and His Holiness the Dalai Lama Agree That One Should Greet Others with Pleasant Words and a Smile

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, and His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama all agree that, upon meeting another person, one should abandon the fault of pretending not to see them, look at them properly with a smiling face, and put them at ease with pleasant words (such as "It is good that you have come" or "I hope you have not become weary") — clearly establishing the point that this practice of mindfulness and awareness must be matched with speech that expresses considerate conduct toward others.

###### 3. Ngulchu Thogme Notes an Alternate Reading of the Root Text in Some Commentaries

Ngulchu Thogme notes that some commentaries give a different reading of the root text, "look for the sake of saying, 'welcome,'" and explain the verse accordingly.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches a skillful method by which the discipline, taught above, of not looking beyond a yoke's length does not become rigid or inflexible. The training in mindfulness and awareness is not meant to render one as stiff and thought-free as a piece of wood; rather, this verse makes clear the important point of how one should act fittingly, with rest for the body and mind, and with conduct that is kind and respectful toward others.

**Key points.**

- **The practice of resting the gaze.** The chief point of this passage is that when the eyes and mind become tired from a long period of looking downward, one is permitted to look occasionally in the four directions for the sake of rest; hence the practice of guarding this discipline is taught to be one that must be applied skillfully, not held rigidly like a thought-free piece of wood by its very nature.
- **Conduct upon meeting others.** Likewise, if another person happens to come into one's field of vision, one should not, out of a concern to guard one's ethical discipline, evade them or make as if not to notice them; rather, one should look at them directly, and, with a smile, speak a respectful and auspicious greeting fitting to local custom, such as "It is good that you have come." As Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, and others explain, asking with affectionate concern, "Have you not become weary on the road?" or "Did you have a comfortable journey?" is, in substance, a skillful means of extending loving-kindness in keeping with the practice of mindfulness and awareness, not in conflict with it.
- **A variant reading of the root text.** Ngulchu Thogme notes that some other Indian commentaries give a variant of the root text, reading "look for the sake of saying, 'welcome,'" though the essential meaning remains fixed on the single aim of showing others harmony and respect. The practice of guarding one's discipline is, therefore, not a matter of a rigid temperament at odds with other people, but precisely a matter of acting toward others, on the very basis of mindfulness and awareness, with loving-kindness and respect.
