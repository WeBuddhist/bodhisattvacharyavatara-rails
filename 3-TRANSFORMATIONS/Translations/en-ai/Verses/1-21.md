## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three commentators agree that Verse 1-21 introduces the **Headache Analogy** (*śiraḥśūla* / *klad nad kyi dpe*) as a logical stepping stone to demonstrate the immeasurable merit of the aspiring mind of enlightenment. They explain how even a highly localized and limited benevolent intention directed toward others carries boundless positive energy, which serves as a minor reflection of the vastness of Bodhicitta.

### 1. The Headache Analogy & Focus of Attitude
*   **Consensus on Merit:** All three commentators emphasize that even a simple, worldly intention to relieve a minor physical ailment—specifically a headache—for other sentient beings is endowed with immeasurable merit (*aprameya-puṇya* / *bsod nams dpag med*). 
*   **The Scope of Focus (*ālambana-ākāra* / *dmigs rnam*):**
    *   **Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen** explains that wishing to cure a headache represents a "small focus and aspect" (*dmigs rnam chung ngu*). Despite its limited scope, it still constitutes a genuine "beneficial attitude" (*hitāśaya* / *phan gdags bsam pa*) toward others, which is why it yields such vast merit.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** similarly defines it as a "helpful attitude" (*phan bsam*) characterized by the "benevolent thought of wishing to clear just a minor headache of a few sentient beings with medicine."
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** refers to the suffering sentient beings as the "objects of focus" (*dmigs yul*). He asserts that even when the object and the scope of the wish are highly restricted, the underlying altruistic impulse carries immense power.

### 2. The Narrative of Maitrakanyā (The Merchant's Daughter)
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen's Detailed Account:** Sazang Mati Panchen is the only commentator among the three to provide a full narrative of the classical story of the merchant's daughter/son, **Maitrakanyā** (Tibetan: *Mdz'a-bo'i Bu-mo*). He recounts that:
    1.  Maitrakanyā kicked her mother's head and went to sea to fetch jewels.
    2.  Due to the ripening of this negative action, she was reborn in a temporary hell realm (*prādeśika-naraka* / *nye 'khor gyi dmyal ba*), where she experienced the intense agony of a burning iron wheel revolving on her head.
    3.  In the midst of this torture, she generated a powerful thought of compassion: *"May the suffering of all sentient beings who would experience such pain ripen upon me!"*
    4.  By the power of this single altruistic thought, the iron wheel vanished; she died immediately and was reborn as a god in the Tuṣita heaven (or general celestial realm).
    Sazang uses this story to illustrate that even in the lowest realms, a localized thought of taking on others' head pain has the power to shatter immense negativity and yield divine rebirth.
*   **Ngulchu Thokme's Reference:** Ngulchu Thokme acknowledges this narrative tradition, noting briefly that *"some commentators tell the story of the daughter of the merchant Maitra"* to explain how wishing to clear a headache with medicine brings immeasurable merit.
*   **Gyaltsab Je's Omission:** In contrast, Gyaltsab Je omits the story of Maitrakanyā entirely. He focuses purely on the logical and structural analysis of the analogy, contrasting the "small focus and aspect" of curing a headache with the infinite scope of Bodhicitta (which is elaborated in Verse 1-22).
