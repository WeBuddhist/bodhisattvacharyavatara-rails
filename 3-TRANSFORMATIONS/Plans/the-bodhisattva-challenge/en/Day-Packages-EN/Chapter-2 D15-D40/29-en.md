# Day 29 — Gone like a dream, but the wrongdoing stays

**Date:** Aug 3  
**Chapter:** 2  
**Verses covered:** 2-36 to 2-38

<!-- sec:challenge -->
## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 36 through 38 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. We treat our friends, our rivals, and our possessions as if they were going to stay, and we pile up wrongdoing for their sake. In fact every pleasure and every difficulty of this life passes the way last night's dream passes and becomes nothing but a memory, and we ourselves are here only briefly, with no fixed date of leaving. These verses show us that plainly, and urge us to drop the misdeeds that rest on pointless attachment and dislike.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

Think about last night's dream. You ate something in it, you wore clothes, you sat at home, you had an argument with someone. You woke up, and all that is left is a faint sense that you dreamt something. Now think about the early part of this life — the relatives and neighbours you ate and travelled with, the fights you had. Look for them now and you find the same faint trace. That is what everything we have used comes to.

The people we did wrong for are already gone, many of them within this one life. The wrongdoing is not gone, and the commentaries disagree in an interesting way about how it stays. Khenpo Kunzang Palden says it stays the way a shadow does, never separating, waiting there in front of us. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk says something narrower: it stands before our own mind as an imprint, and as nothing more than that.

Either way the trade is bad. The act is over and cannot be undone. The reason we did it dissolved quickly. What it left in us is still being carried.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, remembering that my possessions and the people I like and dislike are as passing as a dream, I will let go of attachment or dislike the moment I notice it rising.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the Mind)* Attachment and dislike feel reasonable while they last, because whatever they are aimed at seems solid and important at that moment. Today I will test that. When I catch myself pulled toward something I want or hardened against someone I resent, I will pause and remember that this will pass the way last night's dream passed, and end up as a memory and nothing more. Then I will let the pull go and carry on with what I was doing. Practising this a few times in a day loosens the grip these reactions have on us and leaves the mind noticeably quieter.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

*(In this teaching the meaning of the verse is explained first and the verse itself is recited afterwards, so the commentary words below are a summary of what was said before the recitation of 2-36.)*

Saying "we were happy back then" is simply an object of memory. There is no difference at all between that and last night's dream, which today is only something to remember. Beyond the fact that there is something left to recall in our thoughts, it will not come again; nor is it something that stays on permanently without perishing. It becomes an object of memory, and then it perishes.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Here is the reasoning for that. Take last night's dream: whatever one experienced in it — enjoying the five sense pleasures beginning with form, subduing enemies, protecting relatives, gaining wealth and honour — once one woke this morning it became no more than an object of memory; it is not actually seen. In just the same way, all the doings of yesterday and before — taking up and rejecting among the five objects such as form, subduing enemies and protecting relatives, trade and farming, gain and honour, reputation, food and clothing and enjoyments, and whatever else one wanted and made use of — all of that can at most become a mere object of memory: "today I had such and such an experience." What has already passed and ceased cannot now be seen, and no benefit or harm from it can be experienced. So all that working for their sake was pointless.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

At the time of one's own death it is just as with the happiness of kingship and the like that one experiences in a dream: whatever things one made use of become no more than objects of memory, and nothing that has passed is seen again. Thus the underlying gloss sets it out.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Just as a little happiness experienced in a dream becomes, when one wakes, no more than an object of memory, so whatever things one made use of before and imagined to be happiness become, at the time of death, no more than objects of memory. Therefore one should resolve again and again, from this very moment, to do nothing but Dharma. Whatever has passed will not be seen again; it is used up into a mere object of memory.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Here verses 2-35, 2-36 and 2-37 are gathered together under one number and explained as one.)*

Thus it is said. The enemies for whose sake wrongdoing was done come to be no more; friends too come to be no more; and I myself come to be no more. In the same way all beings and all enjoyments come to be no more, so the wrongdoing done for their sake was pointless. If one thinks, "but surely the happiness of pleasing one's relatives and the like does have a point, since it follows one into the next life too" — then: whatever such happiness and the like one makes use of now, all of it may later become a mere object of memory, but it will not be seen or experienced, because it has passed. It is like the happiness experienced in a dream, which is no longer experienced once one wakes. Never mind a succession of lives: even within this one lifetime, while one is still alive, many friends and many enemies have passed away, and one can see for oneself that the happiness of keeping company with them does not come back, just as a dream does not. And yet whatever habitual tendency of unbearable wrongdoing was made for their sake stands right in front of one, as if waiting.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Though one experiences all sorts of pleasure and pain in a dream, on waking it becomes a mere object of memory and is not actually seen. In the same way, whatever contaminated things one made use of — fighting with enemies, keeping company with relatives, eating food, amassing wealth and so on — all become a mere object of memory: "I had such and such an experience." Everything that has passed will not be seen again afterwards, nor will it stay; so it is not right to pile up wrongdoing for their sake. And as for seeing enemies and relatives again once they have passed on to another life — that is out of the question.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Here verses 2-34 through 2-38 are gathered together under one number and explained as one; this is the portion belonging to 2-36.)*

Though in a dream one experiences all sorts of pleasure and pain, prosperity and ruin, when one wakes all of it becomes a mere object of memory and is not actually seen or experienced. In just the same way, in this dream-like life one enjoys food and clothing and wealth together with relatives, friends and loved ones and so tastes happiness; and one quarrels and rages and fights with enemies and so tastes suffering. Whatever such pleasure and pain, and whatever contaminated things served as their cause, one made use of — all of it, when death comes later, is like waking from a dream that was still being dreamt: it becomes a mere object of memory, "formerly I had such and such an experience." Whatever has passed will not be seen or experienced again, because by then those objects of experience have already ceased.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Just as one experiences a dream" — take the example. Last night, while asleep, one may have dreamt of eating food, wearing clothes, living comfortably in a house and so on; or one may have experienced being harmed by enemies, spirits, demons and obstructors, and fighting with them or growing angry at them. Either way, after waking this morning, all of it amounts to nothing at all beyond a faint recollection — "what a strange dream I had last night" — and no actual thing remains. Exactly as in that example, whatever things one made use of in the earlier part of this present life — whether keeping company and eating together with one's own relatives and kin, one's father's household, brothers and friends, fellow villagers and neighbours; or fighting and growing angry with enemies and harm-doers, competing and vying with them, and doing all the deeds that go with that — all those things are, if one thinks back now, nothing but a faint memory: they are not there. They have become nothing more than objects of memory. Everything one enjoyed before, good or bad, has passed, has become non-existent, has become empty of any thing; so there is no enjoying or seeing any of it again.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-36]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **dream-experience** (རྨི་ལམ་) → the way that everything one made use of in this life is used up, at the time of death, into a mere object of memory and is never seen again. Though one experiences all sorts of pleasure and pain in last night's dream, on waking one does not actually see any of it and it becomes a mere object of memory. In the same way, all the things one made use of in this life — subduing enemies, protecting relatives, food, clothing, wealth and goods and the rest — likewise become, at the time of death, no more than objects of memory, and are not seen or experienced again. All the commentaries interpret it in agreement on this point.

- **The dream example set out in detail — eating food, wearing clothes, living comfortably in a house, fighting with enemies** (རྨི་ལམ་གྱི་དཔེ་ཞིབ་བཤད་— ཟས་ཟོས་པ། གོས་གྱོན་པ། གནས་ཁང་དུ་སྐྱིད་པོར་སྡོད་པ། དགྲ་དང་འཐབ་པ།) → the way that, after waking, none of the dream's things are there at all, and nothing is left beyond "a faint recollection of having dreamt." Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk works the dream example out separately, illustrating it with food and clothing and lodging and quarrelling with enemies, and explains that all the things of the earlier part of this life too — keeping company and eating with relatives, kin, fellow villagers and neighbours, or quarrelling and fighting with enemies — amount to no more than a faint memory when one thinks back on them now.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Everything One Has Experienced Is Used Up, Like a Dream, Into a Mere Object of Memory

All the commentaries explain in agreement that every single thing one made use of in this life becomes, like last night's dream when one wakes (that is, at the time of death), no more than an object of memory, and that nothing which has passed is actually seen or experienced again.

###### 2. Since Nothing That Has Passed Is Ever Seen Again, It Follows That the Wrongdoing Done for Friends and Enemies Was Pointless

This is the completion of the reasoning begun at verses 2-34 and 2-35: because everything one made use of before is used up into a mere object of memory, Kunzang Pelden and Sabzang Mati Panchen make it clear that piling up wrongdoing in order to protect friends and defeat enemies was pointless, indeed mistaken.

###### 3. The Dream Example Worked Out in Detail

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk works that same dream example out separately in terms of eating food, wearing clothes, living comfortably in a house, and fighting with enemies, and explains that the moment one wakes there is nothing left beyond "a faint recollection of having dreamt."

###### 4. The Instruction to Apply Oneself to Dharma From This Very Moment

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen advises reflecting again and again on the way that all things become mere objects of memory, and resolving over and over, from this very moment, to do nothing but Dharma.

###### 5. Present Happiness and Pain Too Must Be Seen as No Different From a Dream

His Holiness the Dalai Lama advises that since even our present experience of happiness later becomes no more than an object of memory, there is no difference at all between it and last night's dream.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Even in this present life, while we are still alive, many friends and many enemies have passed away" — and once they are nothing more than an object of memory, whatever we treated as so important at the time, taking on hardship for it, and all the wrongdoing we piled up by lying, boasting, deceiving and cheating: all of it, at the very moment it was done, deposited a habitual tendency, a potency, upon the consciousness that was then its cause. From then on it is like a ledger. The tangled, uncomfortable account of what came in and what went out has to be settled, and each of us is left to settle our own. The very people for whose sake that tangle was made have already passed away; and having passed, they amount to no more than an entry in a book or a piece of history — they cannot become anything real that connects with our life to help or harm us. Even in this one life, while we are alive, many friends and many enemies keep passing away: an enemy of the first half of life becomes a relative in the second half, and a relative of the first half becomes an enemy in the second. Someone who a year or two or a few years ago was "my dear, close, honest friend," with whom one drank together and laid plans in private, turns into an enemy over some trifling circumstance. As Je Gungthangpa said: "Today's trusted friend, over a single word said well or badly, is built up by thought into tomorrow's outright enemy — so cut, from that, the grasping at near and far." The heart-close friend one loved and trusted a year or two ago becomes, a few years later, an outright enemy because of something said well or badly, some manner shown well or badly; and then the one exposes the other's hidden lies, the one digs out the other's faults. "But the wrong I did because of them is still right here, waiting for me": the particular objects for whose sake we acted have ceased, while the wrongdoing each of us piled up, taking on hardship for their sake, remains within our own continuum. Unless an antidote arises and removes it, unless we manage to purify the wrongdoing, its potency to ripen into a result sits there always, ready to come to life.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-37]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Never mind the enemies and relatives of former lives: even in this present life, while one is still alive, many friends and many enemies have passed away — they have died and become mere objects of memory, and they can do one not the slightest actual benefit or harm. If one thinks, "then the wrongdoing done for their sake has also passed, and will not harm me" — that is not so. The wrongdoing done for the sake of those friends and enemies on the basis of attachment and aversion, whose ripening is unbearable to undergo, no more separates from one than a shadow separates from the body: it stands right in front of one, as if waiting. As it says in the *Advice to a King*: "When his time draws near and the king must go, retinue and enjoyments, kin and friends do not follow after him; but wherever that person is born, his actions follow him like a shadow." As for "standing in front of one," whatever the fruit of virtue or wrongdoing may be: the Tathāgata led Nanda to the heaven of the Thirty-Three and to the hells, and there Nanda saw the divine palace and the hell-fires and cauldrons that were to be the places of his own birth. And when the householder Radiant merely marked out the lines of the pleasure-grove he was to offer to the Buddha, Śāriputra said that its ripened result was already accomplished from that moment on. It is like this: just as, when a great lama or great personage travels somewhere today, his attendants prepare the ground, make the arrangements and come out to receive him, so — according to each one's own karmic appearances — those with wrongdoing have the vision of Yama's agents coming out to receive them and leading them off to the hells and the like, while those with virtue have the vision of gods and lamas receiving them and leading them to pure realms. So it is taught.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"For a while" — that is, first of all, even in the earlier part of this present life, while one is still living, "many friends and many enemies have passed away." Friends: one's own dear ones and relatives, those of one's father's household, brothers and companions and the like, whether of one's own age or somewhat older or younger — many such have already died and gone. Enemies: those of the type of foes and obstructors — many of these too have passed away and died. And the wrongdoing done for their sake: for the sake of friends, out of attachment and clinging, with the thought of wanting to help them, killing others, stealing and so on, many such wrongdoings; and for the sake of enemies and obstructors, out of the evil intention of repaying hostility and harm, taking up evil means and killing others, beating them, robbing their wealth and so on. All that unbearable — that is, thoroughly evil — non-virtuous wrongdoing has become nothing at all, except that it remains in front of one's own mind, in the form of habitual tendencies.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR -->
##### BCACXX_WR — The Story of Groshünkye

This story is anchored to the line of this verse, "whatever unbearable wrongdoing there was stands in front of me." A son of the chieftain Balasena was born already wearing precious earrings, under the star Groshün, and so was named Groshünkye. When he grew up, since his father's line was that of sea-captains, he went out with merchants to the ocean to fetch jewels. Having obtained many jewels, he grew attached to their worth, and towards dawn he fell asleep; his merchant companions turned back without waiting for him, and he was left behind alone. Waking to find his companions gone, he loaded a donkey and set out; but on the road the donkey bolted and threw off the load, and he, starving, met with hungry ghosts, gods and various non-human beings. They told him how, as the ripened result of wrongdoing done in former lives — malice, miserliness, killing, sexual misconduct and the rest — some had been born as gods and yet suffered in hell by day, and how they underwent all sorts of such conditions. In this way the story shows that the unbearable wrongdoing done in former lives for the sake of friends and enemies, even though those friends and enemies have died, does not pass away but stands in front of one, and that its ripening is unfailing. In the end, it is said, Groshünkye met the noble Kātyāyana, delivered to his parents the messages of the hungry ghosts and the gods, then went forth into the homeless life and attained the fruit of not returning again.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Though Friends and Enemies Have Passed Away, the Unbearable Wrongdoing Done for Their Sake Stands in Front of One

As all the commentaries explain in agreement: even within this one life many friends and many enemies have already passed away, and yet the unbearable wrongdoing done for their sake on the basis of attachment and aversion does not pass away — it stands right in front of one.

###### 2. The Fruit of Wrongdoing Never Separates From One, as a Shadow Never Separates From the Body

Kunzang Pelden cites the *Advice to a King* to explain that wherever a person is born, his actions follow him like a shadow.

###### 3. What "Friends" and "Enemies" Are, and Concrete Examples of the Wrongdoing Done for Their Sake

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk distinguishes friends (dear ones and relatives, one's father's household, brothers and companions) from enemies (foes and obstructors), and sets out concrete examples: for the sake of friends, killing and stealing out of attachment and clinging; for the sake of enemies, killing, beating and robbing them of their wealth in order to repay hostility and harm.

###### 4. "Standing in Front of One" Means Remaining in the Mental Continuum in the Form of a Habitual Tendency or a Potency

Ngulchu Thogme speaks of the "habitual tendency" of unbearable wrongdoing, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk of its "standing in front of one's own mind, in the form of habitual tendencies," and His Holiness the Dalai Lama of a "habitual tendency or potency already deposited upon the consciousness" — all three agree.

###### 5. The Status of Friend and Enemy Is Itself Impermanent, and the Two Can Even Change Places

His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives examples from present-day life: an enemy of the first half of life becoming a relative in the second half, and a relative of the first half becoming an enemy in the second; and he cites Je Gungthangpa to show how a friend can turn into an enemy within a few days.

###### 6. The Story of Groshünkye as an Illustration That Actions and Their Results Are Unfailing

Those born as hungry ghosts and as gods show directly how the unbearable wrongdoing done in former lives for the sake of friends and enemies does not pass away, even after death, but stands in front of one and ripens into the fruit of suffering.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse explains that even within this one lifetime many friends and many enemies have already passed away, and yet the unbearable wrongdoing done for their sake on the basis of attachment and aversion does not pass away but stands right in front of oneself; all the commentaries interpret it in agreement.

**Key points.**

- Friends and enemies themselves have passed away, but the unbearable ripening of the wrongdoing done for their sake does not pass away — it stands in front of one.
- That the fruit of wrongdoing never separates from one, as a shadow never separates from the body, is supported by the line from the *Advice to a King*: "his actions follow him like a shadow."
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk sets out concretely what friends and enemies are, and gives examples of the wrongdoing done for their sake — killing, stealing and the rest.
- ⚑ On the meaning of "standing in front of one," Kunzang Pelden explains it through the example of a shadow that never separates but waits with one, while Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both explain it as a habitual tendency or potency in the mental continuum: the manner of explanation differs.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama shows, citing Je Gungthangpa, that the very status of friend and enemy is impermanent and can turn into its opposite.
- The Story of Groshünkye shows directly how the wrongdoing done in former lives for the sake of friends and enemies ripens into birth as a hungry ghost or as a god.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

So it is: there is the "I" who accumulates the action, and there are the objects of that accumulating — the "my" relatives and the enemies. As the *Pramāṇavārttika* says: "When there is a self, one knows an other; from the parts 'self' and 'other' come grasping and aversion; and from being wholly bound up with these, every fault arises." The root that attachment and aversion are traced back to when they increase is precisely "I" and "mine" — my relatives, my kin, my enjoyments. And as the glorious Candrakīrti says in the *Madhyamakāvatāra* (Entering the Middle Way): "First one clings to a self, thinking 'I'; then, thinking 'this is mine,' one generates attachment to things. I bow to that compassion for beings who, like a bucket on a waterwheel, have no freedom at all." The reason this state of affairs comes about is that we cling to the impermanent as permanent, and cling as a self to what is not established as a self. What is in fact selfless and impermanent in nature, our minds — under the sway of the grasping at permanence we have grown used to since beginningless time — cling to as permanent; and under the sway of self-grasping we take as a self, and so divide the world into sides of attachment and aversion. So, not knowing that in the actual condition of things everything is sudden and fleeting in nature, I fell under the power of confusion and of grasping at things as true; and out of that I did and accumulated the many kinds of wrongdoing that consist in being attached to relatives and hostile to enemies.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-38]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

To gather the sense of all this: that I am something sudden and fleeting, like an evening bee or a guest — with no telling where I came from before or where I am going now, and vanishing in an instant — this I did not realize or understand. And so, with a rigid grasping at permanence and with the confusion that is bewildered about what to take up and what to reject, with the attachment that desires my own side and whatever I want, and with the anger that hates the other side and whatever I do not want, I have done and accumulated many different kinds of wrongdoing — killing, taking what is not given, and the rest.

Therefore one should reflect that death is certain, that its time is uncertain, and that at the time of death nothing but Dharma is of any help; and, contemplating the dangers of the lower realms, one should cultivate regret for the wrongdoing one has done, again and again.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Not Realizing That One Is Oneself Subject to Sudden Death Is the Root of Doing Much Wrong

All the commentaries agree in the end that because one did not realize before that one is subject to going suddenly, with no certainty as to when death will come, one did many kinds of wrongdoing with body, speech and mind through the three of confusion, attachment and anger.

###### 2. This Sudden Nature Is Like a Guest or a Bee: There Is No Telling Where One Came From Before or Where One Goes Now

Kunzang Pelden interprets the word "sudden" as being like an evening bee or a guest, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam likewise speaks of being "like a sudden guest."

###### 3. The Wrongdoing Thus Accumulated Belongs to the Class of the Ten Non-Virtues, From Killing Through Harsh Speech

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk works out in detail what "many kinds of wrongdoing" means: killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, lying, divisive speech, harsh words and the rest.

###### 4. The Ultimate Root of Wrongdoing Is Self-Grasping, Which Divides Self From Other and So Generates Attachment and Aversion

His Holiness the Dalai Lama uses the passages from the *Pramāṇavārttika* and the *Madhyamakāvatāra* to explain that under the sway of self-grasping, grasping and aversion arise from the parts "self" and "other," and that the confusion of not knowing that in the actual condition of things one's nature is sudden and fleeting is what accumulates the wrongdoing of attachment to relatives and hostility to enemies.

###### 5. In Response, One Must Reflect on the Dangers of Falling and Cultivate Regret Again and Again

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen advises reflecting that death is certain, that its time is uncertain, and that at death nothing but Dharma helps, and then cultivating regret again and again in the light of the dangers of the lower realms.

###### 6. Verses 2-38 and 2-39 Are Set Out as a Single Statement, Showing the Same Point: If One Wishes to Be Purified in This Life, One Must Purify Quickly

Ngulchu Thogme links the two verses together and explains them as one.
