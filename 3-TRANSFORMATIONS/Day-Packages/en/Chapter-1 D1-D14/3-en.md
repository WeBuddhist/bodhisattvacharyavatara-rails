<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Yesterday's verses were about urgency. Today's three give the answer. Ordinary good deeds are too weak against the weight of our harm. The buddhas searched for ages and found that this one mind works. So if you long to end suffering, your own and everyone's, hold on to it.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These three verses show three wonderful benefits of the awakening mind.

**It overpowers wrongdoing.** Our good deeds are weak, and our wrongdoing is very strong. But as the sutra says, "just as mice are overpowered when a cat looks at them," the moment perfect bodhicitta arises, it overpowers all of that strong wrongdoing.

**It brings happiness quickly.** The Buddhas thought deeply and carefully for countless ages. In the end they saw that the one and only path that easily brings help and happiness to countless beings is this awakening mind.

**It fulfils every wish.** So whether a humble person wants to end their own suffering, or a great person wants to end the suffering of others, or someone simply wants to enjoy much happiness — this good mind is the best way to make all those wishes come true. The advice is this: never let this good mind slip away from your heart.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will begin my actions with Bodhicitta—the sincere wish to help all living beings.

**Explanation**: Today's verse explains that after countless lifetimes of deep thought, the Buddhas realised that this loving intention is the best way to bring true happiness to everyone. By starting your daily tasks with a kind heart and the wish to benefit others, you are putting their greatest wisdom into practice.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

For us, through the force of habituation since beginningless time, the side of faults is very strong — the misdeeds and downfalls accumulated in the past and the wrongdoing still being accumulated now form a powerful, discordant force. The single unsurpassed method for clearing all of that away — a method that is like one thing that suffices for everything, a gathering of all in one — is nothing other than bodhicitta, the mind that cherishes others more than oneself. As for the word "enlightenment" (byang chub): put simply, byang ("purified") means that the cognitive obscurations that obstruct the knowing of all knowable things, the afflictive obscurations that establish their habitual imprints, and all the classes of faults of body and speech — the corrupt states and misdeeds that arise from those two — are exhausted forever and purified without remainder; and chub ("perfected") means the attainment of the ultimate, unsurpassed knowledge that, by the power of having exhausted all the obscurations to knowing, sees the two truths simultaneously. If bodhicitta arises by the power of great compassion, focused on all sentient beings, then in a single instant it can overcome the entire discordant side.

##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Second, the benefits of bodhicitta — the [awakening mind that is the] support. This has three: the benefit that is superior to other virtues; the benefit that transforms one's very name and status; and the benefit shown by way of an example. This is the first.

For the reasons explained above, therefore: the mind that wishes to accomplish virtue, like a flash of lightning, is feeble and weak in strength; whereas the mind of wrongdoing, of non-virtue, like the darkness within thick clouds, with its great power hurls one into the lower realms and is extremely hard to reverse — and so is unbearable. That very [wrongdoing] — if not for this very precious mind of perfect enlightenment, which shines forth like the sun — apart from this, what other ordinary virtue could overpower it? None could.

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

The second [main section] has four parts: explaining the benefits of bodhicitta; explaining the essential nature of bodhicitta; explaining the reason for those benefits; and praising the one who undertakes the training. The first of these has three: overcoming great wrongdoing; attaining supreme happiness; and accomplishing all wished-for aims. The first of these has three again: the benefit of relative bodhicitta; the benefit of ultimate bodhicitta; and the benefit of both. This is the first (how relative bodhicitta purifies wrongdoing):

For us, in this time when virtue is weak and wrongdoing is strong, it is fitting to strive at cultivating the antidote to wrongdoing — that is, to strive at cultivating the two [kinds of] bodhicitta. For that unbearable wrongdoing — the cause of the hells and so on — what virtue other than the mind of perfect enlightenment could overpower it? Nothing else could overpower it.

That ultimate bodhicitta also has the power to eliminate the seed of the obscurations; but that is not the chief thing to be explained here. What is taught here is the benefit of relative bodhicitta: its power to purify the wrongdoing accumulated earlier — the cause of the lower realms — and to cut off the stream of such [wrongdoing] arising in the future. The phrase "virtue is always feeble" is explained by the accompanying commentary [as applying to virtue]; and it is also explained so that one may understand it in the context of virtue being weak in strength and wrongdoing being powerful.

##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Second, the reason for giving rise to bodhicitta. The first [point] is abandoning wrongdoing:

Because the [precious human] support and the [right] intention are hard to find and short-lived, the antidote — virtue — is always feeble, remaining weak in strength at all times; whereas, because the causes of non-virtue are easily gathered, powerful and utterly unbearable wrongdoing arises in a continuous stream. ##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Second, the benefits of bodhicitta — the [awakening mind that is the] support. (First) taking it up firmly because it clears away great wrongdoing:

Since the freedoms and advantages are hard to find, a Dharma-minded attitude is rare to arise, and even when it arises it does not last long — therefore, the virtue that partakes of merit, being like a flash of lightning, occurs only now and then, and so is always feeble and weak in strength; whereas non-virtuous wrongdoing, like the darkness within thick clouds, with its great power produces the fierce suffering of the hells and the rest, and is utterly unbearable. That [wrongdoing] — if not for the mind of perfect enlightenment, which bears the essence of emptiness and compassion — what other ordinary virtue could overpower it? None could. But bodhicitta does overpower it; so it is fitting to take it up firmly. *[The scriptural passage on the four dharmas → see Scriptural Quotations.]*

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Second, explaining the benefits, in three parts. This is the first (overpowering the great wrongdoing that is to be abandoned):

The side of the antidote — merit and virtue — is like lightning within the darkness: feeble, weak in power and strength, occasional and short-lived, and so is always [only] intermittent in its continuity. But the side of wrongdoing that is to be abandoned — non-virtue — like the darkness within thick clouds, possesses great power and force, is hard to overcome with an antidote, and can even cast one into the terrifying sufferings of the hells and so on; it is therefore utterly unbearable... [and] if not for that very precious and powerful mind of perfect enlightenment, what other antidote among ordinary virtues could overpower it? None could.

An alternative reading: connecting the word "always" with the latter part, it is also explained as "wrongdoing, non-virtue, is always utterly unbearable and arises in a continuous stream."

The main point taught here: what is chiefly taught here is precisely that Mahayana relative bodhicitta has the power to purify the wrongdoing done earlier — the cause of the lower realms — and to cut off the stream of such [wrongdoing] arising later. *[The Ornament of the Sutras and a jātaka passage → see Scriptural Quotations.]*

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

The second [main section] has four parts. This is the first, the benefit that is superior to other virtues:

For the reasons explained above, therefore: the direct cause of virtue — that which brings about the temporary [attainment of] higher rebirth and the ultimate [attainment of] liberation and the state of omniscience — is extremely feeble and weak in power; whereas the non-virtuous wrongdoing that always casts one into the lower realms — killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, and the rest — is possessed of tremendous power and force, utterly unbearable. So what virtue other than that generation of bodhicitta — the sublime state of perfect enlightenment, the aspiring and engaging mind whose nature is the thought "By all means, for the sake of other beings, I myself shall attain it" — what other virtue could overpower it? It could never fully overpower it.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Virtue Is Always Feeble

Because the precious human support, with its freedoms and advantages, is hard to find, a Dharma-minded attitude is rare to arise, and even when it arises it does not last long, the virtue that partakes of merit is like a flash of lightning — occurring only now and then — and so it remains at all times weak in strength.

###### 2. Wrongdoing Is Powerful and Utterly Unbearable

Because the causes of non-virtue are easily gathered, wrongdoing arises in a continuous stream with great power and force, like the darkness within thick clouds, and produces the utterly unbearable sufferings of the lower realms such as the hells.

###### 3. Perfect Bodhicitta Alone Overpowers It

No ordinary virtue can overpower that great wrongdoing; only the mind of perfect enlightenment — whose nature is both aspiring and engaging bodhicitta, the wish to deliver all beings from the lower realms — is able to overpower it. Khenpo Kunga clarifies that both aspiring and engaging bodhicitta are gathered here.

###### 4. The Benefit of Relative Bodhicitta

As both Gyaltsab and Minyak Kunzang Sonam make clear, what is chiefly taught here is the benefit of relative bodhicitta: it has the twofold power to purify the wrongdoing accumulated earlier — the cause of the lower realms — and to cut off the continuation of future non-virtue. Ultimate bodhicitta also has the power to eliminate the seed of the obscurations, but that is not the chief subject here.

###### 5. The Two Readings of "Always"

Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives two ways of explaining the word "always" in the verse: (1) applied to virtue, so that "virtue is always feeble"; and (2) applied to wrongdoing, so that "wrongdoing is always powerful and unbearable." Both accord with the meaning of the verse and may be taken up.

###### 6. The Power to Overcome the Discordant Side in a Single Instant

His Holiness the Dalai Lama teaches this with the example of a great weapon: just as, however powerful an enemy army decked out with countless weapons may be, a single substance of great force can destroy it in an instant, so bodhicitta too can overcome, in a single instant, the entire discordant side accumulated since beginningless time. He likewise explains the very meaning of the word "enlightenment" (byang chub) — byang = the purifying, without remainder, of the whole class of faults and misdeeds; chub = the attaining of the knowledge that sees the two truths simultaneously — in accord with the key point of this verse.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Verse 1-6 opens the second portion of the first chapter — the explanation of the benefits of bodhicitta — by presenting the first benefit that is superior to other virtues: overpowering great wrongdoing. The point on which all the commentaries agree is the imbalance between virtue and wrongdoing: virtue, like a flash of lightning, is always feeble and hard to arise, whereas wrongdoing, like the darkness within thick clouds, is easily gathered and, with its great power, drags beings into the unbearable suffering of the lower realms.

However great an ordinary virtue may be, it cannot overpower that great wrongdoing; only the mind of perfect enlightenment — the precious mind shining like the sun — has the power of a peerless antidote.

Both Gyaltsab and Minyak Kunzang Sonam clarify an important doctrinal point: what is chiefly taught here is the benefit of relative bodhicitta. Its power is twofold — purifying the wrongdoing accumulated earlier, the cause of the lower realms, and cutting off the stream of future non-virtue before it arises. Ultimate bodhicitta also has the power to overcome the seed of the obscurations, but that is not the chief subject here.

Through the example of the cat's gaze from the Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra, the point is shown by way of direct experience: just as a cat's gaze naturally overpowers mice, so the precious eye of the all-knowing mind naturally overpowers all karma and afflictions, without remainder.

Minyak Kunzang Sonam sets out both readings of the word "always" in the verse — that virtue is always feeble, and that wrongdoing is always powerful and unbearable — and establishes that either accords with the meaning of the verse, the two being mutually compatible rather than contradictory.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Established by the Mighty Sages' Vision and by Scriptural Authority

The force of this line is not mere reasoning but rests on the valid cognition of scripture: the victors, the mighty sages, having examined and analyzed well over countless eons, arrived at the certainty that bodhicitta alone is the single cause of all benefit and happiness for boundless beings. Bodhicitta is explained as the sole cause of benefit and happiness. This certainty — that it is established from this one thing alone, and no other cause — shows why one must give rise to bodhicitta without delay.

Through bodhicitta, even boundless and immeasurable multitudes of beings attain the supreme bliss of perfect enlightenment both easily (without hardship) and quickly (swiftly). All the commentaries present these two qualities as the mark of bodhicitta being the supreme method.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Never Forsake Bodhicitta

The chief point is this: this very bodhicitta must never at any time (rtag tu) be forsaken from one's own mindstream. All six scholars agree here. Khenpo Kunga's explanation is especially clear: it is not merely a matter of not forsaking it for an instant, but of never casting it away from one's mindstream at all.

###### 2. Overcoming the Hundreds of Sufferings

This line has in mind the wish to overcome the hundreds of the sufferings of existence, and so it includes even the intention of the lesser person, who wishes to be freed from one's own suffering of samsara. For such a person too, bodhicitta is taught to be the supreme method, with no other method available.

###### 3. Dispelling All the Unhappiness of All Beings

This is the intention of the great person (skyes bu chen po): the wish to dispel, not merely one's own suffering, but every unhappiness of all beings without remainder. This too is an aim accomplished by bodhicitta.

###### 4. Enjoying Many Happinesses

Both Sabzang and Kunzang Pelden explain that this pervades both higher rebirth (mngon mtho) and definite goodness (nges par legs pa): both worldly happiness and the liberation of enlightenment are aims accomplished by bodhicitta.

###### 5. Benefiting All Three Types of Person

Both Gyaltsab and Minyak read the three verbs of this line (gzhom 'dod / bsal 'dod / spyod 'dod — "wishing to overcome / to dispel / to enjoy") as marking, respectively, the wished-for aims of the lesser, middling, and great persons. They show that bodhicitta is established as the single method that accomplishes all the aims of all three types of person.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Verse 1-8 concludes the sequence of the third benefit, showing that bodhicitta accomplishes all the wished-for aims of the three types of person: the wish to be freed from one's own suffering of samsara, the wish to dispel the unhappiness of all beings, and the wish to enjoy the great happiness of higher rebirth and definite goodness — all three. Because bodhicitta alone accomplishes all of these, it must never at any time be forsaken from one's own mindstream.

**Key points.**

- All six scholars read the three verbs of this line (gzhom 'dod / bsal 'dod / spyod 'dod) in accord with the framework of the three types of person (skyes bu gsum): the lesser person (wishing to be freed from one's own suffering of existence), the middling person (wishing for the liberation of both self and others), and the great person (whose intention pervades the dispelling of all unhappiness and the [attaining of] all happiness of beings, without remainder). Both Gyaltsab and Minyak set this out with great clarity.

- The Vinaya passage cited by Minyak teaches this meaning at its most profound: "Monks, even for the sake of [their] life, should not forsake bodhicitta." This is a statement of the utmost limit — if bodhicitta is not to be forsaken even when one's life is at risk, one should understand that there is no circumstance graver than that.

- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that the positive counterpart of not forsaking it is "holding it in one's own mindstream": not merely refraining from casting it away, but actually holding and guarding bodhicitta within one's own mindstream at all times.

- Verses 1-6, 1-7, and 1-8 together mark the three reasons for giving rise to bodhicitta and never forsaking it: the clearing away of suffering that no other method can overpower (1-6); the buddhas' own analysis establishing it as the single cause of benefit and happiness (1-7); and the accomplishment, without remainder, of every aim any type of person could wish for (1-8).

- His Holiness the Dalai Lama teaches that this benefit is not confined to the single person who holds bodhicitta: since the "thought of benefiting others" (phan pa'i bsam pa) is the root of all that is good, the person in whom it arises brings benefit not only to oneself but to one's companions and one's whole environment. In this he shows the ultimate reason for never forsaking bodhicitta even for an instant.
