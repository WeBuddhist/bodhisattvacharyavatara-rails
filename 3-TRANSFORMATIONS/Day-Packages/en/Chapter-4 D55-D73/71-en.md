## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 43 and 44 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Let us step together through this gateway of courage: even if our head is cut off, we will never bow before the enemy of the afflictions.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a comparison that is easy to understand and encouraging.

The afflictions in our mind and their antidotes, doing good, mindfulness, and wisdom, are like two armies drawn up facing each other. Among these, the afflictions are the enemy's army, and the antidotes are our own army. We must set these two armies fighting one another. Relying on the strength of the army of antidotes, we must defeat the enemy army of the afflictions. We must not stay in the middle and remain uninvolved. We must make up our mind and enter this inner battle. We must hold firmly to the antidotes, and we must hold on to our long-standing grudge against the afflictions that have harmed us since beginningless time.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, even if anger rises in me, I will not speak harsh words. I will not bow my head to the afflictions.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* This verse says that whatever suffering comes, we should not bow our head to the enemy of the afflictions. So when anger or craving arises in our mind, not falling under its power is the best practice of taming the mind. If we do this, our mind will find peace.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

I must cling with intense effort to this task of destroying the afflictions, and, holding a grudge against them, set the object to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle and destroy it. Now, if all afflictions are things to be abandoned, how is it that this clinging and grudge-holding are not themselves to be abandoned? In answer: this affliction-like appearance is itself what destroys the afflictions, and so it is not counted among what is to be abandoned.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This means: one must hold a grudge against this host of afflicted enemies and fight them. One must willingly accept and cling to this manner of fighting, giving them no quarter and, without remaining neutral, regard them one-sidedly as an enemy and wage war on them. Now, one might ask: is not this very holding of a grudge and bearing of resentment itself a fault? In answer: when directed at the afflictions within one's own mindstream — thinking, "this anger in my own mindstream is something that causes harm in every respect; I must get rid of it" — this act of holding a grudge is on the side of what is concordant with the path. Even though such an affliction-like appearance carries a slight trace of resentment and harshness, since it is on the side of what destroys the afflictions, it is said to be faultless.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-43]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Third: regarding the destruction of the afflictions by treating them as an enemy — I must cling with determination to whichever antidote I am already familiar with, and, holding a grudge against the afflictions, thinking "these have harmed me since beginningless time," set the object to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle and abandon the afflictions. Now, if one thinks that this very clinging and grudge-holding, being themselves forms of attachment and aversion, must also be abandoned — no: this affliction-like appearance, consisting of clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned, is, for one who is a beginner on the path, itself what destroys the afflictions, and so is not included among what is to be abandoned — at least not for now. Yet since it is still a cognitive obscuration, it must eventually be abandoned.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-43]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Gyaltsab's explanation of 4-43 continues directly under a heading trimmed here as belonging to the next verse's topic.)*

Since this is what should be abandoned, one must at all times never turn back one's resolve to destroy the afflictions. I must cling to this antidote of the afflictions — deliberately relying on it — and, holding a grudge against the afflictions, set them against each other in battle and destroy them.

If one asks whether this very clinging to the antidote and grudge-holding against what is to be abandoned are themselves afflictions and so must be abandoned: this affliction-like appearance of clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned belongs to the side of the antidote that destroys the afflictions, and so is not included among what is to be abandoned.

The later commentaries' statement that this too must eventually be destroyed means only that once what is to be abandoned is exhausted, the antidote and the object no longer need to be set against each other in battle.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-43]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Ngulchu Thokme quotes verses 4-43 and 4-44 together as one block, with a single unified explanation covering both jointly, closing at his own anchor `^4-89`. Only the portion of the explanation belonging to 4-43 is given below; the passage continues from there into 4-44's own topic, which is given on that verse's own page.)*

Therefore, one must never turn back from destroying the afflictions. I must cling to this act of abandoning the afflictions, and, holding a grudge against them, set them against each other in battle and destroy them. Now, if all afflictions are things to be abandoned, how can one rely on this clinging or grudge-holding? In answer: this affliction-like appearance that destroys the afflictions is, for now, not counted among what is to be abandoned — it is not abandoned for the time being, though in the end it too is abandoned, as is made clear in the commentaries. Some say it is simply not counted among the afflictions at all, which appears to be the position of the Great Commentary — though that is not quite the same claim — and this accords with the point made above.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^4-89]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

I must cling with determination to this antidote that destroys the afflictions, and, holding a grudge against the afflictions — thinking, "these have harmed me since beginningless time" — set the army of the antidote and the enemy of what is to be abandoned against each other in battle and destroy it by force.

If one wonders whether clinging and grudge-holding are not themselves afflictions: this affliction-like appearance, consisting of clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned, may be labeled an affliction in name, but since in reality it destroys the afflictions, it is not included among what is to be abandoned.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-43]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam quotes only the bare root verse under 4-43's own transclusion; his real explanation appears under 4-44's own transclusion and covers 4-43 and 4-44 jointly. Only the portion belonging to 4-43's own content is given below; the passage continues from there into 4-44's own topic, given on that verse's own page.)*

On this: as for taking a firm stand on the antidote, I must, in order to abandon the afflictions, deliberately seek out and rely on this antidote, generating intense aspiration and clinging to it fully; and holding a grudge — thinking, "this affliction has harmed me immeasurably since beginningless time" — I must, through steady diligence and determination, set what is to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle, and, relying on the army of the antidote, destroy the enemy that is what is to be abandoned.

If one asks whether clinging to and being attached to the antidote, and holding a grudge born of aversion toward what is to be abandoned, are not themselves afflictions to be abandoned: this affliction-like appearance, consisting of holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned and generating and relying on clinging to its antidote, may indeed be given the name of clinging or attachment and grudge-holding, but in reality it belongs to the side of the antidote — like mindfulness and vigilant introspection — that destroys the afflictions; it does not bear the defining character of an affliction, and so is not included on the side of the afflictions to be destroyed. Attachment, aversion, and grudge-holding that serve as causes binding one's own mindstream are to be understood here as the very afflictions taught to be destroyed.

As for the statement, found in some commentaries, that it "is not included among what is to be abandoned for now, yet is not abandoned for the time being, and is abandoned later" — the sub-commentary explains this to mean that once what is to be abandoned is exhausted, the antidote and the object no longer need to be set against each other in battle. The Great Commentary says that whatever else produces the causes for accumulating the two collections should be tamed like a thoroughly tamed horse and maintained for as long as the accumulations remain incomplete; later, these too are simply to be destroyed.

Some hold that the afflictions that serve as the cause for taking rebirth are, for the time being, not abandoned — thereby maintaining that a bodhisattva needs the afflictions as the cause for taking rebirth in cyclic existence. This is not tenable.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-44]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"To this" means: whatever virtue I have already grown familiar with and connected to, which serves as an antidote to the afflictions, I must cling to — that is, become attached to — again and again; and, reflecting on how the afflictions have harmed me across beginningless lifetimes, I must hold a grudge against them, and set the affliction to be abandoned and the virtuous antidote against each other in battle, so that the two must fight, in mutual combat.

Now, if one thinks: since clinging-attachment to the antidote and grudge-holding against the afflictions are themselves the nature of attachment and aversion, are they not themselves to be abandoned? In answer: although attachment and aversion in general are indeed to be abandoned, this present clinging-attachment to the virtuous antidote that destroys the afflictions, and this grudge-holding against the afflictions to be abandoned, is, for a person at the stage of a beginner, itself included among the antidotes that destroy attachment and the other afflictions; and although in the final analysis it too is to be abandoned, for now, at this stage, it is something to be relied upon in one's mindstream — and so it is not included among the afflictions to be abandoned. That is the meaning.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^4-43]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Antidote and Object Must Be Set Against Each Other in Battle, Destroying the Afflictions by Clinging Fiercely to the Antidote and Holding a Grudge Against What Is to Be Abandoned

All the interlinear gloss and commentaries agree that I must cling with determination to the antidote that destroys the afflictions, and, holding a grudge against the afflictions that have harmed me since beginningless time, set what is to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle and destroy the afflictions.

###### 2. Clinging to the Antidote and Holding a Grudge Against What Is to Be Abandoned Are Afflictions in Name Only — in Reality They Belong to the Antidote That Destroys the Afflictions, and So Are Not Themselves to Be Abandoned

In answer to the doubt that clinging and grudge-holding, being themselves attachment and aversion, ought also to be abandoned, all the commentaries agree that this affliction-like appearance, though labeled an affliction in name, is in reality what destroys the afflictions, and is therefore not included among what is to be abandoned.

###### 3. This Exemption Holds Only at the Stage of the Beginner; Ultimately It Too Must Eventually Be Abandoned, as Many Commentaries Make Clear

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden says it is a cognitive obscuration and so must eventually be abandoned; Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen says that once what is to be abandoned is exhausted, the antidote and the object no longer need be set against each other in battle; Ngulchu Thogme likewise says it is clear from the commentaries that it is not abandoned for now, but is abandoned in the end; and Minyak Kunzang Sonam likewise says one must maintain it, tamed like a well-broken horse, until the two accumulations are complete, after which it too is simply to be destroyed.

###### 4. The Metaphor of Setting the Object to Be Abandoned and Its Antidote Against Each Other in Battle Illustrates the Task of Abandoning the Afflictions

Sabzang explains that the army of the antidote and the enemy of what is to be abandoned are set against each other and destroyed by force; Minyak Kunzang Sonam likewise explains, using the language of an army and an enemy, that relying on the army of the antidote one destroys the enemy of what is to be abandoned.

###### 5. Holding a Grudge Is Not a Fault When Its Object Is the Affliction Within One's Own Mindstream Rather Than Another Being — This Is Concordant with the Path

In answer to the doubt that holding a grudge and bearing resentment is itself a fault, His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains that since its object is the affliction within one's own mindstream, even though a slight trace of resentment is present, it is on the side of what destroys the afflictions, and so is faultless.

###### 6. The Inner Antidote That Destroys the Afflictions Belongs to the Side of the Antidote — Like Mindfulness and Vigilant Introspection — Not to the Afflictions in the Defining Sense

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned belong to the side of the antidote that destroys the afflictions — like mindfulness and vigilant introspection — and do not bear the defining character of an affliction.

###### 7. It Is Untenable That a Bodhisattva Must Rely on the Afflictions as a Cause for Taking Rebirth; Rather, It Is Compassion That Joins Them to Existence

Minyak Kunzang Sonam refutes the position that a bodhisattva must, for now, retain the afflictions that serve as a cause for taking rebirth, and shows, citing the *Ratnāvalī*, the *Nyinang*, and the *Uttaratantra*, that a bodhisattva takes rebirth in existence through the power of compassion, not through the power of the afflictions.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that I must cling with determination to the antidote that destroys the afflictions, and, holding a grudge against the afflictions that have harmed me since beginningless time, set what is to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle. All the commentaries agree that this affliction-like appearance of clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned is, for a beginner on the path, itself on the side of the antidote that destroys the afflictions, and is therefore not included, for now, among the afflictions to be abandoned.

**Key points.**

- The manner of setting the object to be abandoned and its antidote against each other in battle: one must cling with intense aspiration to the antidote and, holding a grudge against the afflictions, set the army of the antidote and the enemy of what is to be abandoned against each other and destroy the afflictions.
- The difference between name and reality: clinging to the antidote and holding a grudge against what is to be abandoned is, in name, labeled an affliction, but in reality, like mindfulness and vigilant introspection, is on the side of what destroys the afflictions, and does not bear the defining character of an affliction.
- The manner of abandoning, for now versus ultimately: although this is not abandoned at the stage of the beginner, once what is to be abandoned is exhausted and the two accumulations are complete, it too, being a cognitive obscuration, must eventually be abandoned, as the commentaries make clear.
- Focusing on the affliction within one's own mindstream: since the object is the affliction within one's own mindstream rather than another being, holding a grudge against it is on the side of what is concordant with the path and so is faultless.
- ⚑ A difference of opinion on the cause of taking rebirth: although some hold that a bodhisattva must, for now, not abandon the afflictions that serve as a cause for taking rebirth, Minyak Kunzang Sonam refutes this as untenable, establishing through scripture that a bodhisattva's taking rebirth in existence is due to the power of compassion, not to the power of the afflictions.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Even if I am burned and killed" — that is fine. "Even if my head is cut off" — that too is fine; this is the meaning. But in no respect whatsoever will I concede the point to the afflicted enemy, nor, following from that, will I bow to it in any way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-44]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Therefore, from the very outset, we must repeatedly make this pledge: "Even if I am burned in fire and killed, or my head is severed by a sword and falls to the ground, that is the easier and lesser harm. Whatever great injury may come, I will never, in any respect or at any time, fall under the power of this afflicted enemy — attachment, aversion, and the rest — nor bow my head to it."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-44]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for the Bodhisattvas)

*(Gyaltsab's explanation of 4-44 is followed by headings trimmed here as opening the next topic.)*

If one asks whether, since abandoning the afflictions brings limitless hundreds of sufferings, it might not be better simply to follow along with them — in answer: even if I am burned in fire and killed, or my head is cut off, that is preferable, for it amounts to no more than discarding this present life's body; whereas bowing to the afflicted enemy in every way produces the great suffering of the hells and the like, and obstructs the attainment of what one truly desires. Therefore, I will never bow to the afflicted enemy, in any respect, nor fall under its power.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains verses 4-43 and 4-44 together as one continuous passage anchored under 4-44's own transclusion. The material on establishing and relying on the antidote, and the extended discussion of whether an affliction-like antidote — or an affliction that produces rebirth out of compassion — is ever exempt from final abandonment, belongs to 4-43's own topic and is given on that verse's own page. Only the portion glossing 4-44's own wording is drawn here.)*

If one asks whether, since abandoning the afflictions brings hundreds of hardships, it is easier simply to bow to them and rely on them — in answer: "I will be pierced and killed" means being stabbed with something like a short spear, having one's entrails pierced, and being killed on the spot — this is what "burned and killed immediately" means, and it is also said to extend to being cut apart limb by limb while still alive. So: even if I am killed in that way, or my head is severed with a knife and falls to the ground and I am killed, that is preferable — these amount to no more than the loss of this present life's body and life; whereas bowing to the afflicted enemy in every way and falling under its power produces every kind of unwanted suffering, such as that of the hells, obstructs the fulfillment of every wish, and utterly destroys all benefit and happiness.

In some editions of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* the reading appears as "burned," while some commentaries gloss it instead as "stabbed/pierced" — killed without being burned — yet the meaning is fixed as given above, since in all the earlier old editions of the root text and commentary, and in other places where it is cited as scripture, the reading "pierced" appears; it seems that the reading "burned" is a later alteration.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-44]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Even if some other person, like a king or a powerful ruler, were to say, by force, "if you do not abandon your afflictions, you will actually be burned in a mass of fire and killed," or, "I will cut off your head or neck" — let them cut it off; that is fine. Other than that, in no respect, at no time, staking my very life on it, will I bow to the afflicted enemies such as attachment, or continue to rely on them within my own mindstream. This is the meaning: one must engage in abandoning the afflictions with an utterly resolute mind, having cut off all other options.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. An Unwavering Vow: Better to Be Burned or Beheaded Than to Bow, in Any Respect, to the Afflicted Enemy

All the commentaries agree in explaining that even if one is burned and killed by fire, or one's head is cut off, one must hold firmly to the resolve never, under any circumstance, to fall under the power of the afflicted enemy.

###### 2. Death Is Merely the Loss of This Life's Body and Life, but Bowing to the Afflicted Enemy Produces the Great Suffering of the Hells and Destroys All Benefit and Happiness

On close examination, giving up one's body and life is limited to this present life, whereas falling under the power of the afflictions brings the greater, ultimate harm — so between the two options, the former is the one to choose.

###### 3. "In Every Respect" Implies "At Any Time Whatsoever," Showing That This Vow Encompasses Every Occasion and Circumstance

The vow never to bow to the afflicted enemy is explained as remaining constant through every difference of time or circumstance.

###### 4. "Bow" Means to Follow After the Afflicted Enemy and Submit to Its Rule

The interlinear gloss explains it as "bowing one's head and following after," while His Holiness the Dalai Lama clarifies it as "conceding the point to the afflicted enemy."

###### 5. Ngulchu Thogme Explains That This Verse Firmly Establishes, by an Extreme Example, the Preceding Verse's Resolve Never to Turn Back from Destroying the Afflictions

The pledge that one must never turn back from destroying the afflictions is made firm by this verse's example, carried to its furthest extreme.

###### 6. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Explains That Even Under a King's Coercive Power, One Must Resolve Never Again to Rely on the Afflictions

Even if compelled by another's coercive power, one must cut off all other options with utter resolve and never again rely on the afflictions.

###### 7. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Explains That, Despite a Textual Variant Between "Burned" and "Pierced," the Meaning Is Fixed and Unaffected

Although the wording differs between editions, he clarifies that there is no difference in meaning between the two readings.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Even if burned in fire and killed, or beheaded, and thereby parted from this present life's body and life, one must never, at any time, bow one's head to the afflicted enemy or follow after it, but must hold firmly, with utterly resolute mind, to this vow. For giving up one's body and life is limited to this present life, whereas falling under the power of the afflictions produces the great suffering of the hells and the like, and destroys all benefit and happiness entirely.

**Key points.**

- Even though grave harm — the loss of one's body and life — may come, one must repeatedly renew the vow never to bow to the afflicted enemy at any time, nor to fall under its power.
- Being burned and beheaded is merely the loss of this present life's body and life, yet bowing to the afflicted enemy produces the great suffering of the hells and the like, and destroys all benefit and happiness entirely — so death is judged the lesser harm.
- "Bow" is to be understood as bowing one's head to the afflicted enemy and following after it, or, as His Holiness the Dalai Lama explains, as conceding the point to it and thereafter falling under its sway.
- Even if compelled by the coercive power of a king or a powerful ruler and burned in a mass of fire, one must, with utterly resolute mind, refuse ever again to bow to the afflicted enemy or rely on it, and must abandon the afflictions.
- ⚑ Although some root and commentarial editions read "burned" (consumed by fire), the older editions read "pierced" (struck with a weapon and killed) — Minyak Kunzang Sonam clarifies that the meaning is the same in either case.
