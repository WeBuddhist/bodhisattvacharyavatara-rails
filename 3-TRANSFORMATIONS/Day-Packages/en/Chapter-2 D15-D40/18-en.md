## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on verses 8 and 9 from the second chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. It teaches us how to offer our bodies and lives to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, becoming their willing servants. It also guides us to let go of past mistakes and help others fearlessly. This is a practice of breaking down our selfishness to serve others with courage.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These verses show us how to overcome our selfish attachment to our own bodies and comfort. Master Shantideva says that even if we have no material wealth, we can offer our entire body to the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas forever. By doing this, we choose to become their subjects or servants — servants of compassion.

Just as someone protected by a powerful king feels no fear of enemies, being protected by these holy beings makes us fearless in the face of life's sufferings. This fearlessness allows us to boldly help others. We also make a strong promise to purify our past mistakes and vow never to repeat them, even at the cost of our lives.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, letting go of selfishness, I will see myself as a servant of compassion and fearlessly do one good deed to help someone else.

**Explanation:** *(Doing Good)* Normally, we are so attached to our own comfort that we hesitate to help others if it requires effort or causes us slight trouble. Today, following the teachings of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, I will imagine that I have offered my body entirely to the service of love and compassion. Guided by the thought, "I am protected and have nothing to fear," I will not shrink away from small difficulties. Instead, I will boldly step up to help someone in need. This practice breaks down our selfish habits and builds deep courage and kindness.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Pledge to Offer the Whole Body Forever

Even without other offering-substances, one has one's own body under one's control; so, to the victorious buddhas and their heirs, the bodhisattvas, one offers all one's body — the present body and all the bodies one will take up in the future — ceaselessly and forever, as the commentaries make clear.

###### 2. The Prayer That the Supreme Beings Fully Accept the Body

One prays that the victors together with their heirs take the offered body as their own, and one takes on the very state of being a servant or subject who carries out the command of the holy beings.

###### 3. The Respect of the Three Doors With Which One Becomes a Servant

After offering the body, one pledges to come before the field of offering — the buddhas and bodhisattvas — in the manner of a servant or attendant, with the respect of the three doors.

###### 4. The Purpose of Abandoning the Thought of Holding the Body as One's Own

It is said that one offers the present body and all future bodies having let go of the thought of holding them as one's own; this is set forth as a means of using the body for the welfare of others alone, not seeking one's own livelihood of food and clothing.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse, following the offering of substances, pledges to offer all one's body under one's own control — the present body and all the bodies one will take up in the future — forever, to the victorious buddhas and their heirs, the bodhisattvas; and, with the respect of the three doors, one takes on the state of being their servant or subject.

**Key points.**

- Having no offering-wealth but possessing a body under one's own control, one pledges to offer it to the victors together with their heirs.
- The body offered is not only this present body but is explained to include all bodies one will take up in future lives, offered ceaselessly and forever.
- One prays that the supreme beings, the victors together with their heirs, fully accept the offered body as their own.
- Having offered the body, one takes on, with the respect of the three doors, the state of being a servant or attendant of the field of offering, carrying out their command.
- The purpose of offering the body having abandoned the thought of holding it as one's own is set out as a means of using the body for the welfare of others alone, not seeking one's own livelihood of food and clothing.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Because I have been made a servant of the buddhas and their heirs, whose capacity of knowledge and love is perfected, then, according to their instruction, I am to accomplish the welfare of others and not act for myself alone. Since the wrongdoing gathered in the past makes even my own aims unattainable and weakens my capacity to act for others, from now on I shall, each to the best of his ability, strive to give up wrongdoing and accomplish virtue. Seeing as faults those wrongdoings gathered before, I truly pass beyond them — that is, I confess them without concealing them — and I hold to the resolve of pledge: "I will not do other wrongdoing again." (This may be the continuation of the theme of offering the body as a servant; the confession seems to come out here as a separate topic.)

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

"Because you have fully accepted me and taken me up as a servant": that is, since you have taken me as your own, in order to please you, even while I abide in existence, in samsara, I shall — for the sake of others — be unafraid of the sufferings of samsara and accomplish the benefit and happiness of all beings. In that case, if I myself were possessed of the wrongdoing of acting non-virtuously, it would harm your renown, fame, and dignity, and I would be unable even to accomplish my own aim, let alone others'. Therefore, I truly pass beyond the former wrongdoings — that is, I confess them — and with a mind of restraint I pledge: "I will not do any other wrongdoing again." It is also said that offering the body in this way is the highest of the guardings [of the vows].

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Just as no harm from others befalls the retinue and subjects who rely on a great king, so I, lowly and afflicted, when you — who have become the supreme refuge free of all fear — fully accept me as your subject under command, am unafraid of, and unterrified by, the suffering of this place of samsaric existence. And having come under the refuge of the unsurpassed refuge, I too, by the strength of freedom from fear, am able to bring benefit — accomplishing only benefit and happiness through the conduct of the buddhas' heirs, exemplified by the three kinds of giving, both immediate and ultimate — for those afflicted beings who lack a refuge and protector. Yet, even relying on the supreme refuge, if my being were still corrupted by the wrongdoing and non-virtue gathered in former times, that would be unfit for a subject of the Three Jewels, and it would greatly obstruct even the accomplishment of my own aim, let alone the benefit of others. Therefore I truly pass beyond those former misdeeds and non-virtuous actions — that is, by the power of rejection and the rest, I confess and free myself from them; and further, since it is unfitting for one who has become a subject of the Three Jewels to do any other wrongdoing or non-virtuous deed, I rely on the firm resolve of restraint: "from now on, even at the cost of my life, I will not do non-virtuous deeds."

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

I myself, like an ordinary being wandering in samsara, having now been fully accepted in compassion by you, the holy field, the buddhas and bodhisattvas — I am now, in this place of the samsaric existence of the three realms, unafraid of any suffering such as birth, aging, sickness, and death; and, like a person who relies on someone great, in a manner free of fear and dread, I duly accomplish whatever brings benefit to other beings, both immediate and ultimate. Moreover, if you buddhas and bodhisattvas fully accept me in compassion, then whatever wrongdoing and non-virtuous, evil deeds of the three doors I have gathered from former lives up to now, I truly pass beyond them — that is, having duly confessed them, I make myself clean; and, further, from now on I make the pledge: "the other wrongdoing and non-virtuous deeds impelled by attachment, aversion, and delusion, I will not do again, even if my life is at stake." These are what is called "the offering actually set out," of which there are the offering of things owned, the offering of things not owned, and the offering of one's own body.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Theme: The Two Pledges Made After Offering the Body

All the commentaries agree that this verse expresses two things after the offering of the body in the earlier verse: the pledge to act for the benefit of beings, and the resolve of restraint — to pass beyond former wrongdoing and not to do other wrongdoing again.

###### 2. Through the Power of the Refuge One Becomes Fearless and Glad to Act for Others

Relying on being fully accepted in compassion by the victors together with their heirs, one becomes unafraid of any suffering in the place of existence and able to benefit beings; this is illustrated by the way no harm from others befalls the retinue that relies on a great king.

###### 3. The Reason for Confessing Wrongdoing — While Possessed of Wrongdoing, One Cannot Accomplish Even One's Own Aim, Let Alone Others'

Kunzang Pelden and Sabzang Mati both make clear that, since acting non-virtuously makes even one's own aim unattainable, let alone benefiting others, one must truly pass beyond former wrongdoing.

###### 4. The Means of Confession — Passing Beyond by Way of the Four Opponent Powers

On the way of passing beyond former wrongdoing, both the annotation gloss and Minyak Kunzang Sonam name the power of rejection and the rest — the four opponent powers — directly, as the manner of "truly passing beyond."

###### 5. The Aspect of Restraint — the Firm Pledge Not to Do Wrongdoing Again Even at the Cost of One's Life

Gyaltsab and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both set out the sense of "not doing other wrongdoing again even if one's life is at stake," making clear how firm the resolve of restraint is.

###### 6. Within the Three Kinds of Offering

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk gathers everything explained up to this verse under "the offering actually set out," and explains it as belonging to the three: the offering of things owned, the offering of things not owned, and the offering of one's own body.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse expresses two things after the offering of the body: the pledge to act for the benefit of beings, and the resolve of restraint — to pass beyond former wrongdoing and not to do other wrongdoing again. All the commentaries explain it in agreement.

**Key points.**

- Relying on being fully accepted in compassion by the victors together with their heirs, one becomes unafraid of any suffering in the place of existence and able to benefit beings.
- Since being possessed of wrongdoing makes even one's own aim unattainable, let alone benefiting others, one must truly pass beyond the former wrongdoing.
- The means of passing beyond wrongdoing is by way of the four opponent powers, such as the power of rejection.
- One establishes the firm resolve of restraint: not to do other non-virtuous deeds again, even at the cost of one's life.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk gathers everything explained up to this verse under "the offering actually set out" and places it within the three — offering of things owned, of things not owned, and of one's own body.

The person making the offering imagines, in the space before him, an utterly vast hall for offering the bathing of the bodies of the buddhas and bodhisattvas. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Beginning of the Mind-Emanated Offering — the Offering of Bathing

All the commentaries agree that from this verse the mind-emanated offering, within the offerings of body, speech, and mind, begins, and that its first part is the offering of bathing.

###### 2. The Purpose of Bathing — Although the Victors and Their Heirs Have No Stain of Fault, It Is to Cleanse the Stains of One's Own Three Doors

Kunzang Pelden clearly explains that, although the victors together with their heirs have no stain of fault, one bathes the deities in order to cleanse, by bathing, the stains of the three doors of oneself and other beings.

###### 3. The Arrangement of the Bathing Hall — Sweet Fragrance, a Crystal Floor, Jeweled Pillars, a Canopy of Pearls

All the commentaries explain in one voice that the bathing hall is utterly sweet in fragrance from sandalwood, camphor and the like; that its crystal floor is bright and shining; that it has lovely pillars ablaze with jewels; and that above it a canopy of pearls is spread.

###### 4. The Detailed Exposition of the Bathing Hall — the Four Pillars, the Bathing-Basin, the Four Tiers

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk describes every part of the bathing hall in detail: the four jeweled pillars, the four cross-bars, the bathing-basin, and the arrangement of offering-substances — flowers, incense, lamps, scented water, food, and so on — on the four outer tiers.

###### 5. The Throne at the Center of the Bathing Hall — Various Jeweled Thrones

Kunzang Pelden explains that, upon the skylight at the center of the bathing hall, held up by short pillars, there is a domed roof crowned with a precious wish-fulfilling jewel, within which one imagines it together with various jeweled thrones.

###### 6. The Parallel Image — the Worldly Custom of First Offering a Bath to a Chief Guest

Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives, as a parallel, the worldly custom by which, when a king or the like is invited as a guest, one first offers a bath — so one likewise offers the bathing of the bodies of the recipients of offering one has invited with faith and respect.
