## Per-Commentary Summaries

### [[en-AI-generated-commentary-gyaltsab(old)|Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen]] Summary
Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen explains that the human body of leisure and fortune is extremely rare and precious. He breaks down the causes, the definition of non-leisures, and the ten fortunes.
- **Rarity of Causes:** Merely obtaining a basic human body requires guarding at least one type of ethical discipline. To obtain the exceptional state of leisure and fortune (with all eighteen qualities), one must have pure ethical discipline as the foundation, generosity and other perfections as companions, and stainless aspiration as the connecting link. Since this combination is extremely rare, the resulting support is exceptionally difficult to find.
- **Eight Non-Leisures:** These are the states where there is no opportunity to practice virtue.
- **Ten Fortunes:** He lists the five personal fortunes (being human, born in a central land, having complete faculties, having an unreversed end of actions—defined as not committing or causing the five heinous crimes, and having faith in the source—defined as faith in the three baskets of scriptures) and the five circumstantial fortunes (a Buddha has appeared, teaches the holy Dharma, the teaching remains, followers remain, and there is loving compassion for others).
- **Great Benefit of the Support:** Having obtained this exceptional support, which is capable of accomplishing the goals of a person (high rebirth and definitive goodness), one must make great efforts to practice the Dharma. If, due to the delusion of immortality, one wastes this opportunity, it will be extremely difficult to obtain it again, as we mostly reside in states of non-leisure where we do not accumulate the causes of happy rebirth. He cites Nagarjuna's *Letter to a Friend* and its famous blind turtle analogy to emphasize this rarity.

### [[en-AI-generated-commentary-sazang(old)|Sazang Mati Panchen]] Summary
Sazang Mati Panchen Lodro Gyaltsen details the contemplation of leisure and fortune as a preliminary to engaging in the Dharma.
- **Contemplation of Non-Leisures:** He cites Prajñākaramati's *Great Commentary* to define the eight non-leisures (hell-beings, hungry ghosts, animals, long-lived gods, barbarians, wrong views, absence of a Buddha, and being mute) where there is no opportunity to practice the Dharma.
- **Ten Fortunes:** He lists the five personal fortunes and five conventional (circumstantial) fortunes.
- **Rarity of the Support:** He cites the *Sutras on the Perfections* to show that even a simple human rebirth is rare, so perfect leisure and fortune is exceptionally difficult to find.
- **Extracting the Essence:** Since we have obtained this exceptional support for accomplishing higher rebirth and definite goodness, we must practice virtue immediately. If we fail to do so, we may never obtain it again. He quotes the *Letter to a Disciple* to show how foolish it is to make this human state, which excels even a wish-fulfilling jewel, fruitless.

### [[en-AI-generated-commentary-thokme|Ngulchu Thokme Zangpo]] Summary
Ngulchu Thokme Zangpo explains the difficulty of finding the support of the body (leisure and fortune) as the first part of the support of the person who enters the path.
- **Eight Non-Leisures and Ten Fortunes:** He defines the eight non-leisures and ten fortunes (five personal and five circumstantial), which together constitute "leisure and fortune" (dal 'byor).
- **Difficulty of Finding:** Citing the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* and the *Biography of Noble Maitreya*, he emphasizes that both being free of non-leisure and obtaining pure leisure and fortune are extremely difficult.
- **Urgency of Practice:** Having obtained this support that can accomplish high rebirth and definitive goodness, if one does not practice virtue, it is impossible to obtain it again. This is because we mostly reside in non-leisure states where we do not create the causes of happy rebirth. He quotes the *Letter to a Friend*'s blind turtle analogy to illustrate this.

## Combined Commentary Synthesis

### Consensus
All three commentators agree that Verse 1-4 (Tibetan 1-1) emphasizes the supreme value, extreme rarity, and urgent necessity of practicing the Dharma on the basis of a precious human life of leisure and fortune (*dal 'byor*).
- **Consensus on the Eighteenth Qualities:** All three define leisure and fortune using the traditional framework of being free from the **eight non-leisures** and endowed with the **ten fortunes** (five personal and five circumstantial/conventional).
- **Consensus on the Goal:** The primary purpose of obtaining this support is to accomplish the goals of a person, which they agree are **high rebirth** (*mngon mtho* / *abhyudaya*) and **definitive goodness** (*nges legs* / *niḥśreyasa*).
- **Consensus on Rarity and Danger of Wasting:** They agree that because sentient beings mostly reside in states of non-leisure (bad migrations), obtaining a human life again is nearly impossible once this one is lost. Therefore, wasting it due to the delusion of immortality is a grave error.
- **Shared Metaphor:** Both Gyaltsab and Thokme cite the famous blind turtle analogy from Nagarjuna's *Letter to a Friend* to illustrate the mathematical improbability of regaining a human rebirth.

### Divergences and Unique Insights
- **Gyaltsab's Scholastic Breakdown of Causes:** Gyaltsab provides a detailed causal explanation of how leisure and fortune is obtained: a basic human body requires at least one type of ethical discipline, but the full eighteen-fold leisure and fortune requires **pure ethical discipline as the foundation**, **generosity and other perfections as companions**, and **stainless aspiration as the connecting link**.
- **Sazang's Citations and Metaphors:** Sazang explicitly cites Prajñākaramati's *Great Commentary* to define the non-leisures, references the *Sutras on the Perfections* to establish its rarity, and quotes the *Letter to a Disciple* to compare the human body to a wish-fulfilling jewel, showing how foolish it is to make it fruitless. He also frames this entire section as the "manner of engaging" (how to enter the Dharma).
- **Thokme's Structural Context and Sources:** Thokme places this section under the "support of the person who enters," distinguishing the support of the body (leisure and fortune) from the support of the mind (the virtuous mind, verse 1-5). He cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* and the *Biography of Noble Maitreya* as textual authority for the rarity of leisure and fortune.
