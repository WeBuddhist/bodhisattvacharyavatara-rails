## Combined Commentary Summaries
All three classical commentators agree that Verse 1-23 demonstrates how the precious mind of enlightenment (Bodhicitta) is exceptionally distinguished from the world. They argue that the absolute, universal altruism of wanting to establish all sentient beings in supreme awakening is completely unprecedented and cannot be found anywhere in the ordinary world—neither among intimate worldly relations nor among the highest worldly deities and sages.

### 1. Comparison with Worldly Relationships
*   **Consensus:** The commentators point out that while deep love and a desire to benefit others certainly exist in the world, they are always limited and partial. No ordinary relative or friend possesses the vast wish to establish all beings in ultimate, supreme bliss.
*   **Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen** explains that even parents, who naturally wish to benefit their children, lack a beneficial attitude of this caliber that seeks to accomplish supreme bliss for all.
*   **Sazang Mati Panchen** expands this list to include other intimate relations, asking if any ordinary person—whether a father, a mother, a sibling, or a close friend who wishes to benefit others—possesses the thought of wanting to establish all sentient beings in great awakening, concluding that none do.
*   **Ngulchu Thokme** supports this point by citing the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṅkāra*: *"O King, parents, friends, and relatives do not wish for their children and relatives what the Bodhisattvas, with their beneficial minds, wish for all wandering beings."*

### 2. Comparison with High Worldly and Spiritual Beings
*   **Consensus:** The commentators agree that even the most exalted beings in traditional Indian cosmology—who are celebrated for their wisdom, truthfulness, and spiritual accomplishments—do not possess this universal altruistic resolve.
*   **The Sages and Gods:** All three commentators specify that neither the worldly gods nor the truth-speaking sages/seers (*ṛṣis*) harbor such an attitude.
*   **The Creator God Brahma:**
    *   **Sazang Mati Panchen** provides a highly detailed characterization of Brahma as one *"who has trained in the four immeasurables and wishes to be the grandfather of all."* Yet, despite Brahma's meditation on the four immeasurables (loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity) and his role as a cosmic protector, Sazang emphasizes that even he does not possess the specific intention of leading all beings to supreme awakening.
    *   **Gyaltsab Je** similarly notes that even Brahma, though *"abiding in the four immeasurables,"* does not possess such a beneficial attitude.
    *   **Ngulchu Thokme** aligns with this, stating that neither the gods, the seers, nor Brahma possess this mind.

### 3. Doctrinal and Philosophical Significance
*   **Ultimate vs. Temporary Benefit:** The commentators contrast the worldly desire to provide temporary relief or happiness with the Bodhisattva's ultimate desire to establish all beings in perfect Buddhahood. Even when high deities like Brahma practice the four immeasurables, their focus is on temporary, worldly happiness (such as rebirth in higher realms), whereas Bodhicitta aims at complete, unsurpassable liberation.
*   **The Unprecedented Nature of Bodhicitta:** By showing that this mind is absent in parents, friends, gods, sages, and Brahma, the commentators establish that the arising of Bodhicitta is an extraordinary, wondrous event—a "jewel of the mind" that is completely unprecedented in the samsaric world.
