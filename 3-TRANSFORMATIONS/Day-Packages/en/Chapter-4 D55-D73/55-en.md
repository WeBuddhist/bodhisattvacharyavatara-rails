# Day 55 — Heedfulness that holds bodhicitta firm, examined and beyond retreat

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the three verses 1 to 3 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. This promise has already been well examined. So now let us go forward with courage, with no doubt and no drawing back.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a passage of scripture that is easy to understand and encouraging.

> Carefulness is the place of the nectar,
> and carelessness the place of death: so the Sage taught.
> Therefore, in order to increase what is good,
> always act with respect and with care.

As today's first verse says, it is very important to be careful: to hold bodhicitta firmly and not let the training decline. Carefulness is the root and the support of everything good. It keeps the promise of bodhicitta and the good we do alive, and makes them grow.

But if we become careless, every kind of wrongdoing arises, and it turns into a cause of the suffering of samsara. So we must not fall into carelessness even for a moment. We should be careful always.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, before I begin anything, I will examine carefully whether it is right to do or not.

**Explanation:** *(Wisdom Practice)* Acting in a rush, without examining, brings harm to ourselves and to others alike. So whatever the task, we should examine it well before we start. Then there is no regret, and the aim is accomplished by itself.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Now, to show that **enlightenment belongs to those who make practice the essential thing, and not to anyone else,** the heirs of the victorious ones, having just been shown this, **should hold bodhicitta firmly** by that same rite, and — **without the laziness that lets things slide** — **should always strive, at every time,** not **to stray from** the **training of a bodhisattva.**

As for that training, one should train in it extensively as it is set out — divided into the perfections, the immeasurables, the means of gathering disciples, and so on — in sūtras such as the *Akṣayamatinirdeśa* (Sūtra of Inexhaustible Intelligence) and the *Ratnamegha* (Cloud of Jewels).

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

The heirs of the victorious ones, having first called to mind the benefits of bodhicitta in this way and sustained the object of bodhicitta in meditation, take hold of it firmly through the rite of generating the mind, once some real experience has begun to take hold, so as to make it firm. In general terms he teaches that one must then strive, without ever letting it slip, to never stray from the training of a bodhisattva.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the first of the three chapters that keep the precious bodhicitta, once it has arisen, from declining: the chapter on carefulness, whose main concern is paying close attention to what to engage in and what to turn away from. All the paths of the Great Vehicle come down to two practices: the aspiring bodhicitta of intention, and the engaged bodhicitta of application in the six perfections. Generosity has already been taught in the earlier chapters, so ethical discipline is now taught through four further chapters, beginning with this one on carefulness and the one that follows on vigilant introspection. The chief means of guarding ethical discipline is repeatedly meditating on carefulness and mindful introspection. Nāgārjuna explains ethical discipline as the ground that supports every good quality, and Vasubandhu advises meditating on it by relying on study and reflection.

Having settled this with certainty, the bodhisattva heirs of the victorious ones should — through the complete preparation, main part, and conclusion of the rite — hold bodhicitta firmly in their own being, never giving it up even at the cost of their life, and should always strive, without slipping into laziness or turning back, by every means, to keep it from declining, never transgressing the trainings of what is to be done and what is to be avoided, such as the twenty root downfalls of a bodhisattva.

The name "heirs of the victorious ones" cannot be earned merely by having a loving heart toward beings; it applies once one has properly taken up the bodhisattva vow, through a valid rite, chiefly out of a wide-ranging intention embracing both one's own and others' good. "Holding it firm" means that from the moment bodhicitta is generated, one must hold firmly to the resolve that this bodhicitta will not be let go of, whatever comes. "Not straying from the training" means that fine words and clever talk are not enough; one must identify the essential points of a bodhisattva's training and guard them — the eighteen root downfalls to be given up, the downfalls specific to the aspiring and engaged trainings, and so on — without transgressing them even at the cost of one's life.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

The bodhisattva heirs of the victorious ones, having as explained before held both aspects of bodhicitta firmly, should never let their mind slip even for an instant, and should always strive, with carefulness, to guard against transgressing the training in the six perfections and the four means of gathering disciples, so that it does not decline.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

The heirs of the victorious ones, having through the complete preparation, main part, and conclusion held bodhicitta firmly in this way, should always strive, without the slightest slackening, never to transgress its trainings.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The bodhisattva heirs of the victorious ones, having as explained before generated the supreme aspiring bodhicitta and held it firmly, never giving it up even at the cost of their life, should strive — without the slackening of idleness, continuously and at all times — to make earnest practice their very essence, engaging in the vast trainings of a bodhisattva without transgressing them. Carefulness is the root of all virtuous qualities, so relying on it is extremely important.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The bodhisattva heirs born from the heart of the victorious ones, having through the complete preparation, main part, and conclusion held both the aspiring and engaged bodhicitta firmly, never to be let go, should make earnest, careful practice their very essence — striving with carefulness, from now on, without the slightest slackening born of laziness and at all times, never to transgress even at the cost of their life the vast range of trainings such as the six perfections, the four means of gathering disciples, and the four immeasurables — and should guard it so that it does not decline. Carefulness is the root of all virtuous qualities, so relying on it keeps every root of virtue in what is to be accomplished and trained in from declining, and makes it grow ever further. Carefulness is the ground of all virtuous qualities and the source from which the deathless nectar of nirvana arises, while carelessness gives rise to every kind of wrongdoing and becomes the cause of experiencing the suffering of birth and death in samsara.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

As explained above, the bodhisattva heirs of the victorious ones, having through the fully pure rite of preparation, main part, and conclusion held that same aspiring and engaged bodhicitta firmly within their own being, should — without their mind slipping elsewhere or falling into carelessness, continuously and at all times — strive with diligence never to transgress, in any of its three aspects, the bodhisattva discipline: restraining from wrongdoing, gathering virtuous qualities, and acting for the welfare of beings. The manner of striving is this: with respect to the vows of bodhicitta, one should give up both an actual downfall and its mere semblance, and adopt both the absence of downfall and its semblance.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^4-1]]

#### Metaphors and Examples

- **Catching lucidity within a dream** (རྨི་ལམ་ཟིན་པ) → the power of setting a firm resolve to secure the irreversible retention of bodhicitta. In explaining "holding it firm," Khenpo Kunzang Pelden says that from this moment one must set a firm resolve that bodhicitta will not be let go of, whatever comes; just as setting one's resolve on catching lucidity within a dream makes it possible to catch it, so setting one's resolve in this way is of great importance.
- **The abode of the nectar of deathlessness and the abode of death** (བདུད་རྩིའི་གནས་དང་འཆི་བའི་གནས) → carefulness sustains and increases all virtue, while carelessness is the cause of samsaric suffering. Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that carefulness is the ground of virtuous qualities and the source of the deathless nectar of nirvana, while carelessness gives rise to every wrongdoing and becomes the cause of experiencing the suffering of birth and death in samsara, quoting passages to this effect from a sūtra and from the *Letter to a Friend*.
- **The king's servant carrying a vessel of oil across slippery ground** (རྒྱལ་པོའི་བྲན་གྱིས་མར་སྣོད་ཐོགས་ཏེ་སྟེག་འདྲེད་པར་འགྲོ་བ) → the vigilant, unremitting attention that is the very essence of carefulness. Citing the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that carefulness means paying close, wary attention, going in advance of the fear that one might lose what one wants or meet with what one does not want — just as a servant of a fierce and hard-to-please king carries a vessel filled with oil across slippery ground.

#### Scriptural Quotations

> Ethical discipline, learning, generosity, patience, and the rest — however many things are spoken of as virtuous qualities — carefulness is the root of them all: the Blissful One taught that it is what wins the treasure.

— *Samādhirāja Sūtra* (King of Concentration) (heedfulness named as the root of all qualities reckoned as virtue)

> What is carefulness? It is this: dwelling in freedom from attachment, hatred, and delusion, together with diligence, one guards the mind against defiled states while cultivating virtuous qualities — and this brings about, and fully completes, every worldly and transcendent excellence.

— *Compendium* (ཀུན་ལས་བཏུས) (definition of carefulness's essence and function)

> Stealing the wealth of the Three Jewels is reckoned a downfall of defeat; causing someone to abandon the sacred Dharma the Sage taught as the second. Toward a monk of corrupt discipline too: snatching away his saffron robes, beating him, forcing him into labor, casting him out of the monastic life, or taking his life — these make the third. Committing any of the five acts of immediate retribution, holding wrong views, and destroying towns and the like — these too the Sage taught as root downfalls. Teaching emptiness to beings whose minds have not been trained for it; turning back, from complete enlightenment, those who have entered upon buddhahood; causing someone to wholly abandon the prātimokṣa vows and apply themselves only to the Great Vehicle; holding that attachment and the rest cannot be abandoned through the vehicle of the hearers, and leading others to hold such views; praising one's own qualities and, for the sake of gain, honor, and repute, disparaging others in verse; falsely claiming "I have realized the profound teaching"; speaking what is simply false; having renunciates struck down by punishment; taking what has been given to the Three Jewels, or accepting such a gift oneself; abandoning calm abiding; and giving away, for the sake of recitation, resources set aside for meditative equipoise — these are the root downfalls, the cause of the great hells. As in a dream, I sit before noble Ākāśagarbha and make confession of them.

— *Ākāśagarbha Sūtra* (the eighteen root downfalls of a bodhisattva, condensed into verse in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*; Khenpo Kunzang Pelden takes these as the principal content of "the trainings" not to be transgressed)

> Great king, you have many things to do and many duties to perform, so if you cannot bear to train in everything, in every respect, from the perfection of generosity through to the perfection of wisdom, then, great king, hold in mind — whether walking, standing, sitting, lying down, waking, eating, or drinking — always and without a break, this aspiration, this faith, this earnest wish, this prayer for complete enlightenment. Keep it in mind, hold it in your thoughts, meditate on it … and so on.

— *Sūtra of Advice to the King* (enjoining ceaseless mindfulness of bodhicitta in all activities and postures — glossed by Khenpo Kunzang Pelden as the "principal points of training" one must know)

> Carefulness is the place of the nectar, and carelessness the place of death: so the Sage taught. Therefore, in order to increase what is good, always act with respect and with care.

— *Letter to a Friend* (Suhṛllekha) (carefulness as the abode of the deathless nectar, carelessness as the abode of death)

> Just as the ground may be walkable or unwalkable, so too, it is taught, ethical discipline is the ground that supports every good quality.

— Nāgārjuna (ethical discipline as the ground supporting all qualities, as walkable and unwalkable ground)

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Having Held Bodhicitta Firm, One Must Strive Without Slackening Never to Stray from the Training

This verse opens the fourth chapter, the chapter on carefulness. All the commentaries agree in showing that the heirs of the victorious ones, having held both aspects of bodhicitta firmly as before, must always strive, without ever letting it slip, never to transgress the training.

###### 2. This Chapter Is the First of Three That Keep Bodhicitta from Declining, and Follows Generosity with the Training in Ethical Discipline

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that, since generosity has already been taught in the earlier chapters, ethical discipline is now taught through this chapter on carefulness together with a further chapter on vigilant introspection, and two more besides. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk likewise explains it as the first of three chapters that keep the precious bodhicitta, once generated, from declining.

###### 3. The Name "Heirs of the Victorious Ones" Applies Once the Bodhisattva Vow Has Been Properly Taken Up Through a Valid Rite

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that a mere loving heart toward beings is not enough to earn this name; it applies once one has taken up the bodhisattva vow through a valid rite, chiefly out of a wide intention embracing both one's own and others' good.

###### 4. "Holding It Firm" Means Resolving Never to Let Bodhicitta Go, Whatever Comes

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that from the moment bodhicitta is generated one must hold to the firm resolve that it will not be let go of, whatever happens — illustrating this with the image of setting one's resolve on catching lucidity within a dream.

###### 5. "Not Straying from the Training" Means Guarding the Eighteen Root Downfalls and the Rest, Without Transgressing Them Even at the Cost of One's Life

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that fine words are not enough; one must identify the essential points of a bodhisattva's training, and cites the eighteen root downfalls taught in the *Ākāśagarbha Sūtra*.

###### 6. Carefulness Is the Root and Ground of All Virtue, So Relying on It Keeps the Roots of Virtue from Declining and Makes Them Grow

Both Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the *Samādhirāja Sūtra* and the *Compendium*, explain carefulness as the root of all virtue.

###### 7. Carefulness Is the Source of the Deathless Nectar; Carelessness Is the Cause of Samsaric Suffering

Minyak Kunzang Sonam, citing the *Letter to a Friend*, illustrates carefulness and carelessness with the images of the abode of nectar and the abode of death.

###### 8. The Discipline to Be Held Has Three Aspects — Restraining Wrongdoing, Gathering Virtue, and Benefiting Beings — None of Which Is to Be Transgressed

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that one must strive with diligence never to transgress any of these three aspects of ethical discipline.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse opens the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, the chapter on carefulness: having held both the aspiring and engaged bodhicitta firmly as before, the heirs of the victorious ones must always strive, without letting it slip, never to stray from the training. All the commentaries agree on this common ground.

**Key points.**

- This chapter is the first of three that keep the precious bodhicitta, once generated, from declining, and follows generosity with the training in ethical discipline.
- The name "heirs of the victorious ones" applies once the bodhisattva vow has been properly taken up through a valid rite, out of a wide intention embracing both one's own and others' good.
- "Holding it firm" means resolving never to let bodhicitta go, whatever comes — like setting one's resolve on catching lucidity within a dream.
- "Not straying from the training" means guarding the eighteen root downfalls and the rest, without transgressing them even at the cost of one's life.
- Carefulness is the root and ground of all virtue and the source of the deathless nectar of nirvana, while carelessness is the cause of samsaric suffering, as Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both explain.
- The discipline to be held has three aspects — restraining wrongdoing, gathering virtue, and benefiting beings — none of which is to be transgressed, as Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains.

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^4-2`):*

> If you start something in a rush or without really thinking it through, it is fine to reconsider — even if you had already promised to do it — and decide whether to continue or let it go.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

First of all, one should recognize whether or not one has the capacity for **the bodhisattva vow,** and only then take the vow. Then the bodhisattva who has taken the vow should think as follows: **whatever has been begun rashly, without examination,** or whatever **has been examined only slightly and not properly** — **even if a pledge has already been made** to do it, it is right to engage with it only after **examining it again:** shall I **do it, or set it aside?**

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^4-2]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"Begun rashly" means a task that arose on its own, without any examination or reflection, or one that was begun after some thought of one's own but without a detailed examination. He says that even if one has already pledged to carry out such a task, it is right, going forward, to give it careful, detailed thought and then examine whether to do it or to set it aside.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-2]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Rash — that is, undertaking a task, or committing to accomplish it, without having examined anything at all; or a task examined a little, as to whether it serves a purpose or not, but not examined properly, in detail. Even where one has already committed to accomplishing such a task and made a pledge, it is right and proper, before doing exactly as pledged or dropping it, first to examine and investigate it in detail, and only then to engage in choosing to adopt or reject it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-2]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Whatever task has been begun rashly, without examining its faults and merits, or whatever task has been examined only a little and not properly — even if one has already made a pledge to it at first, it is right that later one should examine whether to do it or to set it aside, and engage or disengage accordingly.

*(Note: this commentary's prose treats this verse's assertion together with the immediately following verse's reply — the buddhas'/one's own examination of bodhicitta — in one continuous run; only the material pertaining to 4-2's own claim is drawn on here.)*

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-2]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Whatever task has been begun rashly, without examination, or whatever task has been examined a little but not properly — even if one has pledged to do it, it would be right to examine further whether to do it or to let it go, and to engage accordingly. But here it is not like that.

This means: as a reason why one should not give up bodhicitta itself — a task such as beginning to undertake, or committing to accomplish, some worldly matter like building a house, rashly and without examining anything at all; or a task examined a little as to whether it serves a purpose, but not examined properly, that is, completely — even where such a task was at first pledged and committed to, it is right and proper that afterward one calmly examine and investigate it once more, whether one goes on to complete it exactly as pledged when the conditions for completing it are in place, or sets it aside when they are not.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. There Are No Grounds to Withdraw from a Task Merely Because It Was Not Properly Examined

This verse states a general principle: whatever has been undertaken rashly, or examined only slightly, may rightly be examined again and either done or set aside, even after a pledge has already been made.

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Ngulchu Thogme, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam all treat these two verses as one continuous passage: this verse states the general reasoning that one may reconsider, so that the next verse can reply that bodhicitta is not such a case.

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk distinguishes a task begun rashly, without any examination at all, from one examined only a little but not completely.

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk uses the ordinary example of building a house to make clear what "rash" means.

The interlinear gloss explains that a bodhisattva who has recognized their own capacity and taken the vow should think in the way this verse describes.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse sets out a general principle: whatever task has been undertaken rashly, without any examination, or examined only a little and not completely, may rightly be reconsidered and either done or set aside, even where a pledge has already been made. - Tasks not properly examined fall into two kinds: those begun with no examination at all, and those examined only a little but not completely.

- This verse and the next are treated by several commentaries as one continuous passage, in which this verse's general reasoning is answered in the next.
- A bodhisattva who has taken the vow, having first recognized their own capacity, should reconsider any undertaking in just this way.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Here he means that the buddhas together with their heirs, having examined this too, saw with their own insight that this bodhicitta is the unsurpassed means of accomplishing what beings need, both for the time being and in the end. And from the side of the one taking the vow as well: having personally examined again and again that the benefits of bodhicitta are an unmistaken and unsurpassed means of accomplishing what is needed, both for the time being and in the end, one thinks it through and arrives at certainty. Not only that — at the time of actually generating bodhicitta, one calls the buddhas and their heirs to witness, makes the pledge in their presence, and resolves firmly on this commitment. Having examined and investigated in this way and then committed oneself, he says, what grounds could there possibly be to turn back? There is never any room to slacken in its training.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-3]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

It is not like that. The buddhas, who are without the slightest trace of error, together with their heirs — great bodhisattvas such as Mañjughoṣa and Maitreya, endowed with great wisdom — examined this well and taught that this precious bodhicitta has great benefit. And I myself, too, from the chapter on its benefits onward, examined it again and again — as it is said, "a matter of very great importance should be stated once, and repeated a second time" — and, seeing that it is endowed with immeasurable qualities, have already taken up this bodhicitta within my own being. So now, saying "I cannot manage to train in it" and turning back, or drawing back — what grounds are there for that? There are none. One must therefore make effort at the trainings.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-3]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

As it is taught in the *Sūtra Requested by Suvikrāntavikrāmin*, the buddhas and their heirs, such as Maitreya, endowed with great wisdom, examined this mind in the *Gaṇḍavyūha* and other sūtras, praised it for its benefits, and taught that it should not be given up. And I myself, too, having earlier examined it well by many lines of reasoning — thinking of its benefits and so on — and seen that it must certainly be taken up, have taken it up. So what grounds are there to turn back from it? One should guard it without letting it decline until buddhahood is attained.

As for the training in bodhicitta — which the buddhas and their heirs of great wisdom have examined well, and which I myself, too, examined again and again before taking the vow — what grounds are there to turn back from it? There are none.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Here, the buddhas — who are without the slightest trace of error — together with their heirs, such as Maitreya and Mañjughoṣa, endowed with great wisdom, examined this well and taught that it has great benefit; and I myself, too, having examined it again and again and seen that it is endowed with good qualities, have committed myself to accomplishing this bodhisattva conduct. So what grounds are there to turn back from it? There are none, ever; one should make effort at accomplishing it. <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The buddhas, who are without the slightest trace of error concerning any knowable thing, examined bodhicitta with their all-knowing wisdom and spoke at length of its praises throughout the Great Vehicle sūtras. And the buddhas' heirs too — those endowed with marvellous, great wisdom, such as noble Maitreya, Mañjuśrī, and Avalokiteśvara — examined bodhicitta, saw that it is endowed with immeasurable good qualities, and in the *Gaṇḍavyūha* and other sūtras spoke of its immeasurable benefits, teaching that it is not to be given up. And I myself, too, having beforehand thought of its benefits and examined it well and with whatever power I had, and, examining it again and again, saw that it is endowed with immeasurable good qualities; so, recognizing that it was right to take it up for that reason, I took it up deliberately and pledged myself to it. So what grounds are there to turn back from it? There are none whatsoever; therefore one must strive to guard it firmly, so that it does not decline, in every way possible, until great enlightenment is attained. As it is said in the *Maitreyasiṃhanāda*: "Those who strive for enlightenment are, like Mount Meru, of steadfast and unshakable strength; they do not release their pledge" — and as it is said in the *Mañjuśrī-buddhakṣetra-guṇavyūha*: "Whether a bodhisattva is a householder or one who has gone forth, once firmly established in properly holding the vow of ethical discipline, they do not transgress its training even at the cost of their life."

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This means: the fully perfect buddhas, the blessed ones, who are entirely without the stain of error, and their heart-sons — venerable Mañjughoṣa, the protector Maitreya, noble Avalokiteśvara, and the rest, all endowed with great wisdom — not only examined and investigated the generation of bodhicitta again and again themselves, but I, too, in the chapter that earlier taught the benefits of generating bodhicitta, examined and investigated it repeatedly on my own part. Now there is nothing left to do but accomplish, properly, those very trainings of a bodhisattva. So what grounds could there possibly be for giving up that practice, for turning back or drawing away from it? There are none whatsoever, not even the slightest. That is the meaning.

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Ngulchu Thogme, and Minyak Kunzang Sonam treat the two verses as one continuous passage, in which this verse answers that bodhicitta is not a case of an unexamined undertaking.

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains this clearly; Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain it the same way.

Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both give this counsel.

###### 7. The Scriptural Source Cited Is the Maitreyasiṃhanāda's Image of Those Who Strive for Enlightenment Being as Unmoving as Mount Meru

Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam both cite this passage.
