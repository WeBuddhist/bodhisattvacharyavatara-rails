## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the eleventh and twelfth verses of the fifth chapter. Let us look together at this point: we do not need to fight enemies as numerous as space. By defeating only our own angry mind, we win a great victory.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a valuable teaching that is easy to understand and encouraging.

Long ago, King Manicuda gave his finest elephant to a brahmin. That brahmin sold the elephant to a lesser rival of the king. The rival king then led an army, together with that same elephant, to attack King Manicuda. Even so, King Manicuda did not commit the wrongdoing of striking back in anger, for the sake of this one life. He never retaliated at all. He even gave his own kingdom to the one who had attacked him.

This shows us how to give up anger. We can practice patience without striking back at those who harm us.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, the moment anger arises in my mind, I will catch it with mindfulness.

**Explanation:** *(Patience Practice)* The unruly beings and enemies in this world are as vast and endless as space. We can never defeat and tame every outer enemy all at once. But if we defeat and calm only the anger in our own mind, we gain the same benefit as defeating every outer enemy. So instead of feeling anger toward difficult outer conditions, we should recognize anger the moment it arises in the mind, and work to tame it, starting today.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Rather, the sutras teach that the perfection of moral discipline consists in attaining the mind that abandons all wrongdoing — such as killing living beings oneself and the rest.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that the perfection of moral discipline is not established by harm in the world having been utterly eliminated; rather — needless to say regarding actually harming others — it is established when even the harmful intention itself, within one's own mind-stream, has been restrained at its root and brought to an end. Thus he teaches that this good quality, which depends on one's own mind-stream, must be accomplished within that very mind-stream.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Moral discipline, too, arises from the mind. Take killing, for instance: since there is no way to drive fish and other beings away, in any place whatsoever, to somewhere they will never be killed, or to establish a place where others will not kill them, moral discipline does not consist in whether or not others actually kill them. Rather, it consists in genuinely attaining the mind that renounces harm — thinking, "even should it cost my own life, I will not kill" — regardless of whether another kills them or not. This is why the sutras teach: "What is the perfection of moral discipline? It is the intention that renounces harming others." In the same way, there is no way to guard one's vows merely by first eliminating objects such as women; rather, if one has, in one's own mind, the very intention that renounces attachment toward women and the like, that is moral discipline — and without it, merely not committing a wrongdoing for lack of opportunity is not moral discipline. Hence moral discipline, too, arises from the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

It follows that the completion of the perfection of moral discipline does not depend on having eliminated all creatures liable to be killed, such as deer and fish; for it is impossible to drive away and remove every one of them, everywhere, from a place where no one could kill them. For this reason, the perfection of moral discipline is completed by fully attaining familiarity with the intention that renounces the wish to harm, the wish to take what is not given, and so forth — as the sutras teach: "What is the perfection of moral discipline? It is the intention that renounces harming others."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

If the perfection of moral discipline meant that harm had entirely ceased, then even the past Protectors would not have reached that perfection, for wherever fish, frogs, and other living beings, or others' wealth, women, and the like exist, harm — such as killing and stealing — remains as long as they have not been removed. Therefore, the perfection of moral discipline consists in attaining, toward those beings that do exist, the mind that renounces harming them and the like — as the sutras teach: "What is the perfection of moral discipline? It is the intention that renounces harming others."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

If someone holds that moral discipline is complete once harm such as killing has ceased toward all sentient beings, then even the buddhas of the past would not have completed the perfection of moral discipline: for wherever fish, deer, and other creatures exist, it is impossible to drive them all away from harm, and there still appear to be many who inflict harm. Hence, even though harm to others exists, the perfection of moral discipline is said to be fully completed by attaining, purely, the mind that renounces the very basis of harming all sentient beings by oneself. As stated before: "If one relies constantly on a loving mind, cultivates it, and brings it repeatedly to mind, moral discipline will become complete," and: "What is the perfection of moral discipline? It is the intention that renounces harming others." Therefore moral discipline, too, is accomplished by making the mind serviceable.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Some hold that the measure of the completed perfection of moral discipline is that every last instance of harm — killing, stealing, and the like — has ceased without remainder, meaning that beings liable to be killed and wealth liable to be stolen no longer exist; they thus consider the outer deed to be of primary importance. But if that were so, then even the buddhas of the past would not have completed the perfection of moral discipline: for wherever fish, birds, deer, and other creatures liable to be killed, one's own or others' wealth, others' women, liquor, and the like exist, it is impossible for anyone whatsoever to drive them all away entirely so that no one could ever kill or take them — and, in fact, since they have not been driven away, killing, stealing, and other harms are still widely seen to occur. This shows that the completion of moral discipline does not depend on the exhaustion of beings liable to be killed and the like. One might then ask: how, then, is it completed? It is taught that, whether or not sentient beings outwardly become free of harm, moral discipline is completed by bringing to perfection one's familiarity with the mind that turns away from inflicting harm within one's own mental continuum. That is: the mind which turns away, in intention, from the very basis of harming others — through the wish to kill, the wish to take what is not given, and so forth — is moral discipline; and the sutras teach that the perfection of moral discipline is completed by attaining ever-greater familiarity with that mind. Accordingly, moral discipline too is to be understood as consisting in the very mind that renounces harm, and its completion is established through bringing that familiarity to its culmination — so that the intention is what chiefly matters.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

There is no way that fish, deer, pigs, and other such creatures, or insects, birds, and other such beings, in whatever place they may be, could be driven away to, or kept safe in, a place where none of them would ever kill or harm one another. Rather, the sutras of the buddhas teach that the perfection of moral discipline is brought to completion in dependence on this: that the bodhisattva, even should death fall upon his or her own life, cultivates again and again, and grows thoroughly accustomed to, the utterly pure intention or pledge of renunciation not to take the life of any other sentient being oneself — and, having thereby attained the culmination of that familiarity, the perfection of moral discipline itself likewise reaches its culmination.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-11]]

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR_bo -->
##### The Father and Son Who Both Became Monks

In a side-note to Ngulchu Thogme's commentary, it is told that a father and son, both of whom had become monks, were traveling together. The father, who lived a life of very strict asceticism, would grow weak if he missed a meal when the day grew late. Fearing that his father would collapse from exhaustion, the son accompanied him; and as they traveled, though he urged his father to walk faster, the father's pace was weak, so the son pushed him from behind — but the father's head struck a stone, and he died. Yet, since the son's intention held no wish whatsoever to harm his father, but only the wish to help him reach shelter quickly, the father, upon dying, was reborn as a god. This is cited by the followers of the Great Vehicle as an example showing that intention is what matters most: the wholesomeness or unwholesomeness of an action is determined not by its outer result, but decisively by the intention that motivates it.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Perfection of Moral Discipline Does Not Depend on Eliminating Every Being Liable to Be Killed

All the commentaries agree that the completion of the perfection of moral discipline does not depend on having removed every sentient being liable to be killed, or on having made every place free of harm — for no one could ever drive all beings into a place entirely free from harm.

###### 2. The Measure of Moral Discipline Is Bringing to Completion the Mind That Renounces Harming Others

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains, using the example of renouncing attachment toward women, that the measure of moral discipline is bringing to completion one's familiarity with the mind that renounces, at its root, the very intention to harm other sentient beings — such as killing and taking what is not given — even at the cost of one's own life.

###### 3. Merely Not Committing Wrongdoing for Lack of Opportunity Is Not Moral Discipline

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains clearly that merely failing to commit wrongdoing because one lacks the ability or opportunity to do so is not moral discipline; an actual intention that renounces harm must be present in the mind.

###### 4. The Example of the Father and Son Shows That Karma Is Determined by Intention

In the Great Vehicle's system, the wholesomeness or unwholesomeness of an action is determined not by its outer result or deed, but decisively by the intention that motivates it — as illustrated by the story of the father and son.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The perfection of moral discipline does not depend on outer circumstances — such as placing every outer sentient being in a state free from harm — but is established by cultivating and bringing to completion, within one's own mind-stream, the intention that renounces at its root the wish to harm and injure others.

**Key points.**

- **The measure of moral discipline does not depend on outer circumstances.** Outer sentient beings such as fish, birds, and deer can neither be driven away everywhere so that they are never killed, nor placed beyond harm; and if the absence of all outer harm in the world were made the measure of the perfection of moral discipline, then even the buddhas of the past would not have completed it.
- **The measure of moral discipline is cultivating the mind that renounces harmful intention.** The completion of the perfection of moral discipline consists in cultivating and purifying, even at the cost of one's own life, the intention that renounces at its root the causing of harm to others, such as killing.
- ⚑ **Merely doing no wrong is not enough.** Merely refraining from wrongdoing for lack of ability or opportunity does not constitute moral discipline; an actual intention that renounces harm and attachment must arise in the mind-stream.
- **The example of how intention determines karma.** The wholesomeness or unwholesomeness of an action is determined not by its outer result alone, but chiefly by the intention that motivates it — as illustrated by the story of the father and son, two monks, in which the son, having only a wish to benefit his father, caused his father's death yet incurred no harmful intent, so that the father was reborn as a god.
- **Scriptural authority.** The sutras likewise teach that moral discipline is perfected through the intention that renounces harming others, and through relying upon and cultivating a loving mind.

Some hold that the measure of the completed perfection of patience is that all enemies have been subdued and exhausted, and that mutual harm has entirely ceased so that no anger or resentment remains between beings — considering the outer deed to be of primary importance. But if that were so, then the patience of the past Protectors would not have been completed, for unruly sentient beings are limitless like space; there is no way whatsoever that anyone could tame or overcome them all, and, in fact, harm-doing enemies and mutual injury are still widely seen even now. For this reason, even though many harm-doers exist, if one conquers and abandons, by the power of the sharp weapon of the antidote — mindfulness and introspection and the like — this single angry mind of one's own, it is as though all those outer enemies have been conquered: because, by not holding harm-doers to be enemies, the mind is not disturbed, and because in this life and every future life all enemies become pacified. As the sutra says: "If anger is subdued, enemies are pacified; otherwise, enemies increase."

However, if the bodhisattva who strives in the trainings conquers, through meditating on patience alone, this very intention of anger and hatred toward other sentient beings, then it is as though all those outer harm-doing enemies and adversarial spirits have already been conquered.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. The Perfection of Patience Does Not Depend on Subduing Every Unruly Being

All the commentaries agree that the perfection of patience does not depend on subduing or taming every unruly sentient being — beings limitless like space — but is completed simply by conquering, through the meditation of patience, this single angry mind of one's own.

###### 2. Not Holding Harm-Doers to Be Enemies Both Calms One's Own Mind and Pacifies All Enemies

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains clearly that conquering one's own angry mind is equivalent to conquering every outer enemy, because not holding harm-doers to be enemies leaves the mind undisturbed, and because in this life and every future life all enemies come to be pacified.

###### 3. The Sutra Teaches: If Anger Is Subdued, Enemies Are Pacified — Otherwise They Increase

Both Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam cite this scriptural passage as their authority.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The perfection of patience does not depend on exhausting every outer harm-doer and enemy; rather, it is accomplished, through conquering this single angry mind within one's own mind-stream, as though every outer enemy had thereby been conquered.

**Key points.**

- **Harm-doers are limitless.** Outer harm-doing, unruly sentient beings are limitless like space, so they can never be entirely exhausted.
- ⚑ **The untenable notion of completing patience by subduing all enemies.** Some hold that patience is completed once all enemies have been subdued and harm has ceased; but on that view even the buddhas of the past would not have completed patience, so patience does not depend on exhausting every outer enemy.
- **Conquering the angry mind.** If one conquers, through the meditation of patience, this single angry mind within one's own mind-stream, it is as though all outer enemies have been conquered at once.
- **The practice of mindful vigilance.** If one conquers this single angry mind of one's own by the weapon of mindfulness and introspection, then, since one no longer holds harm-doers to be enemies, the mind is not disturbed, and all enemies become pacified.
- **Scriptural authority.** As the sutra states, if anger is subdued, enemies are pacified; otherwise, enemies increase.
- **By way of an example.** King Maṇicūḍa gave his elephant to a brahmin; though later attacked by an outer army, he said, "For the sake of this one life, I will not commit wrongdoing," and, without retaliating, even gave away his kingdom — illustrating patience through the abandonment of the angry mind.
