## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the thirty-first, thirty-second, and thirty-third verses of the fifth chapter. Just as a sighted person stands beside someone who is blind, this teaching clearly shows that the buddhas and bodhisattvas are always watching our conduct.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us an example that is easy to understand and encouraging.

If a sighted person sits in front of someone who is blind, then no matter what the blind person does, the sighted person sees everything. In the same way, even though we cannot see them, the buddhas and bodhisattvas directly see every one of our bad actions of body, speech, and mind, large or small.

So even when we are alone in solitude, we should never engage in bad conduct. As it is said: even if we do wrong thinking no one sees, how could we ever be at ease, as if we had eaten poison? The gods and the yogis, with their pure eyes, cannot possibly fail to see.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will remember that the buddhas and bodhisattvas are watching, and with a sense of conscience and dignity, I will give up wrongdoing.

**Explanation:** *(Ethics Practice)* Remembering that the buddhas and bodhisattvas see everything without any obstruction is the best way to restrain our bad conduct. By remembering this constantly, a sense of conscience and dignity arises, and we turn away from wrong actions. Relying on mindfulness and awareness, our discipline becomes pure.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He teaches that the buddhas, together with their heirs, possess a wisdom-vision of knowing that is free of attachment and obstruction. When we think that we ourselves are dwelling right now before their ever-watching gaze, a sense of care and conscientiousness can arise in us, unlike anything possible before an ordinary gaze, where hidden things go unseen by ordinary people. Before the vision of unobstructed wisdom-knowing there is nothing hidden that needs to be regretted later, and no possibility of acting one way in front and another behind. We may manage to deceive and mislead ordinary people, but there is no place to practice deceit or trickery before the buddhas' vision, or with regard to the workings of karmic cause and effect. It is therefore extremely important that we constantly maintain an honest, upright manner, with no difference between what is done openly and what is done in secret.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Furthermore, one should think: "The buddhas and bodhisattvas possess a wisdom-vision that is unobstructed with respect to any knowable thing, at all times — there is not the slightest thing they fail to know or fail to see — and in the presence of all of them, I dwell, without a single moment's interruption, forever." For the buddhas and bodhisattvas are like a sighted person standing beside someone blind: they directly know every one of my bad actions of body, speech, and mind, great or small, and would be pained at heart by them. Therefore it is not fitting to act badly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Furthermore, the buddhas and bodhisattvas possess vision that is unobstructed with regard to all knowable things, in every place and at every time; thinking, "I dwell, without any concealment, forever in the presence of all of them," one should, on that basis, take up — for one's own sake — a sense of conscience, together with respect for the Teacher and the Dharma and fear of ripening effects, and in this way hold to mindfulness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Furthermore, the buddhas and bodhisattvas possess eyes that see, at all times, all knowable things without obstruction; thinking, "I dwell forever in the presence of all of them," one should, in that way, take up a sense of conscience, respect for the training, and a fear of shame before them, and thereby hold to mindfulness.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Furthermore, thinking, "The great buddhas and bodhisattvas possess wisdom-eyes that see all knowable things unobstructedly at all times, and in the presence of all of them I dwell continuously and forever, without interruption" — holding this thought in mind, and, for one's own sake, taking up a sense of conscience that shrinks from wrongdoing, faith in and respect for the trainings, and a sense of shame that fears the criticism of others, one should abide accordingly. If one does so, mindfulness will arise with ease.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Whether or not a guru or spiritual companion happens to be nearby, whichever buddhas and bodhisattvas there may be who possess the wholly pure vision of a wisdom-eye that engages every knowable thing, in every place and at every time, without obscuration or obstruction — in the presence of all of them, I too dwell forever, or dwell openly and without any concealment. Thinking and reflecting on this in one's mind, and thereby taking up, for one's own sake, a sense of conscience that shrinks from unbecoming actions, wrongdoing, and downfalls; respect for the Teacher and for the trainings he taught; and a sense of shame that fears the ripening of karma — thinking, "It would not do to bring shame upon the hearts of the buddhas and bodhisattvas" — along with a fear of others' censure, if one thinks and takes this up in just this way, guards the three doors, and dwells well, mindfulness will readily arise.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Furthermore — take, for instance, the occasion when one has become far removed from one's abbot, preceptor, and spiritual teachers, and dwells alone in a mountain retreat or the like. Even then, though other people — one's own guru or Dharma companions — do not see one, the fully enlightened buddhas and bodhisattvas possess vision that engages every knowable thing, at every time, in a manner free of attachment and obstruction; they are thus like a sighted person, while one is oneself, lacking the eye of wisdom, like someone blind. Just as a sighted person standing before a blind person sees all of that blind person's circumstances, so too the buddhas and bodhisattvas see everything of mine, and I dwell continuously, at all times, in the presence of all of them. Therefore, thinking, "If I engage in a path of non-virtuous action motivated by attachment and the like, the buddhas and bodhisattvas will be disheartened," one should apply oneself to abandoning wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-31]]

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree That Remembering the Buddhas' Unobstructed Vision Gives Rise to Mindfulness with Ease

All the commentators uniformly present that, although one dwells alone, remembering that one always stands before the unobstructed knowing and seeing of the buddhas and bodhisattvas gives rise to a sense of conscience and shame, so that mindfulness arises with ease.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites the Kāśyapa-parivarta as Scriptural Authority That the Buddha Knows Even What Is Done Behind Closed Doors

Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the Kāśyapa-parivarta, showing that the Buddha knows without obstruction even what a renunciate does after shutting the door of his cell.

###### 3. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Teaches That There Is No Place, Before the Buddhas' Unobstructed Vision, for Acting One Way in Front and Another Behind

His Holiness the Dalai Lama, the fourteenth, teaches that before the buddhas' unobstructed wisdom-vision there is not even room for the very idea of acting one way openly and another way in secret — a point he applies as relevant to how people should conduct themselves honestly even in society at large today.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden Teaches That This Practice's Fruit Is Not Only Ease of Mindfulness but the Effortless, Repeated Arising of Recollection of the Buddha

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden teaches that the fruit of practicing this root verse is not merely that mindfulness arises with ease, but that the recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānusmṛti) also comes to arise, again and again, without effort.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches an outstanding method for continually holding to mindfulness: even while dwelling alone in a solitary place, one reflects that one stands always in the presence of all the buddhas and bodhisattvas, whose wisdom-eyes are unobstructed by time or place. Remembering this in such a way gives rise, in one's own mind-stream, to a sense of conscience and shame, and thereby the mindfulness and awareness that guard the conduct of the three doors without slackening arise of their own accord.

**Key points.**

- **Matching the image to its meaning; scripture confirms it.** As the learned commentators explain, since the buddhas' wisdom-vision is unobstructed, meditating that one dwells before the buddhas and bodhisattvas even while alone is the one method for readily giving rise to mindfulness. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both give the image of a sighted person standing beside someone blind: though we ourselves do not see the buddhas, the buddhas see clearly every action of our three doors. Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the Kāśyapa-parivarta to show that a corrupt renunciate who shuts himself away to entertain unchaste thoughts, thinking the buddhas do not know, is nothing but a thief dressed in the ochre robes, and must be trained not to act so. And as the jātaka verse says, doing wrong in secret is like drinking poison and hoping to be at ease — it is impossible that the gods and accomplished yogis, with their pure eyes, fail to see it.
- **His Holiness the Dalai Lama on honesty before the buddhas' vision.** His Holiness the Dalai Lama, the fourteenth, teaches that since there is no place before the buddhas' unobstructed wisdom-vision to act one way in front and another behind — unlike deceiving ordinary people — it is of great importance that we hold to an honest, upright manner at all times, with nothing hidden; a teaching whose relevance extends well beyond the spiritual path to how people conduct themselves in society generally.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Furthermore, since the buddhas together with their heirs possess a wisdom-vision of knowing that is free of attachment and obstruction, when we think that we are dwelling right now before their ever-watching gaze, a sense of care can arise unlike anything possible before an ordinary gaze, where hidden things go unseen. Before the vision of unobstructed wisdom-knowing, there is nothing that needs to be regretted later, and no possibility of acting one way in front and another behind. The root verse's word for "take up" carries the sense of "give this some thought" — it is an exhortation urging us to reflect on this. He explains that when practice of this kind is accomplished, not only does mindfulness arise, but the recollection of the Buddha comes again and again as well.

Thinking again and again in this way without forgetting it, take up, for one's own sake, a sense of conscience that shrinks from wrongdoing and respect for the training, and, for others' sake, a sense of shame endowed with fear that shrinks from wrongdoing, and dwell accordingly. For one who dwells in this way, not only does mindfulness alone arise, but — among the six recollections taught in the bodhisattva training (recollection of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, giving, ethical discipline, and the gods) — the recollection of the Buddha also comes, again and again, to that person, bringing this good quality with it.

For one who thinks and dwells in the manner just explained, not only does mere mindfulness arise; because this way of meditating is a profound point of practice, moreover, the special recollection called recollection of the Buddha's qualities, which carries inconceivable benefit, arises for that person not just once but again and again, effortlessly. Thinking in this way, one should take up — with reference to oneself, a sense of conscience together with respect for the trainings in one's own continuum; and, with reference to others, namely the buddhas and bodhisattvas, as well as one's own preceptors and spiritual teachers, a sense of fear that they might be displeased — and dwell in that manner. <!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree That a Firmly Established Sense of Conscience and Shame Brings the Recollection of the Buddha Effortlessly, Again and Again

All the commentators uniformly present that, as the fruit of firmly establishing a mind endowed with conscience and shame, the recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānusmṛti) arises again and again, of its own accord and without exertion.

###### 2. ⚑ Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Number the Recollections Differently, Yet Point to the Same Essential Meaning

The commentators give differing counts for the recollections: Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk enumerates six recollections (of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, ethical discipline, generosity, and the gods), while Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the Śikṣāsamuccaya (Compendium of Trainings) for a set of twelve mindfulnesses, explained there as being for the abandonment of fruitless conduct. Though the counts differ, both are presented as pointing to the same essential point.

###### 3. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Glosses the Root Verse's Word "Take Up" as "Give This Some Thought"

His Holiness the Dalai Lama, the fourteenth, explains the root verse's word for "take up" as carrying the sense of "give this some thought," offering a close reading of the term's precise meaning.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Teaches That Even the Mere Name "Buddha" Arising in the Mind Brings Immeasurable Merit

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that even if only the mere name "Buddha" arises in the mind, it brings about an immeasurable mass of merit, thus placing the benefit of recollecting the Buddha at the heart of the teaching.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches the fruit of holding to the mindfulness — taught in the immediately preceding verse — that the buddhas always see us. When one gives rise to a sense of conscience toward oneself, respect for one's teachers and the trainings, and a sense of fear and shame regarding wrongdoing, the mind of recollecting the Buddha arises of its own accord and by its very nature, and this in turn generates the great strength needed to remain established in the bodhisattva's conduct.

**Key points.**

- **From cause to fruit.** Where the immediately preceding verse taught reflection on the buddhas' and bodhisattvas' unobstructed wisdom-vision as always seeing us, this verse teaches the fruit that arises from that cause: it shows how conscience in one's own continuum, respect for the teacher and the training, and fear and shame regarding wrongdoing give rise to these excellent mental factors. His Holiness the Dalai Lama's gloss of the root verse's word for "take up" as "give this some thought" is an exhortation that mere understanding is not enough — one must reflect on and cultivate this repeatedly in one's own mind.
- **⚑ Differing enumerations, one shared point.** Once a mind of conscience, respect, and fear is thus firmly established, mindfulness recollecting the Buddha arises by its own nature. The commentators trace this back to their sources in differing ways: Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk links it to recollection of the Buddha among the six recollections, teaching that merely holding the name of the Buddha in mind brings immeasurable merit, while Minyak Kunzang Sonam traces it to the twelve mindfulnesses taught in Śāntideva's own Śikṣāsamuccaya (Compendium of Trainings), explained there as required for abandoning fruitless conduct and for not transgressing the Tathāgata's word. Although the enumerations differ — six recollections as against twelve mindfulnesses — there is no conflict on the essential point that conscience and shame, by restraining wrongdoing, cause the root of virtue that is recollection of the Buddha to increase; on this, all the commentaries arrive at one and the same intent.

##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

When there comes about a firm grasp, through mindfulness, of the way of adopting and discarding — the knowledge of what is and is not fitting, held fast and not forgotten — at that time, once mindfulness stands firm at the gate of the mind for the sake of guarding, awareness arises that keeps watch over how one's own conduct of the three doors engages with whatever basis is at hand. ##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

How awareness arises from such mindfulness is as follows: when mindfulness — which does not forget the objects of virtue — comes, like a doorkeeper, to stand close by at the doorway of the house of the mind, for the sake of guarding against the horde of afflictive thieves such as non-awareness and the rest, then awareness, too — which examines and correctly knows whatever state the three doors are in — comes without effort; and even what has, on some occasion, declined and strayed elsewhere, as well as what has never arisen before, newly arises through the power of mindfulness, and having arisen, remains firm without declining. This shows how awareness arises and is sustained in dependence on mindfulness. There are also occasions when mindfulness is, in turn, drawn near by means of the examining and analyzing carried out by awareness; since the two thus generate each other reciprocally, they go together like two companions on the same path — and, in the same way, when either one declines, the other declines along with it.

When mindfulness — that is, not forgetting what is to be adopted and discarded, which is like a doorkeeper — comes to stand at the door of one's own mind, that house-like dwelling, for the sake of guarding it, then, at that time, engaging with awareness in the conduct of body, speech, and mind — thinking, "the body should act thus, speech should speak thus, the mind should think thus" — also comes about of its own accord. And if there are occasions when one forgets from time to time, or mindfulness has somewhat declined, these too, gradually, come to be accompanied by awareness; and once one has mindfulness, it comes back and one becomes mindful again — this and similar such benefits are what is being taught here.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen Marks This Verse as the Text's Pivot from Sustaining Mindfulness to Sustaining Awareness

All the commentators uniformly present that, as the fruit of mindfulness guarding the gate of the mind and barring the afflictive thieves, awareness (vigilance, saṃprajanya) arises of its own accord, without effort, and even what has declined arises again. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen marks this as a major structural juncture in the text, identifying it in his own outline as the point where the text turns from the topic of sustaining mindfulness to the topic of sustaining awareness.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Likens Mindfulness and Awareness to Two Companions Traveling Together on the Same Path

Minyak Kunzang Sonam teaches that mindfulness and awareness mutually generate each other, and that when either one declines the other declines along with it — a relationship he compares to two companions traveling the same path together — thereby clearly setting out the reciprocal, causal relationship between the two.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Concludes That Sustaining Mindfulness and Awareness Together Is the Unsurpassed Method for Guarding the Mind

Minyak Kunzang Sonam further teaches that accomplished masters skilled in the essential points of practice have repeatedly explained that, ultimately, the arising and increase of every quality of the grounds and paths belonging to both sūtra and mantra within the Great Vehicle comes down to nothing other than the manner of sustaining mindfulness and awareness — thereby showing that this method of sustaining mindfulness and awareness together is the unsurpassed method for guarding the mind.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse shows how, in dependence on sustaining mindfulness at the gate of the mind, awareness arises naturally of its own force. When the task of guarding the gate of the mind with mindfulness is made firm, awareness — which examines the conduct of body, speech, and mind — comes about without artifice, and even wholesome qualities that had earlier declined through carelessness are restored again. This is, so to speak, a crucial pivot-point in the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*'s method for guarding the mind.

**Key points.**

- **The text's pivot point; mindfulness as door-keeper.** Following Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen's reading of the text's structure, this verse marks the great pivot-point where, having already taught the manner of sustaining mindfulness, the text turns to teach how awareness arises from it. As Khenpo Kunzang Pelden, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, and others explain, once mindfulness stands in the house of the mind like a door-keeper, the horde of afflictive thieves cannot enter, and by that power, the awareness that constantly examines the actions of body, speech, and mind comes about effortlessly and of its own force. Even if mindfulness and awareness should decline through carelessness, merely relying on mindfulness again restores and revives the abandoned wholesome qualities along with mindfulness and awareness themselves.
- **Two companions on the path; the root of all path qualities.** As Minyak Kunzang Sonam further explains, mindfulness and awareness mutually support and generate each other like two companions setting out together on the same path, so that when one declines, the other necessarily declines as well. He concludes that the great adepts of old, learned in both sūtra and tantra, taught that the root from which every quality of the grounds and paths of both sūtra and mantra arises comes down, in the end, to nothing but this very practice of sustaining mindfulness and awareness in union — establishing this as the unsurpassed method for guarding the mind.
