# Day 64 — Burning flesh, burning mind: why knowingly returning to hell is unforgivable

**Date:** Sep 7  
**Chapter:** 4  
**Verses covered:** 4-25 to 4-26

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the two verses 25 and 26 from the fourth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. If we know right from wrong and still lead ourselves back into the fires of hell, nothing but regret can come of it.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a story that is easy to understand and encouraging.

For example, when a father sets out to sea, he leaves instructions with his relatives for his young child. In the same way, when the Teacher, the Buddha, was about to pass into nirvana, he gave this instruction to the noble Avalokiteshvara and Manjushri: "When the beings now dwelling in hell are one day freed and gain a human body, hand over to them this treasury of Dharma gathered over many eons."

So this precious human body we have gained right now has come to us because we were freed from hell in the past. Having found this rare human body in this way, it is truly unfitting to do wrongdoing and go back to hell again.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, when I know that some wrongdoing I want to do is not right, I will give it up and not do it.

**Explanation:** *(Avoiding wrongdoing)* To make this human body meaningful, we must guard our own mind. If we do wrongdoing while knowing right from wrong, deep regret will follow. If we act badly while knowing it is not right, we will go to hell. So it is very important to respect and guard our own wisdom.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

To show that there is yet another result of carelessness: when the utterly unbearable fire of the hell-beings burns my body for a long time, then, thinking "why did I act in such a way," the blazing fire of unbearable regret will inevitably torment my mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^4-25]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

This refers to the time of actually facing death, when some people, as an unpleasant sign of being about to be reborn in a lower state, are struck with terror. Some, even while covered with a warm blanket at the time of dying, say "I am freezing — please make it warmer," or panic and cry "someone, please pull me out!" Some, saying "I am burning," seek out a cold sensation, while others, saying "I am freezing," seek out a hot one — this is a well-known sign that arises before death. When a sensation of intense cold arises and one thinks "if only there were a little warmth," a craving for warmth, even burning heat, is generated; this, as a condition, ripens the karma, accumulated over a long time before, for being reborn in a hot hell. Likewise, when a sensation of burning or heat arises and one thinks "if only there were some coolness," a powerful wish for coolness is generated, and this ripens the karma for being reborn in a cold hell. At such a time, even though one has already determined with certainty — from the sign that has arisen indicating where one will be reborn — that unbearable regret arises, he says, there is nothing at all one can do about it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-25]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

In the phrase "when the hells' fire, so unbearable, burns...": since it is unbearable, when for a long time my body — even more tender-fleshed now than in this life — comes to experience the suffering of being burned, then, thinking "if I had accomplished some Dharma practice, there would have been no point in experiencing this; it is because I did not accomplish the Dharma" — or, when those fallen into hell are told, "because you did such-and-such a deed, you now experience this suffering" — an unbearable regret blazes up like a fire in the inner mind, and there is no doubt whatsoever that the mind will thereby experience the harsh torment of suffering, hard to bear.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^4-25]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Through the fault of degenerating bodhicitta and the like, one is born in hell, and when the unbearable fire of hell burns my body for a long time, then the blazing fire of unbearable regret will inevitably torment my own mind — therefore one should strive to remain untainted by faults and downfalls.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^4-25]]

After dying, when the unbearably intense fire of hell burns my body for a long time, the blazing fire of unbearable regret will inevitably torment my own mind.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

After death and transmigration, one falls into hell, where, as is said, the fire of ordinary fire, the fire of sandalwood, the fire at the end of an age, and the fire of the eight hot hells each burn four times hotter than the one before — by such utterly unbearable fire, my tender-fleshed body is burned for a long time and made to suffer. At that time, the guardians of hell say, "because you did such-and-such a deed, you now experience this suffering," and an unbearable regret, like a blazing fire, arises, so that the mind inevitably comes to experience intense torment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^4-25]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Through the fault of degenerating bodhicitta and one's vows, and so forth, after death one is born in hell, and when the utterly unbearable fire of hell burns my extremely tender-fleshed body for a long time, causing me to experience unbearable suffering, then not only does grief arise in myself, but the hell-guardians also say, "because you committed this sinful deed when you had obtained a human body before, you now experience this ripened result" — and reflecting on this, an unbearable regret blazes up like fire and inevitably burns and torments one's own mind. Therefore one must strive again and again to purify the sins and downfalls that are the cause of being born in the lower realms. On this point, the Indian text says: "Because I did not train, later too the fire of torment will burn my mind for a long time" — and the Indian commentaries explain it in the same way; "did not train" means one did not train in the trainings.

As for the difference in heat among hell-fires: it is said that the fire of sandalwood is four times hotter than ordinary fire, and likewise the fire at the end of an age and the reviving fire and so forth — the fires of the eight hot hells — are each, in turn, four times hotter than the one before.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-25]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"If the unbearable fire of hell burns my body for a long time, the blazing fire of unbearable regret will inevitably torment my mind" — this means: in a future rebirth, being born in a hot hell, and when one is burned for a long time by that same fire of hell — so intensely hot that it is utterly unbearable to endure — at that time one recalls: "when I obtained a Dharma-endowed human body in this world, I generated the supreme aspiration to enlightenment, yet I did not properly strive at and accomplish its trainings and practices." Generating regret over this, one's own mind is inevitably tormented again and again by the blazing fire of unbearable regret and made to suffer — this, he says, needs no further explanation.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^4-25]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Falling to Hell After Squandering the Freedoms and Endowments Inevitably Brings the Suffering of Fire Together with the Torment of Regret

All the commentaries agree in explaining that when the utterly unbearable fire of hell burns the body for a long time, the thought "why did I act that way" blazes up as an unbearable fire of regret, tormenting the mind.

###### 2. Sabzang and Minyak Kunzang Sonam Both Set Out How the Heat of Hell-Fire Intensifies Fourfold, Stage by Stage

It is explained that the fire of sandalwood is four times hotter than ordinary fire, and likewise the fire at the end of an age and the fires of the eight hot hells each intensify fourfold in turn.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites the Compendium, the Sūtra Requested by Prasenajit, and Nāgārjuna's Own Words to Counsel Against Squandering the Freedoms and Endowments

Scripture is adduced to show that even a being born in hell comes to regret the wasted freedoms and endowments, and that one who commits non-virtue without faith in karmic ripening has no answer to give before the Lord of Death.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Explains That Regret Arises Again and Again Over Failing to Strive at the Trainings of Bodhicitta

At the time of experiencing the suffering of fire in a hot hell, regret arises repeatedly over having generated the supreme aspiration to enlightenment before, yet not having properly trained in it.

###### 5. His Holiness the Dalai Lama Shows How the Signs of Cold or Heat That Arise at the Moment of Death Give a Foretaste of the Karma for One's Future Rebirth

When, at the moment of death, a sense of cold or heat shifts and a strong craving for coolness or warmth arises, this, as a condition, ripens the karma for rebirth in a cold or hot hell respectively.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that when the unbearable fire of hell burns the body for a long time, an equally unbearable fire of regret — over one's own wrongdoing — inevitably torments the mind. All the commentaries agree on this basic ground.

**Key points.**

- Falling into hell as the result of carelessness — such as degenerating bodhicitta or one's vows — brings not only the suffering of the body being burned, but also unbearable regret that torments the mind; all the commentaries agree on this.
- Sabzang and Minyak Kunzang Sonam together set out how the heat of hell-fire intensifies fourfold, stage by stage, from ordinary fire up through the fires of the eight hot hells.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam adduces scripture — the Compendium, the Sūtra Requested by Prasenajit, and Nāgārjuna's words — warning that squandering the freedoms and endowments leads to deep regret in the lower realms.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk explains that regret arises again and again from having failed to properly train in the bodhicitta one once generated.
- His Holiness the Dalai Lama adds that the shifting sensations of cold and heat at the moment of death serve as a sign ripening the karma for one's next rebirth, at which point regret arises but nothing can be done.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

*(His Holiness the Dalai Lama gives no separate teaching for verse 4-26; his explanation of 4-26 is combined with 4-27's into one continuous passage that follows the 4-27 transclusion. The same passage is repeated on the 4-27 page.)*

As for this ground that is extremely hard to find, and, once found, easily accomplishes both temporary and ultimate aims — however it may be, one has now found it, through good fortune, by sheer chance; and even while I myself remain in my ordinary state of mind, possessed of the discernment to distinguish good from bad, what to adopt and what to abandon, if I still fall under the sway of procrastination and laziness, fail to accumulate good karma, and let my present conduct run wild in this manner, and thereby lead myself once again to that hell — then it is as though I had been bewildered by a spell, or, disparaging myself, I say "this is exactly the same as having no mind at all." Does it not seem, right in front of oneself, like a genuinely painful predicament? Even so, though it may be difficult to cut off the continuum of wrongdoing all at once, one can at least reduce how often one engages in the basis of wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^4-26]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Reflecting on whichever of the causes, examples, or numbers one likes: having found — after such a long time of not finding it — this kind of basis of leisure and endowment, this basis or ground on which one may accomplish benefit and happiness, on which one is fit to practice the holy Dharma of virtue, obtained now through the good fortune of merit; if I, while not having fallen under the sway of madness or confusion, and while possessed of a mind that knows benefit from harm, nevertheless commit sin and non-virtue yet again, and lead myself, by my own doing, back to that same hell where I was born before — how do we know that this present life has come from hell? For example, just as a father, on setting out to sea, leaves a testament with his relatives concerning his young child, so too, when the Tathāgata was about to pass into nirvana, he gave this command to the noble Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī, and the others, with whom he had some slight connection to myself: "When the beings now dwelling in hell are one day freed from it and obtain a human body, bestow upon them this treasury of Dharma that I have gathered over three countless eons." Knowing this — if I lead myself back to that place again and bring it to ruin—

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

*(Gyaltsab gives no separate prose for verse 4-26 in this file; his explanation of 4-26 is combined with his explanation of 4-27 into one continuous passage that follows the 4-27 transclusion — the passage is headed "First" for 4-26's point and "Second" for 4-27's. The freedoms and endowments are extremely hard to find, and, once found, of great benefit. Having obtained this beneficial ground through sheer good fortune, if, while possessed of the wisdom that knows benefit from harm, I lead myself back to that place, hell, then it is as though I had been bewildered by a spell — I should reflect: "It amounts to my having no mind at all that seeks to accomplish benefit for myself here."

Second: if one asks by what one has been bewildered — I myself do not even know what has bewildered and deceived me; whatever cause of bewilderment lies within me, it must surely exist. Even when one falls under the sway of laziness, one should reflect in just this way.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*(Ngulchu Thokme transcludes verses 4-26 and 4-27 together as one quoted block and comments on both at once; the explanation below covers both. Having obtained this ground of leisure and endowment — extremely hard to find and able to accomplish benefit — through sheer good fortune, if I, while possessed of the intelligence that knows the faults of non-virtue and the merits of virtue, commit sin and lead myself back to hell again, then it is as though I had been bewildered by a spell — it amounts to my having no mind at all here. Third (examining its cause): as for what has bewildered and deceived me, I myself did not previously know; and if one examines what cause within me could bring this about, there is nothing fitting but the afflictions.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

*(Sabzang gives no separate prose for verse 4-26; his explanation of 4-26 is combined with 4-27's into one continuous passage that follows the 4-27 transclusion. The same passage is repeated on the 4-27 page.)*

Moreover, this very basis of leisure and endowment — extremely hard to find, and, being the ground or basis on which benefit and happiness are accomplished, comparable to a great field — however it may be, having obtained it through the good fortune of merit, if I, while possessed of the intelligence that examines and analyzes, once again accumulate sin and lead myself to that hell, then it is as though someone had been bewildered and rendered senseless by a mantra-spell — for me too, at this time, it amounts to being exactly like having no mind at all. As to how I have been bewildered and deluded, I myself did not know; and upon examining what agent within my own body causes this bewilderment, there is nothing but the afflictions — so it is fitting to strive at abandoning them.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

*(Minyak Kunzang Sonam gives no separate prose for verse 4-26; his explanation of 4-26 is combined with 4-27's into one continuous passage, including two scriptural citations, following the 4-27 transclusion. The same passage is repeated on the 4-27 page.)*

This special body of leisure and endowment — extremely hard to find, and, once found, the ground and basis for accomplishing all benefit and happiness, both immediate and ultimate, for oneself and others — is comparable to a fine field that is the ground for the growth of crops and the like. However it may be — that is, obtained now through the good fortune of previously accumulated virtuous merit — if I, even while possessed of the discerning wisdom, arisen from learning and the like, that knows precisely the causes of benefit and harm such as virtue and sin, nevertheless abandon what is fit to be done, accumulate much unwholesome karma of what is unfit to be done, turn back, and lead myself once again to that same fearful place — the hells and so forth — where I was tormented by suffering for so long before, without being freed—it would be just like someone who, having been bewildered by a spell and led into fire, does not even recognize what is happening; likewise, if I too, right now, being reduced to a state as though mindless, like a madman or a corpse, incapable of discernment — for if I truly had no mind at all, it would follow that there is nothing at all in me, so how could it be fitting to lead myself there? This shows that, having wandered from beginningless time through the many narrow straits of the unfree states such as the hells, experiencing much suffering for a long time without being freed, and now, through the strength of virtuous merit, having gained even this brief span of freedom — if I squander it meaninglessly and turn back again to those unbearable realms, then it is exactly as if I had been bewildered by the power of a spell: I should reflect, "I have become one without the very mind that seeks my own benefit," and, relying on this excellent basis, I must strive on the path to liberation in order to turn back rebirth in the ever-continuing cycle of birth and death. As the *Letter to a Friend* says:

> "Having found leisure free from the eight faults of the unfree states, strive to turn back rebirth."

And Āryaśūra also says:

> "Whoever has obtained this — the seed of the transcendent perfections beyond saṃsāra, the supreme seed of the glory of awakening, surpassing even a wish-fulfilling jewel — among such people of continuous good qualities, who here would let it bear no fruit?"

So it is said. Now, one may ask: by what spell or the like has one been bewildered? As to what cause has bewildered me and made me deceive myself, I myself did not previously know; but since there must surely be some cause within me that causes this self-bewilderment, it is fitting to investigate it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^4-26]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

*(Khenpo Kunga gives no separate prose for verse 4-26; his explanation of 4-26 is combined with 4-27's into one continuous passage that follows the 4-27 transclusion. The same passage is repeated on the 4-27 page.)*

This present Dharma-endowed human body is, by way of causes, examples, and numbers, extremely hard to find; and having found it, it is like a ground that is of tremendous benefit to oneself and others — a basis for perfectly pure ethical discipline and the like. However this basis was obtained — whether by good fortune, through favorable interdependence, or through the blessing of the buddhas — if I, even while possessed of the wisdom that discerns good from bad, still fall under the power of laziness, distraction, and carelessness, engage in unwholesome courses of action, and thereby lead myself back once again to that very place, hell, then it is comparable to being bewildered by a mantra-spell: just as one bewitched by a spell of enchantment loses control over his own thinking at that time, so too, in this present moment, or with respect to the true purpose of this life, I am like one who has no mind at all — this, he says, amounts to having no mind whatsoever.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden — a father's testament before a sea-voyage; the Buddha's entrustment of the Dharma to Avalokiteśvara and Mañjuśrī

Just as a father, on setting out to sea, leaves a testament with his relatives concerning his young child, so too, when the Tathāgata was about to pass into nirvana, he gave this command to the noble Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī, and the others, with whom he had some slight connection: "When the beings now dwelling in hell are one day freed from it and obtain a human body, bestow upon them this treasury of Dharma that I have gathered over three countless eons." Khenpo Kunzang Pelden adduces this to teach: since it is known that beings who are freed from hell and reborn as humans will obtain the treasury of Dharma set aside for them beforehand by the Buddha, it is certain that those who obtain a human body were previously freed from hell. Therefore, he says, it is utterly unfitting for one now to destroy this present human body through sin and lead oneself back to hell.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Leading Oneself Back to Hell Through Sin, Even After Obtaining the Extremely Rare Freedoms and Endowments, Is Utterly Unfitting

All the commentaries agree in explaining that, having obtained this ground of leisure and endowment through sheer good fortune, if one still commits sin while possessed of the wisdom that discerns benefit from harm and leads oneself back to hell, it amounts to having no mind at all.

###### 2. Khenpo Kunzang Pelden's Account of the Buddha Entrusting the Dharma Establishes That Those Who Obtain a Human Body Were Previously Freed From Hell

He adduces the command the Tathāgata gave to Avalokiteśvara and Mañjuśrī at the time of his passing into nirvana, to explain that it is utterly unfitting to destroy this human body again through sin.

###### 3. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Counsels Against Wasting the Freedoms and Endowments, Citing the Letter to a Friend and Āryaśūra's Verse

He adduces scripture to show that this basis of leisure and endowment can accomplish all benefit and happiness for oneself and others, and that one must therefore strive on the path of liberation.

###### 4. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama Both Identify Falling Under the Sway of Laziness and Carelessness as the Cause of Being Led Back to Hell

They explain that even while possessed of the wisdom to discern good from bad, engaging in unwholesome courses of action through the power of laziness, distraction, and carelessness leads one back to hell.

###### 5. Ngulchu Thogme Places This Passage Within the Section Examining the Cause of Such Bewilderment

He concludes, continuing from the passage above, that upon examining what could cause such squandering of the freedoms and endowments, there is nothing but the afflictions.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse teaches that, having obtained the extremely rare basis of leisure and endowment through sheer good fortune, if one still commits sin while possessed of the wisdom to discern benefit from harm and leads oneself back to hell, it amounts to having no mind at all. All the commentaries agree on this basic ground.

**Key points.**

- Even having obtained this rare ground, if one commits sin while knowing benefit from harm and leads oneself back to hell, one becomes, like someone bewildered by a spell, as though mindless.
- Khenpo Kunzang Pelden's account of the Buddha entrusting the Dharma to Avalokiteśvara and Mañjuśrī establishes that those who obtain a human body were previously freed from hell.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and His Holiness the Dalai Lama both explain that falling under the sway of laziness, distraction, and carelessness is what leads one back to hell.
- Minyak Kunzang Sonam adduces the Letter to a Friend and Āryaśūra's verse to counsel that this rare basis must not be wasted, but applied to the path of liberation.
- Ngulchu Thogme concludes that, upon examining the cause of such squandering, there is nothing but the afflictions — pointing toward their abandonment.
