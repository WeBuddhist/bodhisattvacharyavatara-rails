## Today's Verse

> Death is not something we can trust or predict. It does not wait until our work is finished. Whether we are healthy or sick, we can never be sure of tomorrow. ^2-33

> I never truly understood that I would have to leave everything behind one day. Because of that, I have done all kinds of wrong — for people I love, and against people I dislike. ^2-34

> The people I dislike will be gone one day. The people I love will be gone too. I myself will be gone. Everything, in the end, disappears. ^2-35

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on verses 33 through 35 from the second chapter of the _Bodhicaryāvatāra_. We live as though this life will hold, and we spend our days pushing away the people we count as enemies and holding tight to the ones we count as friends. But death does not wait for our work to be finished, and when it comes we leave friends, enemies, and everything we own behind and go on alone. These verses are a sharp reminder of that, and an encouragement to set down attachment and aversion that lead nowhere and take up something that is actually worth the day.

## 2) Commentary Explanation

The commentaries tell a story here. A householder named Gautama in central India had a young son, Netso. The boy watched some people take a beehive. The bees that had been gathering the honey were killed, the rest fled, and the hive was simply gone. He said to himself that his father's household and all its wealth was no different from a broken hive. When his father asked what was wrong, the boy answered that the body is like dew, about to fall, and the breath is like mist. The two of them gave away everything they had and turned to practice.

That story is today's three verses in one picture. Death does not check whether our business is finished. When it comes we leave like a hair drawn out of butter: the hair comes out clean and nothing that surrounded it comes with it. Land, house, savings, the people we love and the people we cannot stand, and finally our own body — all of it stays where it is.

And this is what Shantideva is confessing. The friend ends, the enemy ends, we end. Whatever we did wrong for either of them was pointless from the start.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, when anger rises toward someone, I will remember that both he and I will die, and let the hatred go.

**Explanation:** _(Taming the mind)_ Because we quietly assume we will be here indefinitely, we treat our dislikes as permanent business and spend real energy on them, gathering harm along the way. Today I will use these verses as a check. When someone irritates me and I feel the reaction building, I will remember one plain fact: he will die and so will I, and this particular argument will not survive either of us. That thought is not meant to make the day feel grim. It shrinks the grievance to its real size, so it stops governing me and I can take charge of my own mind instead.
