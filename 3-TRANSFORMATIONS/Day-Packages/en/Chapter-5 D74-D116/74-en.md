# Day 74 — The Elephant of the Mind: Bound by the Rope of Mindfulness

## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the first three verses of the fifth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*. Let us think about why watching over our own mind is the root of our happiness, day by day.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us a comparison that is easy to understand and encouraging.

In an ancient Indian battle, an elephant was made wild with liquor. A wheel of weapons was tied to the tip of its trunk, and it was sent into battle. It could destroy an enemy's city and kill many people. This caused great harm. Yet even that elephant cannot do what an untamed mind, ruled by the afflictions, can do. It cannot burn people alive in the lowest hell and cause every kind of harm found there. So we must work hard to guard our own mind.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will bind my own mind firmly to the side of doing good with the rope of mindfulness, and I will not let it wander.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* If we let our own mind wander freely, like an elephant let loose, much wrongdoing and suffering will follow. So if we tie our own mind to doing good with the rope of mindfulness, every fear will disappear and every good action will come about naturally.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

For one who wishes to guard the training of generating bodhicitta without impairment, one must strive with the utmost effort to guard the mind entirely from becoming attached to the enjoyment of objects. Why is this? Because if the mind is not guarded from wavering toward its objects, there is no way to guard the training so that it remains unimpaired.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: those who wish to keep the bodhisattva trainings must guard the mind through great and diligent effort. If the mind is not guarded, the trainings cannot be kept.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-1]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the chapter on examining, again and again, the condition of body and mind — that is, the chapter on introspection. It has four parts: guarding the mind for the sake of guarding the trainings; the manner of relying on mindfulness and introspection for the sake of guarding the mind; the manner of training in the conduct of mind-training through mindfulness and introspection; and teaching the other factors of a perfect practice. This verse belongs to the first of these. What is especially important to understand here is that the essence of mindfulness is not forgetting the points of what is to be adopted and rejected, and the essence of introspection is examining one's conduct of body, speech, and mind again and again in every respect, so as to engage what is to be adopted and rejected with full awareness. For this, one must first rely on a genuine teacher, train through hearing in the points of what is to be adopted and rejected, become learned in them, and thereby come to know clearly what is to be adopted and what is to be rejected. He notes that nowadays many novice monks holding vows, and others who fancy themselves tantric practitioners and are regarded by others as excellent, do not even recognize what counts as a downfall requiring confession or a root downfall.

So, having explained that one must know well and not forget the points of what is to be adopted and rejected, examine them properly, and engage them by guarding the mind, he cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*: "Who but one's own teacher, or one like him, knows one's own hidden faults?" — showing that others cannot recognize the bad thoughts in one's own mind and so cannot set one on the path in one's place. Since the mind is like a wild horse, mindfulness like its tether, and introspection like the watchman who tends it, he explains that unless the mind is guarded by mindfulness and introspection, the trainings cannot be guarded. Therefore, one who wishes to keep the three precious trainings of the higher path properly guarded must strive with mindfulness and introspection, without letting the mind fall under the sway of distraction, and make effort to guard this mind of one's own, single-pointedly, from going astray; for unless the mind is guarded from the afflictions through mindfulness and introspection, there is absolutely no way that the trainings can be kept unimpaired. He explains, therefore, that merely being afraid of kings and so forth and, for a time, confining body and speech like a prisoner while shouldering the burden of external vows and restraints, does not bring one to the final goal — so guarding this mind itself is what truly matters.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-1]]

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

One who wishes to keep the bodhisattva's three trainings in pure ethical discipline must, through mindfulness and introspection, strive with the utmost effort and make effort also to guard this mind of one's own, single-pointedly; for unless this mind is guarded from the afflictions, it will engage in non-virtue, and there is absolutely no way the trainings can be guarded.

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

For a person who wishes to keep the trainings that bodhisattvas must uphold — the six perfections and the rest — entirely free from impairment by unfavorable factors, one must rely on the means of guarding them and strive with the utmost effort to guard, single-pointedly, this very mind of one's own, which otherwise wanders and strays wildly toward mistaken objects. For if this mind — unstable and moving like lightning or ocean waves — is not properly guarded and brought under control, it will engage in wrongdoing, and there is absolutely no way whatsoever that the trainings can be kept in this life. Here, "guarding the trainings" is explained in the great commentary as meaning that those who have generated bodhicitta and taken up the vows do what is permitted and refrain from what is prohibited.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-1]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Wishing to guard the training" means: whether one takes this as the three trainings — of ethical discipline, of concentration, and of wisdom — or, equally, as the trainings of the bodhisattva vow of bodhicitta, which are of one nature with these: the ethical discipline of restraint from wrongdoing, the ethical discipline of gathering virtuous qualities, and the ethical discipline of acting for the welfare of sentient beings — a person who wishes to guard such trainings as these must, through mindfulness and introspection, strive with the utmost effort to guard this very mind of one's own from being scattered by the afflictions. This is the meaning: unless this very mind of one's own is guarded from afflicted thoughts, there is no way whatsoever that these trainings can be kept.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-1]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Guarding the Mind Is the Foundation for Guarding the Trainings

All the commentaries agree: unless the mind's wavering toward mistaken objects is guarded, no bodhisattva training whatsoever can be kept without impairment.

###### 2. This Verse Opens the Chapter on Introspection by Teaching the Means of Examining Body and Mind Again and Again

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that this chapter has four parts — guarding the mind for the sake of guarding the trainings, relying on mindfulness and introspection to guard the mind, training in the conduct of mind-training through mindfulness and introspection, and teaching the other factors of a perfect practice — and that this verse belongs to the first.

###### 3. The Chief Means for Guarding the Mind Are Mindfulness and Introspection

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains mindfulness as not forgetting the points of what is to be adopted and rejected, and introspection as examining one's conduct through the three doors again and again; Sabzang Mati Panchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk likewise explain guarding the mind through mindfulness and introspection.

###### 4. The Mind Is Naturally Unstable and Wavers Toward Mistaken Objects, Which Is Why It Must Be Guarded

Minyak Kunzang Sonam explains that the mind is unstable and wavering, like lightning, clouds, wind, and ocean waves, citing passages from the *Letter to a Friend* and the *Questions of Subāhu*.

###### 5. "The Training" Means the Three Ethical Disciplines, of One Nature with the Bodhicitta Vow

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk identifies the training as the three ethical disciplines — restraint from wrongdoing, gathering virtue, and acting for the welfare of beings — or, equally, as the training of the bodhisattva vow of bodhicitta.

###### 6. Guarding Body and Speech Alone, Without Guarding the Mind, Does Not Bring One to the Final Goal

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains that merely being afraid of kings and the like, and for a time keeping body and speech confined like a prisoner under vows and restraints, does not bring one to the final result — showing that guarding the mind itself is what matters most.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** Those who wish to keep the bodhisattva trainings unimpaired must, through great effort, guard this mind of theirs from afflicted thoughts and from wavering toward mistaken objects; for unless the mind is guarded, there is no way whatsoever that any training can be kept — a point on which all the commentators agree.

**Key points.**

- **Guarding the mind is the foundation for guarding the trainings.** If the mind is not guarded from the afflictions and from wavering toward mistaken objects, there is absolutely no way to keep any bodhisattva training unimpaired.
- **The chief means of guarding the mind.** One must guard the mind single-pointedly by relying on mindfulness, which does not forget the points of what is to be adopted and rejected, and introspection, which examines one's conduct through the three doors again and again.
- ⚑ **Identifying what is guarded as "the training."** Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam explain it in terms of the six perfections and similar trainings against unfavorable factors; Khenpo Kunzang Pelden explains it as the three precious trainings of the higher path; Sabzang Mati Panchen and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk identify it as the three ethical disciplines (restraint from wrongdoing, gathering virtue, and benefiting sentient beings) together with the training of the bodhicitta vow.
- **The mind's wavering and the metaphor for guarding it.** The mind is unstable and wavering like lightning, clouds, wind, and ocean waves — like a wild horse — and if it is not guarded by the rope of mindfulness and the watchman of introspection, it will fall into wrongdoing.
- **Guarding body and speech alone is not enough.** Merely being afraid of kings and the like, and for a time keeping body and speech confined like a prisoner under vows and restraints, does not bring one to the final goal.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: if the mind, like an elephant, is let loose to do whatever it pleases, it inflicts the great harm of falling into the Avīci hell — a harm that not even a mad, untamed elephant could inflict. He applies this in the context of how the elephant of the mind must be tamed.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

This is the first passage teaching that all harm arises from the mind. When the elephant of one's own mind is let loose — following after the habitual tendencies of the past, running out to meet the thoughts of the future, and being cast toward whatever condition presents itself in the present, wild and unrestrained — it accumulates bad karma, and in future lives inflicts the fierce suffering of being roasted and burned in the Avīci hell and other such states. By contrast, even a wild, liquor-maddened battle-elephant of ancient India, though it may wreak great destruction by demolishing an enemy's city, does not inflict here in the human world any harm as great as being roasted in the Avīci hell and the like. He therefore teaches that one must make effort to guard the mind.

##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Just as the elephant of the mind, let loose toward mistaken objects, inflicts the harm of the Avīci hell, whereas an ordinary untamed and intoxicated elephant does not inflict such harm in this world, he explains that one must make effort to guard the mind.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->

He explains that the elephant of the mind, let loose toward mistaken objects, inflicts the harm of the Avīci hell, whereas an untamed and intoxicated elephant does not inflict such harm here; and he explains that the second topic — the benefits of guarding the mind — divides into a brief statement and a detailed explanation.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The elephant of the mind, maddened by afflictions and by improper attention, when let loose toward diverse objects, accumulates bad karma and inflicts the fierce suffering of the Avīci hell and the like — whereas an ordinary elephant, untamed and intoxicated with pride, does not inflict such harm here. He explains that it is therefore fitting to make effort to guard the mind.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-2]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The elephant of the mind, which roams wherever it pleases and is maddened by attachment and other afflictions, when not brought under the control of mindfulness's hook and let loose freely toward mistaken objects, engages in various kinds of wrongdoing and, later, gives rise to great suffering in the Avīci hell. By contrast, an ordinary untamed and intoxicated elephant does not inflict such great harm, whether in this visible world or in worlds unseen. On this basis he counsels that the mind must be guarded without impairment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-2]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

This very mind of one's own is like an untamed elephant let loose. Following after the objects of past, present, and future, running out to meet them, and let loose freely, it accumulates bad karma such as killing, and later inflicts the harm of being burned in the fires of hell. By contrast, even an untamed, liquor-maddened war-elephant with a blade lashed to its trunk-tip, though it may wreak such great destruction as demolishing a city and killing people, does not, in this human world, inflict harm of every kind equal to being roasted in hell.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All Harm Arises from the Mind; Letting the Elephant of the Mind Run Loose Is the Cause of the Harm of the Avīci Hell and the Like

All the commentaries agree: if the mind is let loose toward mistaken objects, it produces the fierce suffering of hell in future lives.

###### 2. Even a Mad, Untamed Elephant Cannot Inflict the Harm That an Unguarded Mind Inflicts

Whatever harm an ordinary untamed and intoxicated elephant may do in this life, all agree that it is surpassed by the harm of hell brought about when the mind is left unguarded toward mistaken objects.

###### 3. Letting the Elephant of the Mind Run After Past, Present, and Future Objects Is the Cause of Accumulating Bad Karma

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain that when the mind follows the habitual tendencies of the past and runs out to meet the thoughts of the future, letting itself be led by present conditions, it accumulates such bad karma as killing.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** All harm arises from the mind, so one must make effort to guard it: when the elephant of the mind is let loose toward mistaken objects, it causes one to suffer the fierce agony of being roasted in the Avīci hell in future lives — a harm that not even an ordinary, untamed, and intoxicated elephant could inflict in this world. All the commentators agree on this.

**Key points.**

- **All harm arises from the mind.** Letting the elephant of the mind run loose is the chief cause of the harm of the Avīci hell and the like.
- **The elephant of the mind runs after past, present, and future objects and is let loose freely.** This accumulates bad karma and later inflicts the harm of being burned in the fires of hell.
- **The metaphor of the mad war-elephant.** Though it may wreak great destruction, such as demolishing an enemy's city and killing people, it cannot inflict harm equal to being roasted in the Avīci hell in this human world.
- **Without mindfulness and introspection to hold it, the mind maddened by the afflictions is let loose freely.** This leads it into various kinds of wrongdoing and gives rise, in future lives, to great suffering.

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^5-3`):*

> If the elephant of the mind is firmly tethered by the rope of mindfulness on every side, all fears vanish and every virtue comes within reach.

---

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

If, by the rope of mindfulness that does not forget the points of what is to be adopted and rejected in every gateway, the elephant of the mind is tightly bound to a virtuous object, then all fears — such as fierce beasts, bad company, and the like — will vanish entirely, and every virtue that serves as the cause of higher rebirth and of the definite goodness of liberation will come to hand.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^5-3]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains: if one has no control over the direction one's thinking takes, dissatisfaction with desirable objects gives rise to all manner of discursive thought, and if the elephant of the mind is left under the sway of attachment and aversion, both oneself and others come to ruin. He explains that one must first learn to distinguish what is fitting from what is not, and, having grasped this through mindfulness, then, the very moment the elephant of the mind heads toward what is unfitting, one must immediately turn the reins toward what is fitting by means of the rope of mindfulness, just as one reins in a horse with a bridle. He explains that if the elephant of the mind is bound firmly by the rope of mindfulness, the fears that arise from an untamed mind will be pacified, and the virtues that arise from a tamed mind will come to hand naturally.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

By the rope of mindfulness — which, through all the gateways of body, speech, and mind, does not forget the points of what is to be adopted and rejected — if this elephant of the mind, maddened with desire, is tied firmly to the post of proper mental engagement, neither pursuing the traces of the past, nor running to meet the future, nor letting present awareness fall under the sway of conditions, then all fears in this life and beyond will vanish entirely, and every virtue beneficial to oneself and others will come to hand effortlessly, as if placed there directly. So he explains.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

By the rope of mindfulness, through all three doors, if the elephant of the mind is tied firmly to a virtuous object, all fears in this life and beyond will vanish entirely, and every virtue belonging to the graduated path of the three types of persons will come to hand. He counsels that mindfulness toward a virtuous object must never be allowed to lapse.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

By the rope of all-encompassing mindfulness, if the elephant of the mind is tied firmly to a virtuous object, all fears will vanish entirely and every virtue will come to hand — so he explains, and the detailed explanation divides into abandoning fear and accomplishing virtue.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

By the rope of mindfulness — clear and all-encompassing, not forgetting the points of what is to be adopted and rejected — if this maddened elephant of the mind is tied firmly to the post of proper mental engagement, all fears that would harm one in this life and beyond will vanish entirely, and every virtue that benefits oneself and others will come to hand as if placed there directly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

By the rope of mindfulness — clear in every respect, not forgetting the points of what is to be adopted and rejected — if this elephant of the mind, drunk and swollen with the intoxicating liquor of the afflictions, is stopped from engaging mistaken objects and bound firmly to the great, sturdy post of proper mental engagement, then all fears of harm in this life and the next will vanish entirely, and every virtue that is the cause of higher rebirth and definite goodness will come to hand effortlessly, as if placed there directly. Citing the sūtra passage "A tamed mind is virtuous; a tamed mind brings happiness," he counsels that one must rely on mindfulness and introspection at all times, in every respect, to guard the mind without impairment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

By the rope of mindfulness — which, through the doors of body, speech, and mind, never forgets in any respect the points of what is to be adopted, namely virtue, and rejected, namely non-virtue — if this elephant of the mind, maddened by desirable objects, is bound firmly to the post of proper mental engagement on impermanence, suffering, emptiness and selflessness, and the peace and bliss of nirvāṇa, then all fears and sufferings in this life and future lives will vanish entirely, and every virtue beneficial to oneself and others will come to hand, bringing one's purpose to its final accomplishment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^5-3]]

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Binding the Elephant of the Mind to a Virtuous Object with the Rope of All-Encompassing Mindfulness Makes All Fears Vanish and Brings Every Virtue to Hand

All the commentaries agree that binding the mind with the rope of mindfulness averts danger and accomplishes good qualities.

###### 2. The Essence of the Rope of Mindfulness Is Not Forgetting, Through All Three Doors, the Points of What Is to Be Adopted and Rejected

Khenpo Kunzang Pelden and Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk both explain mindfulness as neither pursuing the traces of the past nor running to meet the future.

###### 3. The Object to Which the Elephant of the Mind Is Bound Is Proper Mental Engagement with a Virtuous Focus

Some commentators explain this as the post of proper mental engagement with the fourfold contemplation of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness.

###### 4. If the Elephant of the Mind Is Left Under the Sway of Attachment and Aversion, Both Oneself and Others Come to Ruin, So the Mind Must Be Reined In

His Holiness the Dalai Lama makes this vivid through the example of the discursive thoughts born of dissatisfaction with desirable objects.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** By the rope of mindfulness — which, through the doors of body, speech, and mind, does not forget the points of what is to be adopted and rejected — if this elephant of the mind, maddened by the afflictions and by desirable objects, is bound firmly to a focus of proper mental engagement, then all fears in this life and beyond will vanish entirely, and every virtue beneficial to oneself and others will come to hand effortlessly. All the commentators explain this with one voice.

**Key points.**

- **The essence of the rope of mindfulness.** Through all three doors, not forgetting in any respect the points of what is to be adopted, namely virtue, and rejected, namely non-virtue.
- **The object to which the elephant of the mind is bound.** Proper mental engagement with impermanence, suffering, emptiness and selflessness, and the like.
- **The metaphor of reining in a horse with a bridle.** The moment the elephant of the mind heads toward what is unfitting, the rope of mindfulness must immediately turn it toward what is fitting.
- ⚑ **A difference in how the resulting virtue is identified.** Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and Minyak Kunzang Sonam speak of "virtue that is the cause of higher rebirth and of definite goodness," while Gyaltsab Je speaks of "the virtue of the graduated path of the three types of persons."
- **Scriptural confirmation.** As the sūtra states, "a tamed mind is virtuous; a tamed mind brings happiness" — a tamed mind pacifies all fear and brings happiness.
