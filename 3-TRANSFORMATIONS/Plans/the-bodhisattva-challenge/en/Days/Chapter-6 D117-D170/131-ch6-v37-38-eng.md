## Today's Verse

> When defilements overpower them,
> they even kill themselves.
> How can they then
> avoid harming others? ^6-37

> If compassion does not arise for those
> who, driven by defilements,
> engage in self-harm and such,
> what is the point of being angry? ^6-38

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on the thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth verses of the sixth chapter. The person who harms us is bound by hatred and desire, and even harms themselves. Then does anger arise in our mind, or does our mind become gentle?

## 2) Commentary Explanation

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable example, which is easy to understand and encouraging.

A person seized by a spirit loses control. They may even harm someone who is trying to help them. But a doctor does not get angry at that person. The doctor looks at the spirit. In the same way, when anger, desire and other afflictions seize a person's mind, the person has no control. They harm even themselves. So the Buddha does not look at that person. The Buddha looks at the afflictions. So these people are not objects of anger. They are objects of compassion. A bodhisattva wishes that they be free from the spirit of the afflictions, and loves them even more.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, when other people show me anger, I will not get angry. I will meditate on compassion.

**Explanation:** _(Doing good)_ When people fall under the power of the afflictions, they lose control and harm themselves and others. A doctor does not get angry at a person seized by a spirit. In the same way, we must not get angry at the person. We must look at the afflictions. We must let compassion arise for those who harm us, and wish that they be free from the afflictions.
