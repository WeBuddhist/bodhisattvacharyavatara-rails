## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the fiftieth and fifty-first verses of the fifth chapter. When we hear praise, we feel happy. Even if pride arises, a piece of wood stays still.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following key point, which is easy to understand and encouraging.

When others praise us, we may become too happy. The wish to hear still more may arise, and pride may arise. Then we must not keep listening. We must not act with body or speech. We must remain like wood.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, if the thought of praising myself or criticizing others arises in me, I will stay still like a piece of wood and say nothing.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* When praising ourselves or criticizing others comes up, we must not act with body or speech. We must remain like wood. When we are praised, the wish to listen and pride may arise. Not listening and not speaking at that moment is a way to tame the mind.

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

He explains that on occasions such as these one should not act and should not speak, but should remain like wood: when one is highly distracted and restless; when one wishes to engage in mockery (ག་ཞར་བཅས་པ), something like provoking laughter and giggling; when one teases and needles others; when pride goes seeking out others' faults; and when, with "renewed contention" (སྐྱོར་འབྱིན), one speaks crooked words unfit to be said to others and behaves in an unfitting, improper way. Again, whenever one is eager to praise oneself — that is, when others praise one and one becomes so delighted that one wants to hear it still more, becomes attached to it, and pride arises — if that is happening, one should not listen to it, and should not act or speak, but remain like wood. And when one sees oneself agitated, disparaging others, scolding in a way that disparages others' words, and caught up in wrangling and quarrel, one should not act, should not speak, but remain like wood — such is the meaning.

#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. All the Commentators Agree: Remain Like Wood Even in Self-Praise, Disparaging Others, Scolding, and Quarrelling

This verse sets out that even on occasions of self-praise, disparaging others, scolding, and quarrelling and contention, one must remain like wood; the commentaries are in agreement on this.

###### 2. Minyak Kunzang Sonam Cites the Precious Garland to Distinguish Trickery from Guile

Minyak Kunzang Sonam, quoting the *Jewel Garland* (ནོར་བུའི་ཕྲེང་བ — the name the rail's summary gives the work, which the commentary itself cites as the *Precious Garland*, རིན་ཕྲེང) — "Trickery is thorough deception; guile is a crooked mind-stream" — makes clear the difference between trickery and guile.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Divides the Verse into Gateways Ten to Thirteen of the Twenty-Seven

From among the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk divides this verse into four — self-praise, disparaging others, scolding, and quarrelling — and sets out their numbers as the tenth through the thirteenth.

###### 4. ⚑ The Root Verse's "Regret" and the Commentaries' "Quarrel" Are Not the Same Word

The root verse's word འགྱོད ("regret") and the word འགྱེད ("quarrel") quoted by all the commentators (Ngulchu Thogme, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sabzang Mati Panchen, Minyak Kunzang Sonam, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk, and His Holiness the Dalai Lama) are two different words.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

###### 1. Gateways Fourteen to Eighteen: Even in Desire for Gain, Honor, Fame, Attendants, and Personal Service, Remain Like Wood

This verse divides the fourteenth through the eighteenth of the twenty-seven gateways of misconduct into four (into five, if counted as Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk counts them) — gain, honor, fame, attendants, and the wish for personal service — and sets out that even on these occasions one must remain like wood.

###### 2. Sabzang Mati Panchen Sets Apart the Wish for Special Service Within the Desire for Personal Service

Sabzang Mati Panchen separately sets down the aspect of wishing for special forms of service within the meaning of the desire for personal service.

###### 3. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk Distinguishes Seeking Gain from Seeking Honor, and Singles Out the Personal Attendant

Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk separately distinguishes the thought of seeking gain, which depends on offerings, from the thought of seeking honor, which depends on prostration and the spreading of a seat; and, within the category of attendants, he further divides out and explains the "uncommon" personal attendants (ཐུན་མོང་མིན་པའི་རིམ་གྲོ་བ).

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** When an attachment arises that desires gain, honor, fame, attendants, or personal service for oneself, the commentaries agree in teaching that one should not engage in the conduct of body and speech, but should remain unmoving like wood.

**Key points.**

- When the thought of desiring gain, honor, fame, attendants, or personal service for oneself arises, all the commentators are in agreement that one should not engage in the conduct of body and speech, but should remain like wood.
- Sabzang Mati Panchen says that within the desire for personal service is also included the wish for special, exceptional care while being served.
- Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk divides this verse, within the twenty-seven gateways, into five points: wishing for gain through offerings; wishing for honor through prostrations and arranged seats; wishing for widespread fame; wishing for outer attendants; and wishing for inner personal attendants.
