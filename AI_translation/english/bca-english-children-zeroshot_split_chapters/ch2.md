## 2. Chapter Two: Saying Sorry for Wrongdoing ^2-0


To hold on to this precious mind of bodhicitta,
I make wonderful offerings
to the Buddhas, to their true teachings,
to the spotless Three Jewels, and to the children of the Buddhas — an ocean of good qualities. ^2-1


I offer every flower and every fruit there is,
every kind of healing medicine,
every jewel this world contains,
and every clear, lovely stretch of water. ^2-2


I offer mountains full of jewels,
peaceful, beautiful forests and quiet places,
trees decorated with flowers,
and branches bending low with delicious fruit. ^2-3


I offer the sweet smells found among gods and other beings,
incense, wishing-trees, and trees made of jewels,
crops that grow on their own without anyone planting them,
and every other beautiful thing worth offering. ^2-4


I offer lakes and pools covered in lotus flowers,
where swans call out in sweet, lovely voices,
and I offer everything unclaimed
that stretches out across the endless reaches of the sky. ^2-5


Holding all of this in my mind,
I offer it all to the greatest of sages and to his children.
Great and compassionate ones, worthy of every gift,
please think of me with love and accept these offerings. ^2-6


I have no good deeds stored up, and I am very poor;
I have no other riches to offer.
So please, protectors who care only for others,
accept this offering through your own great power, for my sake. ^2-7


To the Buddhas and their children I now
offer my whole body, forever.
Great and noble ones, please accept me completely.
With respect, I will serve you humbly. ^2-8


Since you have taken me under your care,
I can help other beings without any fear in this life.
I will go beyond the wrongs I did before,
and I will never do wrong again. ^2-9


Imagine a bathing room, sweet with perfume,
its crystal floor shining and clear,
with beautiful pillars glowing with jewels,
and a canopy sparkling with pearls hanging above. ^2-10


In a place like that, I bathe the Buddhas and their children
with many precious vases
filled with sweet-smelling water,
while songs and music play all around. ^2-11


Then I dry their bodies
with cloths that have no equal, clean and perfumed,
and I offer them
beautiful robes, dyed in lovely colors and full of sweet fragrance. ^2-12


With soft, fine, many-colored clothes
and hundreds of the finest ornaments,
I decorate the noble ones —
Samantabhadra, Mañjughoṣa, Avalokiteśvara, and all the rest. ^2-13


With the finest perfumes, whose scent
fills all the universe,
I anoint the bodies of every great sage,
shining and glowing like well-polished, pure gold. ^2-14


To the great sage, the finest of all who are worthy of offerings,
I offer beautiful flowers — mandārava, lotus,
blue lotus, and every other sweet-smelling flower,
arranged into lovely, well-made garlands. ^2-15


I also offer clouds of incense,
their wonderful, heart-stealing scent spreading everywhere,
and I offer feasts of food and drink,
every kind of delicious dish, fit for the gods. ^2-16


I offer rows of golden lotus flowers
set with precious lamps,
and on ground sprinkled with perfume
I scatter lovely flower petals. ^2-17


I offer vast palaces filled with beautiful songs of praise,
sparkling with hanging pearls and jewels,
palaces so wide and lovely
they decorate the boundless sky itself, to those whose very nature is compassion. ^2-18


I offer beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles,
their edges decorated all around,
raised up tall, lovely to look at and perfectly shaped —
I offer these always to every great sage. ^2-19


And beyond all this, may there be
clouds of every other offering too,
with sweet music and lovely songs,
enough to soothe the suffering of every being, spreading everywhere. ^2-20


May a rain of precious flowers
fall without ever stopping
on the true teachings, on the Three Jewels,
on every stupa and every sacred image. ^2-21


Just as Mañjughoṣa and others
made offerings to the Buddhas,
in the very same way I now offer
to the Tathāgatas, the protectors, and their children. ^2-22


To these oceans of good qualities
I sing praises, an ocean of beautiful words.
May clouds of sweet praise
rise up for them forever, without end. ^2-23


To all the Buddhas of past, present, and future,
to the true teachings, and to the wonderful community,
I bow down my body,
as many times as there are specks of dust in the world. ^2-24


I bow down before every place
where bodhicitta first arose,
and before every stupa.
I bow down before every teacher and abbot,
and before all who live by pure discipline. ^2-25


Until I reach full awakening,
I go for refuge to the Buddhas.
In the same way, I go for refuge
to the true teachings and to the community of bodhisattvas. ^2-26


To the fully awakened Buddhas
living in every direction,
and to the bodhisattvas
filled with great compassion,
I press my palms together and speak: ^2-27


Since beginningless time, wandering through countless lives,
in this life or in others,
without knowing better, I have done wrong,
or I have told others to do wrong for me, ^2-28


and, overcome by confusion and delusion,
I have even felt glad about wrongdoing that was done.
Now that I see how mistaken that was,
from the depths of my heart, I confess it all to my protectors. ^2-29


Whatever harm I have done, through my disturbed emotions,
with my body, my speech, or my mind,
to the Three Jewels,
to my parents, or to my teachers and elders, ^2-30


whatever wrongdoing, however terrible,
I, full of faults, have committed,
staining myself with so many mistakes —
I confess all of it to the guides of the world. ^2-31


But if death should come to me
before all this wrongdoing is cleared away,
please protect me quickly,
so that I may surely be freed from it. ^2-32


This Lord of Death cannot be trusted;
he does not wait for our work to be finished or not.
Whether we are sick or healthy,
none of us can be sure our life will last. ^2-33


I must leave everything behind and go,
but I did not understand that truth.
So, for the sake of those I love and those I dislike,
I have done all kinds of wrong. ^2-34


But those I dislike will one day be gone.
Those I love will one day be gone too.
I myself will be gone as well.
In the same way, everything will one day be gone. ^2-35


Just like something experienced in a dream,
whatever I have enjoyed in life
becomes only a memory afterward.
Once it has passed, I will never see it again. ^2-36


Even in this one short life,
many I loved and many I disliked have already passed away.
But the terrible wrongs I did for their sake
are still there, waiting ahead of me. ^2-37


I never truly understood
how quickly life passes,
and so, out of confusion, attachment, and anger,
I have done so many wrong things. ^2-38


Day and night, without pause,
this life is always slipping away,
and nothing is added to make up for it.
So how could someone like me not one day die? ^2-39


Even while lying on my bed,
surrounded by all my family and friends,
the feeling of my life slipping away
will be something I face completely alone. ^2-40


When death's messenger has hold of me,
what use are my relatives, what use are my friends?
At that time, only my own good deeds could protect me,
and yet I have not relied on them at all. ^2-41


Careless and unaware, I, without a protector,
never understood how frightening this truly is.
For the sake of this fleeting, impermanent life,
I have built up so much wrongdoing. ^2-42


When a person is being led away today
to a place where their limbs will be cut off,
they become terrified —
their mouth goes dry, their eyes grow wild and strange. ^2-43


So how much more frightening it must be
to be seized by death's terrifying messengers,
their faces full of dread,
and struck down by a great and terrible sickness — how could that not be overwhelming? ^2-44


"Who will truly protect me
from this great terror?"
With wide, frightened eyes,
I search in every direction for refuge. ^2-45


Seeing no refuge anywhere around me,
I sink into deep despair.
If there truly is no refuge to be found there,
what then will I do? ^2-46


That is why, from today onward,
I go for refuge to the Buddhas,
the protectors of the world, who work hard to save all beings,
who are powerful enough to clear away every fear. ^2-47


In the same way, I truly go for refuge
to the teachings they have realized in their hearts,
teachings that clear away the fear of endless rebirth,
and to the community of bodhisattvas as well. ^2-48


Trembling with fear,
I offer myself to Samantabhadra.
I offer my own body
to Mañjughoṣa as well. ^2-49


To the protector Avalokiteśvara,
whose compassionate actions never fail,
I cry out in a voice full of suffering:
"Please protect me, for I am full of wrongdoing." ^2-50


To noble Ākāśagarbha,
to Kṣitigarbha,
and to all the great compassionate protectors,
I cry out from my heart, seeking refuge. ^2-51


I go for refuge to Vajrapāṇi,
seeing whom, the terrifying
messengers of death and other hostile beings
flee in fear in every direction. ^2-52


Before, I ignored your teachings,
but now, seeing this great danger,
I come to you for refuge.
Please, quickly clear this fear away. ^2-53


If we are afraid even of an ordinary sickness,
we must follow a doctor's advice carefully —
so how much more must we do
when struck again and again by the hundred sicknesses of desire and the other disturbing emotions? ^2-54


Just one of these sicknesses alone
could destroy every person living in this world,
and no other medicine to cure it
can be found anywhere, in any direction. ^2-55


So not following the instructions
of the doctor who knows everything, who can remove every kind of pain —
that kind of thinking
is truly, deeply foolish, and deserves to be criticized. ^2-56


If I must be careful
even near a small, ordinary cliff,
how much more careful must I be
near a cliff a thousand miles deep, one I could fall from for a very long time! ^2-57


It is not right to sit here comfortably,
thinking, "At least I will not die today."
The time when I will cease to exist
will surely come, without any doubt. ^2-58


Who can promise me safety?
How can I be sure to escape this?
If it is certain that I cannot avoid it,
how can my mind possibly feel at ease? ^2-59


What do I have left now
from all the things I once enjoyed, which are already gone?
Yet I clung so tightly to them
that I went against the words of my teachers. ^2-60


In this life, and it will be the same in others too —
I must leave behind every relative and friend,
and travel on alone to who knows where.
So what does it matter, in the end, who I loved and who I disliked? ^2-61


"How can I be freed
from the suffering that comes from doing wrong?"
This alone is what I should think about,
day and night, always. ^2-62


Whatever wrong I have done, out of not knowing better and out of confusion —
whether it goes against nature itself
or against a rule I was taught —
whatever kind of wrongdoing it was, ^2-63


I confess it all now,
before the eyes of my protectors,
with my hands pressed together,
my heart full of fear of suffering, bowing again and again. ^2-64


I ask the guides of the world
to accept that my wrongdoing was truly wrong.
Since it was not good,
I will never do it again from now on. ^2-65


This is the end of the second chapter, called "Confessing Wrongdoing," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^2-a
