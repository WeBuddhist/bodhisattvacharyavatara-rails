## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:notification -->
### Notification

**Title:** After helping, notice what you hoped for  
**Detail:** The bodhisattva helps unasked, expecting nothing. Today, notice any wish for credit.

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

We rightly admire someone who returns a kindness. Today's verses point past that. The bodhisattva helps people who never did anything for them, who never even asked. And what they give is not a meal or a favor, but lasting happiness, offered to everyone, with respect, for as long as the world lasts.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These two verses show, in a way that is easy for ordinary people to understand, the wonderful way the bodhisattva works for others.

**First.** In this world, people praise someone even for simply repaying a kindness to a person who helped them first. So how much more should we praise the bodhisattva, who — without ever having been helped, and without anyone asking — is moved by natural, great compassion to bring good and happiness to all beings.

**Second.** Suppose someone gives just a little poor food to a few beings, over a short time and even with a scornful attitude, filling their stomachs for half a day. People will still honour them and say, "They have done a great good deed." So how much more should we honour the bodhisattva, who always gives the highest happiness to countless beings.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, I will step in to help someone with something they need before they even ask.

**Explanation:** Today's verse points out that returning a favour is a wonderful thing that deserves praise. However, a Bodhisattva is even more amazing because they help others naturally, without ever being asked. By looking out for someone and helping them before they request it today, you are practising this same pure and selfless generosity.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR_bo -->
##### BCACXX_WR_bo — Legkye's Story

This is the story given to illustrate the verse's phrase "whatever repayment of a favor there is." The youngest son of King Lhané ⚑ was a boy named Legkye ⚑, seven years old. When he and his father and mother — the three of them — were fleeing the murderous intent of a minister, their food ran out along the way, and the mother resolved to kill and eat her son. At that point Legkye himself offered his own flesh and blood, little by little until it was used up, to sustain his parents. He made the aspiration to attain buddhahood, the protector of all beings, whereupon the earth shook in six ways and even the gods wept with compassion. To test him, Śakra (Indra) emanated as a beggar and as a wild beast and took his flesh; yet Legkye had not the slightest regret, and by his words of truth his body was restored as before. Everyone praised him — "not clinging even to his own body: this is what true repayment of a favor is" — and he was raised, in the end, to the deeds of a king.

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **The point of doing good unasked.** The central reasoning of this verse is an argument "from the lesser to the greater" (how much more…): if merely repaying a kindness done earlier is, for now, praiseworthy in the world, then the bodhisattva — who, with no earlier help received and asked by no one, works by nature to bring about benefit and happiness in this life and the next for all beings — is established as far, far more praiseworthy. All seven commentaries agree on this reasoning.

2. **The point of being drawn by great compassion.** As both Gyaltsab and Minyak Kunzang explain clearly, the bodhisattva's excellent activity is not something merely occasional: they are drawn irresistibly by great compassion, so that working for beings' welfare arises naturally and effortlessly. Minyak Kunzang calls this "natural great compassion."

3. **The point of benefiting others with no hope of repayment or reward.** As both Sabzang and Minyak Kunzang explain clearly, the bodhisattva benefits beings without hoping for any repayment or ripened reward; this is what raises it far above ordinary virtue done for one's own sake. Minyak Kunzang also explains clearly that the bodhisattva looks upon all beings with an even eye, making no distinction between near and far.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse establishes that the bodhisattva is worthy of praise by gods and humans alike, using an argument "from the lesser to the greater" (how much more…). If, in the world, merely repaying a kindness done earlier is for now praiseworthy, then what need is there to speak of the greatness of the bodhisattva, who — though unasked — is moved by great compassion to accomplish, with no hope of repayment and looking with an even eye on all beings, nothing but the benefit and happiness of this life and the next for everyone?

**Key points.**
- Setting aside the worldly custom of praising those who repay favors, the unasked bodhisattva is established as far, far more praiseworthy.
- Acting excellently though unasked is not something adventitious — it arises spontaneously from natural great compassion.
- Without hoping for repayment or ripened reward, the bodhisattva looks upon all beings with an even eye, near and far alike, and benefits them.

#### Main Teaching Points

1. **The point of the lowly donor as example.** Verse 1-32 forms the smaller half of an argument "from the lesser to the greater" that runs across verses 1-32 and 1-33. Whatever donor gives to just a few beings (a small object), for just a moment (a short time), only poor food (a lowly thing), grudgingly and with scorn, filling them merely for half a day — even such a person is praised and honored by the world as "someone who does great virtue." Both KKP and NKW explain this in fine detail.

2. **The three-fold contrast (object / time / thing).** All the commentaries explain that this verse marks out three distinguishing features of the lowly donor across the axes of object, time, and thing: in terms of object, focusing on just a few beings; in terms of time, a very short duration; and in terms of thing, only a lowly gift. Each of these three features is set up to serve as the opposite of the bodhisattva's giving in verse 1-33 — as laid out in the parallel explanations of GDR, NTS, SMPLG, and MKS.

3. **The concluding instruction (GDR).** At the close of his explanation, Gyaltsab gives a direct instruction: "Anyone with sense should devote themselves single-pointedly to the bodhisattva." This is the one direct practice-instruction that emerges plainly from comparing these commentaries side by side; it gives this pair of verses their immediate power to inspire.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse forms the small, lower half of the "how much more" argument spanning verses 1-32 and 1-33. Even a donor who benefits a few beings for a short time — with a lowly gift, given with scorn, merely filling them for half a day — is worthy of the world's honor; and this, the commentaries explain, is no exaggeration.

**Key points.**
- A few beings / a short time / a lowly thing / given with scorn / filling for merely half a day — even a donor lacking on all five counts is honored by the world.
- GDR, NTS, SMPLG, and MKS set up 1-33's bodhisattva-donor — who gives the unsurpassed bliss of a Sugata — as the surpassing counterpart at the matching juncture.
- As the culmination of his instruction, Gyaltsab says: one should devote oneself single-pointedly to the bodhisattva.
