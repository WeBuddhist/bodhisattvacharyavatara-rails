# Day 12 — Why the Sage warned about anger toward a bodhisattva

## Opening

Verse 34 is the first cautionary note in Chapter 1. After several verses praising the awakening mind, Shantideva delivers a warning: harboring anger toward someone who holds bodhicitta carries consequences proportional to the duration of that hostile thought. Understanding why requires one piece of context the verse itself does not give.

## From the Tradition

When you feel quiet contempt for someone who seems earnestly devout — the person who is always volunteering, who talks about compassion, whose sincerity seems like a bit much — that dismissal feels like a small thing. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, a close disciple of Tsongkhapa, explains why verse 34's warning is not an exaggeration. He traces what a bodhisattva actually is: the direct cause of every Buddha. Since all Buddhas arise from bodhisattva practice, harming or wishing harm toward one is harming the source of the Three Jewels themselves. He calls this hostility "the ultimate of negativity" and says practitioners must be extremely careful at exactly that moment of dismissal. The severity of the consequence is not arbitrary. It follows the logic of cause and effect carried to its source.

## Today's Practice Challenge

Think of someone you know who is genuinely trying to practice or help others. Notice whether any trace of quiet dismissal arises when you picture them — mild skepticism, slight irritation, the feeling that their sincerity is a bit much. That mental motion is exactly what verse 34 is pointing at. Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen says to be extremely careful at that moment. You do not need to manufacture admiration. Just catch the dismissal before it settles, and let it go.
