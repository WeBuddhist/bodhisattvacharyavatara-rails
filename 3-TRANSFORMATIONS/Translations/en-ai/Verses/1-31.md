## Combined Commentary Summaries
The three classical Tibetan commentators—Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme—express complete harmony in their interpretation of Verse 1-31. They explain this verse as a profound praise of the Bodhisattva (Hero of Enlightenment) as the supreme, unsolicited benefactor of all wandering beings. By contrasting the ordinary worldly standard of gratitude with the limitless, unprompted altruism of the Bodhisattva, the commentators demonstrate why the heirs of the Buddha are infinitely worthy of praise by both gods and humans.

### 1. The Worldly Standard of Gratitude vs. The Bodhisattva's Altruism
All three commentators structure their explanations around a comparison between worldly reciprocity and the Bodhisattva's spontaneous generosity.
*   **The Worldly Standard:** In ordinary society, reciprocating a kindness is highly valued. **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** note that someone in the world who merely repays a past favor is praised as a "grateful person." **Sazang Mati Panchen** similarly explains that returning a benefit to a prior benefactor is respected in the world as "doing what is proper."
*   **The Contrast:** The commentators argue a fortiori (*argumentum a fortiori*): if a person receives praise for simply returning a favor that they owed, then the Bodhisattvas are infinitely more praiseworthy. This is because they act proactively, helping beings from whom they have received no prior favor and who have not even asked for assistance.

### 2. Key Attributes of the Bodhisattva's Unsolicited Actions
The commentators highlight several defining qualities that elevate the Bodhisattva's actions far above any worldly standard of goodness:
*   **Unsolicited Nature (*avyāpārita* / *མ་བཅོལ་བ*):** All three commentators emphasize that the Bodhisattva acts "without being asked" or "unsolicited." They do not wait for suffering beings to request aid, petition them, or earn their help; they step forward spontaneously as self-appointed guardians.
*   **Motivated by Great Compassion (*snying rje chen po*):** **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** explicitly state that the Bodhisattva is "drawn by great compassion" to act. **Sazang Mati Panchen** similarly attributes their actions to being moved "out of compassion."
*   **Perfect Accomplishment of Benefit and Happiness:** **Gyaltsab Je** and **Ngulchu Thokme** use identical phrasing to describe the ultimate scope of the Bodhisattva's work: they strive to "perfectly accomplish all benefit and happiness" for all wandering beings. **Sazang Mati Panchen** describes this as working to "establish them in goodness and virtue."
*   **Unconditional and Free of Expectation:** **Sazang Mati Panchen** provides a crucial, unique insight by emphasizing that the Bodhisattva acts "without expecting any return or maturation of action." Unlike worldly actors who may seek appreciation, reciprocal favors, or positive karmic payback, the Bodhisattva's dedication is entirely pure, unconditional, and free of self-interest.
