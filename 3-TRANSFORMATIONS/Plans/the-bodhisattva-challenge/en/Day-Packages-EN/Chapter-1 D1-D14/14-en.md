<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Chapter One ends with how we stand toward those who carry this mind. A single moment of faith in such a person, the verse says, brings more good than giving sight back to everyone who is blind. And they are so steady that even people who harm them are led, in time, toward happiness. The chapter closes by bowing to them.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

These two verses easily show the bodhisattva's wonderful qualities, and why we should take refuge in them.

**First.** Anyone who feels faith and devotion toward a bodhisattva gains boundless merit. And whatever harm or trouble comes to these children of the Buddhas, they turn it into part of the path — just as wind only helps a forest fire grow. So their goodness naturally increases, and a harmful thought never arises in them.

**Second.** Whoever they are, high or low, once the awakening mind has arisen in their heart, they become a holy object of homage and refuge. They give the highest happiness even to those who harm them. For example, long ago our own Teacher was a sage known as "the one who speaks of patience." A king cut off his limbs, yet he did not give rise to a single harmful thought; instead, he made a heartfelt prayer for that king. Later, when the sage became a Buddha, that very king became one of his first good followers and was among the first to be led onto the path of happiness. This story shows it well. A good connection can lead to buddhahood in a single life, while a bad connection can keep samsara going without end. So the bodhisattva is truly the real source of all happiness.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

If, focusing on a bodhisattva, a mind of devotion arises through making the mind utterly bright toward them, then — by the measure of however many instants of faith and devotion arise — [the merit] exceeds all counting; "its fruit increases even more," it is said: over more eons than those instants, one comes to enjoy perfect resources, and gains the merit of enjoying them. "Even by great hardship for the buddhas' heirs, no wrongdoing arises, and their virtue naturally increases" — so, because the buddhas' heirs who have trained their minds in the bodhicitta that cherishes others more than self are always meditating on the great courage of bodhicitta, even when they meet with a great [adverse] condition they cannot, on the spot, give rise to a non-virtuous mind; and even when they meet a truly dire, urgent condition, that very urgent situation — in place of the mental faults of attachment and aversion that would [otherwise] arise — becomes the condition by which patience grows, and the mind of special love for others grows, so that virtue naturally increases. Even if one does not have full-fledged bodhicitta in one's continuum, if a good mind concordant with it arises, one cannot be shaken by whatever condition comes. That the compassionate ship-captain, by killing the villain Short-Spear, saved the lives of four hundred and ninety-nine men — that too was a benefit [born] of bodhicitta; so bodhicitta is certain to be a great friend to bringing virtue to its very end.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-35]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Even if great hardship befalls the buddhas' heirs — for as long as the very ends of the many beings, or for the span of many eons, performing deeds hard to do, giving away their head and limbs, their child and spouse, their retinue and kingdom, and so on, and so meeting great difficulty — still, in the continua of the buddhas' heirs, who are endowed with great compassion at the very root, no thought of wrongdoing arises; and, as the *Niyatāvatāra* ⚑ teaches, all adverse conditions are joined to the path of awakening, so that all their virtue naturally increases. As Gyalsé Thogme says: "When the world and its beings are filled with wrongdoing, transform adverse conditions into the path of awakening." For a powerful, strong bodhisattva, [adversity] arises like a shadow [cast] by the sun and moon; for a small, weak bodhisattva, it can become like a lamp blocked by the wind, so that it does not blaze. Yet, just as, once a great forest fire has caught, whatever wind arises becomes a friend to the blaze, so — [in the end] — no wrongdoing arises in the continua of others to be trained, and virtue increases.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

**The first hemistich ("if one makes the mind bright").** However, if some person makes the mind bright with faith toward a bodhisattva, the fruit of that faith increases even more than the fruit of the wrongdoing explained before: however many instants of the bright mind there are, one experiences, over an even greater number of eons, a correspondingly distinguished fruit — as the *Niyatāniyatagatimudrāvatāra Sūtra* ⚑ teaches.

**The second hemistich ("no wrongdoing arises").** Even by great hardship for the great buddhas' heirs — thoughts that would harm or bring them suffering, and the like — indeed, even at the cost of life, no mind of wrongdoing such as anger arises, and their virtue naturally increases. On the commentary's reading, [the point is] that in a bodhisattva's continuum there does not arise even the thought, "there is no use my staying angry here"; so it is explained that it does not arise even under great hardship. The meaning is that the [bodhisattva] heroes, even at the cost of life, do no wrongdoing, and their virtue naturally increases.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

*[Grouping note: Ngulchu Thogme explains the first line of verse 1-35 ("if one makes the mind bright") under a section, "Third (that [the bodhisattva] is a special field)," that also covers verse 1-34; for that portion see Verse 1-34.]*

**The fourth [point] (not being crushed by suffering).** Even by great harm and suffering for the buddhas' heirs, no mind of wrongdoing such as anger arises; and, through the power of that very condition, again their virtue naturally increases. As the *Pāramitāsamāsa* (Compendium of the Perfections) says: "Even by many unbearable kinds of harm, the minds of those supreme beings do not change; and because, by the power of patience, they are exceedingly firm, their aspiration to accomplish supreme awakening grows all the more."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Even by great hardship for the buddhas' heirs, no wrongdoing arises, and their virtue naturally increases. As the *Niyatāvatāra* ⚑ says in general: "Even if honored by a host of rākṣasīs, so long as I am not parted from bodhicitta, I have kept hold of that path"; and [specifically]: "Even by many hardships for the buddhas' heirs, no wrongdoing arises, and, by the four means of gathering, the virtue that works the welfare of beings naturally increases."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Even if, in a single life, hardship extremely hard to describe befalls the buddhas' heirs, in great buddhas' heirs like Thogme Zangpo no thought of wrongdoing arises through that adverse condition; and, moreover, by the power of that very adverse condition they are made to enter the path of awakening with even greater force, so that all their virtue naturally increases. As the *Pāramitāsamāsa* says: "Even by many unbearable kinds of harm, the minds of those supreme beings do not change; and because, by the power of patience, they are exceedingly firm, their aspiration to accomplish supreme awakening grows all the more." If it is so, then in the continua of others to be trained too, no wrongdoing arises and virtue increases — for example, just as the sage Kṣāntivādin (the Patience-Proclaimer), even when the king cut off his head and limbs, abided in the welfare of beings through great compassion. Minyak Kunzang Sonam sets it out with a worldly example too: like a wealthy merchant who, though beset by shivering and cold as he sets out to trade in a city, does not turn back to the tavern but benefits everyone on every side.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

**The first line.** However, if some person of noble conduct, endowed with a good and faithful heart, merely gets a good look at — or merely gazes upon — the body and face of a bodhisattva, with mind made utterly bright, then the virtuous fruit of that merit increases higher and higher, even more than the fruit of the earlier negative thought, so that one comes to possess an immeasurable heap of merit.

**The second line.** For those buddhas' heirs — the heart-sons of the victorious buddhas, who have become the benefactors of all beings of the three realms — however great a hardship arises: whether performing deeds hard to do for the span of the very ends of many beings or for many eons, giving away head and limbs, child and spouse, retinue and kingdom — no wrongdoing of any kind arises, such as turning back from the path of awakening, or losing heart, or growing discouraged; and moreover, the roots of virtue of their diligence — the mind engaged in others' welfare, and the wish to swiftly attain unsurpassed complete awakening — naturally increase higher and higher. For example, just as, once a great forest fire has caught, whatever wind arises becomes a friend to the blaze — the same principle holds, he says.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### The Kṣāntivādin jātaka and the merchant example

Minyak Kunzang Sonam adduces the birth-story of the sage Kṣāntivādin (the Patience-Proclaimer): even when the king cut off his head and limbs, through great compassion he abided in the welfare of beings. He also gives a worldly example: like a wealthy merchant setting out to trade in a city, who, though beset by shivering and much cold, does not turn back to the tavern but benefits everyone on every side. Both illustrate how every great hardship that befalls the buddhas' heirs naturally becomes a cause of awakening.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR_bo -->
##### BCACXX_WR_bo — Nanda's Story

The story given to illustrate this verse's phrase "if someone makes the mind bright [toward a bodhisattva]." When the Bhagavān taught that if one cuts the net of desire with the weapon of wisdom one becomes a pure, desireless field, the bodhisattva Nanda rose from meditative absorption and went to Śrāvastī for alms; there he saw the merchant's daughter Śrīmatī ("the Glorious One") and grew intensely attached. Though his mindfulness slipped, in a moment he recovered it, and examining the phenomenon of attachment he found no phenomenon that becomes "attachment" — and so attained the acceptance of the non-arising of phenomena. The Teacher said to Ānanda, "Relying on the mind of desire, Nanda defeated the hosts of Māra and turned the wheel." Śrīmatī, tormented by desire, died and was reborn among the gods; recognizing [her past] through recollection, she made offerings to Nanda. Though her parents were angry, saying "Nanda has bewitched her," when they understood what had happened, five hundred kinsfolk generated bodhicitta. This story shows that one should have faith in the conduct of the bodhisattvas.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^1-11]]

<!-- story:BCACXX_WR_bo -->
##### BCACXX_WR_bo — The Monkey's Story

The birth-story given to illustrate this verse's phrase "even by great hardship for the buddhas' heirs, no wrongdoing arises and their virtue naturally increases." Long ago, when the Teacher was born as a monkey: a man had fallen to the bottom of a ravine and, unable to escape, was wailing; the monkey heard him, and out of compassion trained by lifting heavy stones, then carried the man out on his back. The man, [pretending] to repay the kindness, while the monkey slept, took up a stone to smash its head and kill it for food. The monkey woke, looked upon the man with compassion, and led him safely to the edge of a village. Later that man's whole body broke out [in sores] and was cast off; when the king asked the reason, [it was told that] "for a mere flower's worth of harm done to loving kindness, he experienced the suffering of hell in another world." That great monkey, though repaid with such evil for his kindness, still, through great compassion, strove again at virtue.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCACXX_WR_bo.md#^1-12]]

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### The Compassionate Ship-Captain

A compassionate ship-captain, seeing the villain "Short-Spear" who had plotted to kill the four hundred and ninety-nine men aboard the ship, himself killed Short-Spear — both to protect that man from his own great wrongdoing and to save the lives of the four hundred and ninety-nine. This is given as an example of the benefit of bodhicitta, showing that even without full-fledged bodhicitta in the continuum, a good mind concordant with it gives a firmness that cannot be shaken by whatever condition arises.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-35]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **How adverse conditions turn into virtue.** This verse shows the reverse [counterpart] of the point of verse 1-34: just as, earlier, anger toward a bodhisattva increases one's wrongdoing over as many eons as the instants [of anger], so here a bright, faithful mind (first hemistich) increases one's merit over a like number of eons; and though great hardship befalls the buddhas' heirs (second hemistich), no wrongdoing arises, and moreover their virtue naturally increases. Khenpo Kunga's fire-and-wind image makes this vivid: whatever wind blows, it becomes a friend to the blaze.

2. **The four means of gathering (KS).** Khenpo Zhenga explains the specific reason adverse conditions do not produce wrongdoing but produce virtue in a bodhisattva's continuum: relying on the four means of gathering (generosity, kind speech, teaching what accords [with each being], acting in accord [with what one teaches]), the bodhisattva benefits beings, and so, even amid great hardship, does not turn back from these but has their virtue naturally increase.

3. **The distinction of strength (KKP).** Kunzang Pelden marks a distinction of bodhisattvas' strength with the images of the sun and moon and the lamp: the strong cannot be harmed by adversity; even a weak one, though a few harms may come, gives rise to no wrongdoing at all and has virtue increase, slowly, all the same. Kunzang Pelden brings in Thogme's verse — "when the world and its beings are filled with wrongdoing, transform adverse conditions into the path of awakening" — as the practice-instruction of this very point.

4. **The firmness of steady courage (Tenzin Gyatso).** Tenzin Gyatso explains the reason behind "virtue naturally increases": because the buddhas' heirs are always meditating on the great courage of bodhicitta, even when they meet a great, urgent condition no fault of mind arises; rather, that very urgency becomes the condition by which patience and love for others grow. Even without full-fledged bodhicitta, a good mind concordant with it gives a firmness that whatever condition arises cannot shake — as the story of the compassionate ship-captain shows.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse joins, as a comparison with verse 1-34, a concordant ground with a discordant fruit: if one makes the mind bright toward a buddhas' heir who is a benefactor, then — even more than [the fruit of] a negative mind — merit increases over many eons; and though the greatest hardship befalls the buddhas' heirs, no wrongdoing arises in them; just as a fire is made to blaze by the wind, all adverse conditions make their virtue increase all the more.

**Key points.**

- If one makes the mind bright [with faith] — one gains an immeasurable heap of merit over many eons.
- Even by great hardship no wrongdoing arises — adverse conditions, like wind to a fire, fan the strength of the path of awakening.
- Khenpo Zhenga explains that, relying on the four means of gathering, adverse conditions turn to virtue.
- Kunzang Pelden marks the distinction of strength and joins Gyalsé Thogme's verse, "transform adverse conditions into the path of awakening," at the crux of the point.
- Tenzin Gyatso adduces the story of the compassionate ship-captain, explaining that even a good mind concordant with bodhicitta gives a firmness that whatever condition arises cannot shake.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

To the bodies of those beings, whoever they are, who have in their continuum the bodhicitta that cherishes others more than self, one pays homage — recollecting such benefits of bodhicitta, bowing with reverence to the lowest part of the body, the feet. As in the Teacher's former birth-stories: not only does benefiting one who bears bodhicitta in the continuum [bring good], but even if one forms not a good but a bad connection with them, by the power of that bodhicitta one comes, in turn, to have an extraordinary connection oneself. For example, that in a former time King Maitrībala, by the power of love, gave his flesh and blood to a rākṣasa, and thereby set down the karmic link by which [the rākṣasa] would be born first among his retinue in the future — that too came about through the power of bodhicitta. So what need is there to speak of the happiness that comes from benefiting [a bodhisattva], when even bringing them harm can, in a roundabout way, come to benefit oneself? Since [the bodhisattva] is thus the ultimate source of happiness, the very place from which the happiness of beings arises, one goes for refuge to them, recollecting their qualities.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^1-36]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whatever person, high or low, in whose [continuum] there has arisen that precious mind of awakening — the most sublime of all the mind's thoughts, which removes all the decline of existence and peace — to the body of such a one Shantideva pays homage with great reverence of the three doors. And though some person does harm to a bodhisattva — scorning them and the like — it is as the saying goes, "a good connection [leads to] buddhahood in a single life; a bad connection [leads to] samsara that has an end"; so, by the power of [the bodhisattva's] compassion, bodhicitta, skillful means, and aspiration, [the doer of harm] is joined to — that is, attains — happiness, temporary and ultimate. For example, [it is] like the sage Kṣāntivādin, and like King Maitrībala ("Power of Love").

Therefore, since in every way a connection [with a bodhisattva] is meaningful, I go for refuge to the buddhas' heirs, the sublime beings, the source of all happiness of this life and the next. Now, does this not contradict what was said above — that giving rise to a negative mind toward a bodhisattva causes rebirth in hell — with what is said here, that doing harm connects one with happiness? It does not contradict: the former was spoken with reference to the immediate [result] of a negative mind, birth in hell, from the standpoint that karma and its fruit are unfailing; while here [the point] is that, through the bodhisattva's compassion, bodhicitta, mastery of skillful means, and the force of aspiration, [the doer of harm] is taken up [and led on]. So there is no contradiction.

Thus, understanding that there are [such] qualities in one who bears bodhicitta in the continuum, one must confess and vow restraint for whatever faults of the three doors one has committed toward the buddhas' heirs before, and take refuge. Because the sutras teach that in this degenerate age the fault of [wrongly] relying on other persons arises very readily for bodhisattvas, it is important to train in pure perception, not looking at beings' faults. Just as the brahmins of India who hold the moon divine do not bow to the full moon but bow to the crescent moon, so one should bow to a bodhisattva [likewise]. And even if some fault or flaw is present, since it is compounded [and impermanent], it is exhausted by the power of meditating on the path, and [the being] becomes a buddha; so not looking at beings' faults is, he says, a most important pith instruction.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

To the body of the person in whom that sublime mind — which removes all decline and grants every prosperity, like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel — has arisen, I pay homage at all times with reverence of the three doors.

Not only does the bodhisattva give happiness to whoever benefits them; even to one who does harm, by scorning them and the like, through great compassion they connect the [harm-doer] with happiness both temporary and ultimate, and bestow it. Having pledged always to rely on that sublime being who is, without exception, the source of all happiness, I entrust my mind to them and go for refuge.

That giving rise to a negative mind toward a sublime object leads to falling into hell does not contradict [this], and that those endowed with great compassion, by the force of their skillful means and aspiration, join [the harm-doer] in the end to great happiness does not contradict [it] either; so, through recollecting the benefits of bodhicitta, one should also have devotion toward the sublime persons endowed with it.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Thus, in the continuum of any being whatsoever — high, low, or middling; male or female; old or young; without distinction — in whom the sublime mind, the precious bodhicitta, has arisen (the good intention wishing to attain, and to enter the path toward, the state of perfect buddhahood for the sake of other beings), to the body of such a one — bowing even to the very lowest limb, the sole of the foot, with the crown of his own head — Shantideva makes offering, praises, and pays homage.

And though someone does harm to a bodhisattva endowed with the good intention of bodhicitta, that doer of harm is, now and lastingly, joined to benefit and happiness — that is, set in a place of happiness: [the bodhisattva] has become the source of all the happiness [that comes of] such a connection. By the power of the bodhisattva's resolve and aspiration, even though the doer of harm must, on account of the harm done in the interim, experience over a long time the ripened fruit — the suffering of the hells and the rest — in the end there comes a time when, [his] samsara [made to] have an end, he is taken up by the bodhisattva's compassion, or, by the force of [the bodhisattva's] aspiration, is freed from samsara.

In our Teacher's former birth-story: when the king, in the guise of a hunter, "Little-Quarrel" (Kaliṅga), with his five attendants cut off all the head and limbs of the sage Kṣāntivādin and did violence to his very life, at that time the sage held [them] with compassion and made aspiration-prayers; and so, when the Teacher fully awakened as a buddha, the first field of disciples at Vārāṇasī, where he first turned the wheel of Dharma — the good group of five, Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya and the rest — were, it is taught, the final rebirths of King Little-Quarrel and his five attendants. To such a source of happiness, that bodhisattva, at all times and in all situations, I go for refuge. Shantideva too pays homage and goes for refuge.

#### Stories and Illustrations

**The Kṣāntivādin jātaka (NKW).**

In our Teacher's former birth-story, the king in the guise of a hunter, "Little-Quarrel" (Kaliṅga), with his five attendants cut off all the head and limbs of the sage Kṣāntivādin and did violence to his life. On that occasion the sage held the five harm-doers with compassion and made aspiration-prayers; by that power, when the Teacher awakened as a buddha, the first field of disciples at Vārāṇasī, where he first turned the wheel of Dharma — the good group of five, Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya and the rest — were the final rebirths of King Little-Quarrel and his five attendants. This is given to illustrate directly how, though harm is done, the doer of harm is in the end freed into happiness — making manifest the power of the "source of happiness" to take up [even one's harmers].

**King Maitrībala and the five yakṣas (KKP, NTS).**

For example, just as King Maitrībala ("Power of Love"), by the power of love, brought the five yakṣas to happiness, now and lastingly (NTS) — and as the sage Kṣāntivādin, and as King Maitrībala (KKP). Though the five yakṣas had earlier done harm, [the king] connected them with happiness, temporary and ultimate — KKP and NTS both give this as the example of the bodhisattva's transforming all harm into a connection with happiness.

**His Holiness the Dalai Lama — King Maitrībala giving flesh and blood to a rākṣasa.**

In a former time King Maitrībala ("Power of Love"), by the power of love, gave his own flesh and blood to a rākṣasa, and thereby set down the karmic link by which that [rākṣasa] would be born first among his retinue in the future. This is given as an example of how, by the power of bodhicitta, even a connection [formed through] harm turns in the end to benefit. *[Cross-reference note: this is the same King Maitrībala birth-story adduced by KKP and NTS above, told here in His Holiness the Dalai Lama's manner, which fixes the gift of flesh and blood as the specific deed that established the karmic connection.]*

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **The object of homage.** The object of homage in this verse is the precious bodhicitta itself: no matter in whom it arises — high, low, or middling, male or female, old or young, without distinction (KKP: "in whomever, high or low"; NKW: "any being whatsoever, high, low, or middling, male or female, old or young, without distinction") — [that being] becomes worthy of homage. With this verse, Chapter One — the explanation of bodhicitta's benefits — closes with Shantideva himself paying homage and going for refuge to the beings who possess bodhicitta.

2. **How the doer of harm too is brought to happiness.** Even one who does harm to a bodhisattva is, in the end, connected with happiness temporary and ultimate; this is accomplished by the fourfold power of the bodhisattva's compassion, bodhicitta, skillful means, and aspiration. Khenpo Kunga sets this out directly with the Kṣāntivādin jātaka: the five attendants who cut off the sage's head and limbs were later reborn as the good group of five, Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya and the rest, the first to be trained at Vārāṇasī.

3. **That 1-34 and 1-36 do not contradict.** Verse 1-34 (that anger toward a bodhisattva causes rebirth in hell for as many eons as the instants [of anger]) and verse 1-36 (that even doing harm connects one with happiness) may seem to contradict; but Kunzang Pelden, Sabzang, and Ngulchu Thogme all explain that there is no contradiction: the former is spoken, from the standpoint that karma and its fruit are unfailing, of the immediate [result] of a negative mind, birth in hell; while here the point is that the bodhisattva's compassion and aspiration take up the very doer of harm and, in the end, [make his] samsara have an end. So both are true at once.

4. **Training in pure perception (KKP).** Kunzang Pelden gives an emphatic counsel at the chapter's close: recognizing that beings endowed with bodhicitta are objects of homage for the world with its gods, and future buddhas, one should confess and vow restraint for whatever faults of the three doors one has committed toward the buddhas' heirs before, train in pure perception, and not look at beings' faults. Shown by the image of the [waxing] moon: even if a fault appears in a bodhisattva, because it is compounded it is exhausted by the power of meditating on the path and [the being] becomes a buddha; so it is seeing-as-a-fault that is itself the fault — the bodhisattva has none.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** At the close of Chapter One, this "seal" verse — in which Shantideva pays homage and goes for refuge to all who have generated bodhicitta in their continua — brings the chapter to its end. In whomever, high or low, bodhicitta has arisen is an object of homage; and because [the bodhisattva], even when harmed, frees the harm-doer into happiness in the end, one goes for refuge to them as the source of happiness. Though it seems to contradict verse 1-34, it does not: though [karma] is drawn out on the unfailing side of cause and effect, by the power of compassion and aspiration [the bodhisattva] frees even the doer of harm into happiness in the end — as King Little-Quarrel and his five attendants became the field of disciples at Vārāṇasī.

**Key points.**

- The object of homage — in whomever, high or low, the precious bodhicitta has merely arisen, [that one] becomes an object of homage and offering.
- The doer of harm is freed into happiness — the power of the "source of happiness" is made manifest through compassion, skillful means, and aspiration.
- "A good connection [leads to] buddhahood in a single life; a bad connection [leads to] a samsara that has an end" — both harm and reverence end, in the end, in happiness.
- Kunzang Pelden closes with the counsel to train in pure perception.
- Tenzin Gyatso adduces King Maitrībala's giving flesh and blood to a rākṣasa, explaining that even a connection [formed through] harm turns, by the power of bodhicitta, to benefit in the end.
