<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Given the way we are and the state we are in, "day and night" means that not for a moment, day or night, does life stand still: the time in which our life is being used up is counted out second by second within every passing instant, and life keeps draining downward. Day and night wear life away and the hours turn; there is no such thing as staying on permanently — there is none of it at all, and moment by moment it is falling apart. [→ illustrated by Gungthangtsang's verse; see the Scriptural Quotations.] So, as it is said, life diminishes and wastes away; and if there were some addition to be made to it that would be easy, but making an addition is extremely difficult. We do long-life empowerments and long-life practices and hope for some extension of life, but it is hard. It would require, on one's own side, some uncommon karmic connection, and the one bestowing the long-life practice and empowerment would have to be someone with realization and inconceivable power — then the auspicious conditions could be arranged; but that is very difficult. So a fresh addition is all the harder; and however much of a lifespan was projected by former merit, that too grows shorter and shorter with every instant. For that reason, how could someone like me not die? There is simply no basis for placing our confidence in the thought that we will not die and will remain a long while — so he says.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

If one does not confess at this very moment: life, having no addition and only diminishment, is like a pond whose feeding spring has been cut off — day and night, moment after moment, come one after another in sequence and life is consumed, so that there never arises even a single moment's opportunity in which life is not being consumed and simply stands still. This life is therefore always "draining," that is, diminishing. And as it is said, "Even if the King of Physicians came in person, once the momentum of the lifespan is spent, life cannot be spliced on to." So if no addition to one's lifespan comes in from outside — that is, from somewhere else — how could I, a being in samsara, and others like me, not certainly die? Die we certainly shall; therefore one must make an effort to confess one's wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

If one does not confess quickly, then — since through all the days and nights there is not even a moment's pause — this life is always draining away, like a pond cut off from the spring that feeds it; and if no addition to one's lifespan ever comes in from elsewhere, how could someone like me not die? Thus, gathering the essential point in brief, he makes clear the reason why wrongdoing must be confessed quickly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Whichever of the three one is doing — walking, sitting, or lying down — there is never even a moment's pause, day or night, and so this life is always draining, that is, being reduced; and if not the slightest addition ever comes in from outside, what reason is there that someone like me should not certainly die? Die one certainly shall. And since there is no certainty as to when death will come, one should make an effort to purify one's wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

"Thus, that I am only passing through" — because I did not understand this, I committed many kinds of wrongdoing out of deluded confusion, attachment, and anger. If one wishes to be purified within this life, one must purify quickly: for with no pause, day or night, this life drains away in a moment, and no addition ever comes in from outside. Therefore, how could someone like me not die? Die one certainly shall.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

As day and night come by turns, there never arises even a moment's opportunity to stand still, and so this human life is always draining, that is, dwindling; and if no addition to one's lifespan ever comes in from outside, how could someone like me not certainly die? Since die one will, it is only right to make the effort.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

(Detailed note: here verses 39–41 are gathered together and explained as one passage.) Suppose someone says: granted that one must die, yet how could death come quickly before the full measure of the lifespan is complete? There is no need to be so afraid. — Through all the days and nights, in whatever situation one is in, walking, sitting, lying down or moving about, there never arises even a moment's opportunity to stand still; or else, as day and night come one after the other by turns, there never arises even a moment's pause or anything like a rest. These formative factors of life are always "draining" or wasting — that is, being reduced or used up; and as much as is spent on the far side, not the slightest addition of that measure ever comes in from outside on this side. So what reason is there that someone like me should not certainly and quickly die? Die one certainly shall. And even if one lives long, since the end of living does not escape death, then by remembering that death is certain and its time uncertain, one should always and quickly make the effort to purify wrongdoing and to accomplish virtue. Further, some explain that here "draining" (གོད) has the sense of being reduced and used up, while "laying out" (འགོད) has the sense of day and night laying out one's life in separate portions; and there is also the reading "there is no addition by any means of extending life."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

Therefore, apart from having to confess those wrongdoings quickly, I am someone who has no leisure to remain here for a long life. And there is no certainty that this life will stay unexhausted even for a single day or a single night: this very life, like a pond whose feeding spring has been cut off, is always draining and can only be used up; like the sun going down, like a river running into the ocean, the time is quickly consumed and finished. And at the point when life is spent, if there is absolutely nothing of the nature of an addition to that life coming in from outside — that is, from some other side — nor anything that could be added on, then why should someone like me, an ordinary being in samsara with no power over his own life, not die quickly and suddenly? Since I am by nature certain to die suddenly, as though struck by lightning, it is fitting to strive quickly at the means of confessing wrongdoing.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-39]]

<!-- div:divergences -->
##### ⚑ Divergences (where the commentaries differ)

- **kunpal:** he glosses the sense of the word "འགོད" as day and night laying out one's life in separate portions.
- **minyak-kunzang-sonam:** he identifies both traditions of explanation as existing — "གོད" in the sense of being reduced and used up, and "འགོད" in the sense of day and night laying out one's life in separate portions.

The meaning does not conflict, but since there are differing traditions of explanation as to whether the word-form is "གོད" or "འགོད", it is noted separately.

#### Stories and Illustrations

<!-- story:BCAC13_KTB -->
##### BCAC13_KTB — The Fleeting Nature of Life

As an illustration of how short and impermanent the lifespan is: a man fell into a great lake below a high cliff and caught hold of a clump of reed-grass; and a little honey was dripping down from above onto his mouth, and he became attached to it. Meanwhile two mice, one white and one black, took turns gnawing the grass through, and when it was gone the man fell and was carried off into the sea. Yet he made no effort to climb up, nor did he guard against the mice — so that man was utterly foolish. In the same way this is applied, point by point, to the ocean of samsara, the cliff of the lower realms, our present attachment to the taste of desirable things now that we have gained leisure and endowment, and to those who, though certain to die once day and night have consumed their life, still do not practise the Dharma.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC13_KTB_bo.md#^2-39]]

<!-- sub:teaching-points -->
#### Main Teaching Points

1. **Life never pauses even for an instant, but is always diminishing.** — All the commentaries agree in the end that in no situation, day or night, is there any opportunity for life to stand still even for a moment: this life is always draining and diminishing.
2. **No addition to one's lifespan ever comes in from outside.** — They explain in one voice that there is absolutely nothing of the nature of an addition coming in from elsewhere, nor anything that could be added on, to match the measure by which life diminishes.
3. **Therefore someone like me will certainly die.** — Because life diminishes with no addition, without any doubt I too will certainly die; all of them make clear that an ordinary being in samsara has no power over his own life.
4. **Hence one must strive to confess wrongdoing quickly.** — Since there is no certainty as to when death will come, Kunzang Pelden, Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk and others make clear that, knowing this essential point of the impermanence of life, one must always and quickly strive to confess wrongdoing and to accomplish virtue.
5. **The comparison — diminishing with no addition, like a pond cut off from its feeding spring.** — The way life diminishes is explained through the example of a pond whose feeding spring has been cut off; and the *Discourse on Impermanence* states the very same thing in verse.
6. **The story-illustration — the two mice, white and black, gnawing through the grass held by the man on the cliff.** — This very point, that life diminishes with every instant, is conveyed by the story of the mice, showing the fault of those who, though certain to die once day and night have consumed their life, still do not practise the Dharma.

#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** This verse states that through all the days and nights life does not pause even for an instant but is always diminishing, and that there is no possibility whatever of an addition coming in from elsewhere — so that I too will certainly die. All the commentaries explain this in agreement as the reason why wrongdoing must be confessed quickly.

**Key points.**

- All the commentaries agree in the end that in no situation, day or night, does this life pause even for a moment; it is always draining and diminishing.
- Since there is no possibility whatever of an addition to life coming in from elsewhere, an ordinary being in samsara like me will certainly die.

- Therefore, since the time of death is uncertain, they explain that one must always and quickly strive to confess wrongdoing and to accomplish virtue.

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

On that last bed one is seized by a severe terminal illness. When the karma runs out through a mortal illness, taking medicine does not help, and performing rituals does not help either; the doctor's last resort collapses, and one reaches the point where the lama's last resort collapses too. When one reaches the point where there is nothing left for the lama to give and nothing left for the doctor to do, everyone reaches the point of letting their faces fall. It is then that people reach the point of saying, "He seemed better yesterday — how is he now?" On that last bed one lies like an old tree fallen over, able and yet unable to turn from side to side. And though one is surrounded on every side by the dear friend of one's heart, from whom one could not bear to be parted even for a short while in ordinary times, and by all the relatives and friends who are like one person and one heart, still that feeling of life being severed is something each of us must experience alone — there is no such thing as anyone dividing it up with us, or anyone taking a turn in our place. At the point called death, when body and mind separate and consciousness departs from this body — as a sign preceding it, as it is said, "The final severing is by water and the rest" — those sufferings of the severing at the disturbance of the four elements are something each of us must experience alone; there is nothing like our companions dividing it up, nor anything like their taking turns at it, one before and one after. So he teaches.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Thus, when life is spent and one comes to die: while *I* lie on the last of *beds* and *remain* there, *though* relatives close to me — father, mother, brothers and sisters — and *friends* and companions and loved ones and physicians and the rest, *all* of them, *surround* me on *every* side, and though they keep watch over me while they are themselves tormented by a suffering that all but makes them die too, it is of no help: the feelings of the unbearable suffering of the final severing at the *cutting off of life* — those *fierce feelings*, which no one will take on — *I myself alone* shall *experience*.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

In answer to the objection that there is nothing to fear because one has relatives and friends, he makes clear that at the time of the severing of life, though all one's relatives and loved ones surround one on every side, the feelings of suffering are something I alone shall experience, and that not even the smallest part of it can be passed on to anyone else.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

If one has not made merit, then at the time of death, while I lie on my bed, however great the tenderness and love of the relatives and friends who surround me on every side, the sufferings of the feeling of life being cut off I myself alone shall experience.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Granted that one will certainly die — but suppose one thinks that at that time one will need one's relatives and so on. At the time of death, while I lie on my bed, though all my relatives and friends surround me on every side, the feeling of the severing of life I myself alone shall experience; they cannot share it out.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

While I lie on the sickbed, though relatives and kinsmen and companions and loved ones and friends and physicians and nurses and the rest surround me on every side and guard me completely, still at the time of the severing of life the fierce feelings of the suffering of the final severing — which not one of those close to me will take on — I myself alone shall experience.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Here, when I am drawing near to my own death: while I lie on that bed where illness has struck me down, though all my loving relatives and friends, and all those who do me good such as physicians and nurses, sit surrounding me completely on every side, the harsh and unwelcome feelings of that moment when life is severed are something I myself alone shall experience — and there is no way for relatives, friends or anyone else to take a share of the suffering or to help dispel it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-40]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

While I myself, my life spent and death upon me, lie on that bed, my own relatives and kinsmen, brothers and companions and the rest — those friends — all gather and surround the edge of my bed and sit watching me with their eyes; yet when the actual moment comes and my death arrives, that fierce feeling of the final severing at death, the cutting off of life, which must be experienced — that is something I myself alone shall experience. However many of those relatives and friends there may be, there is no such thing as their dividing it among themselves so that I need not experience it. Since that is so, the suffering of the final severing at death, the delusive appearances of the intermediate state after death, and all the suffering of the lower realms in the next life fall upon me and are experienced by me — therefore it is fitting to strive quickly at the means of confessing wrongdoing. That is the meaning.

### Verse 2-41

#### Root Verse

*Plain English translation (source: `3-TRANSFORMATIONS/Translations/en-translate/BCA-Full-Plain-English.md#^2-41`):*

> When Yama's messengers finally come for me,
> what good will family do? What good will friends do?
> Only merit can protect me then —
> and I have built so little of it.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

When I am seized by the terrifying messengers of the Lord of Death, what good will relatives do? What good will loved ones and friends do? At that time merit alone is fit to be a refuge — and even that merit I did not cultivate in my own being.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-41]]

<!-- sub:commentary -->
#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, when one has been seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death and has reached the point where death is settled, there is no relative, kinsman or loved one of one's own, and no one at all capable of protecting, who can be of any help; and there is no such thing as dividing the suffering up. What, then, will help at that juncture? If, during the time one was alive before dying, one has accumulated a good strength of virtue within one's being, that can protect and that will help — apart from that, nothing helps at all. The one refuge that is certain to help at that juncture is one's own merit. And if one does not have that confidence of mind which, from the depths of the heart, thinks with ease, "I have accumulated merit while I was alive," then that juncture will be hard.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

At the time of the dissolution stages, the *messengers* of the Lord of Death, the terrifying forms of one's own deluded bad karma, *seize* one directly, bind one's neck with a black noose, and lead one away alone, *inflicting* suffering such as beating one with a hammer. At that point, what good will *relatives* — one's father, mother, children and other kin — *do*? And *what* good will *friends*, companions and loved ones *do*? No one can protect one. But at *that time*, if one had gathered and accomplished some *merit*, some virtue, *that alone* would become the supreme *refuge* — *yet even that I* did not *cultivate* before. So, because I did not gather it before, what can be done about it now?

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-zhengah -->
##### Zhenphen Jampé Gocha (annotation commentary)

In Zhenga's annotation commentary, the underlying gloss is set out from the side of the annotation commentary itself, without any difference as to whether a full explanatory commentary is appended. When one is seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death, no relative or friend is of any help; and though at that time merit alone is fit to be a refuge, I did not cultivate it before in my own being. Thus, in brief, the underlying gloss sets it out.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KS_bo.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Not only is there nothing to dispel the terror; when one is seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death, terrifying in form, what good does having relatives do, and what good do friends do? The Dharma alone becomes one's refuge.

At that time, though merit alone — going for refuge, keeping ethical discipline and the like — is the supreme refuge, "even that I did not cultivate." This is regret at not having done virtue.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

That may be so, but suppose one thinks that they can still protect one at that time. When one is seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death, what good do relatives do? What good do friends do? They do no good. Then what does help? At that time merit alone would protect — and even that I did not cultivate.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

When one is seized directly by the terrifying messengers of the Lord of Death and bound with a noose, beaten with a hammer, and led away into wildernesses thick with thorns and among sheer crags with one's limbs being cut — what good does having relatives and kin do, and what good do companions, loved ones and friends do? No one can protect one. But at that time the merit one had gathered would alone have become the supreme refuge — and even that I did not cultivate before, so I feel regret.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_SMPLG_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Not only that: when one is actually seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death, terrifying in form, bound at the neck with a noose and led away alone so that one is utterly wretched — what good do relatives do? Or, what good does a remedy do, and what good do companions, loved ones and friends do? No one at all can protect one from such suffering. By this it is taught that for those who have done wrong, never mind the next life, even in this very life, at a time like the moment of death, the suffering of the severing of life must be experienced by oneself alone. In particular, if one has accumulated grave wrongdoing, then at the moment when life is cut off, by the force of the disturbance of the three — fire, water and wind — one must also undergo the fierce and unbearable feeling of the piercing pain of the final severing, as though struck by a sharp weapon, and the suffering of thirst and burning of the body and the rest. And it is taught that those sufferings too must be borne by oneself alone, and that relatives, companions and the rest have no means of protecting one. In brief, it is taught that if one has not oneself abandoned wrongdoing, the cause of harm, then when one experiences the suffering that is the harmful result, no one has any means of dispelling it.

Then what does help and protect at that time? What certainly helps at that time is merit: the sublime Dharma alone — the supreme of virtuous actions, such as going for refuge and pure ethical discipline — becomes the supreme refuge, the sublime protector. Yet "even that merit, that virtuous action, I did not gather before, and did not cultivate in my being." This is the way regret arises at not having gathered virtue. In brief, it is taught that if one has not oneself accomplished the sublime Dharma, the cause of benefit, there is nothing else that can help.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_MKS_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

So then, if one is such a wrongdoer — someone who, having accumulated much wrongdoing and non-virtuous action in this life and in former lives, is certain to go to the lower realms such as the hells in the next life — then at the point when death arrives, the messengers of the Lord of Death, the executioners with the masked heads of tigers, leopards, bears and brown bears and the rest, utterly terrifying and horrifying, seize one directly with the noose and the iron hook of the Lord of Death; and when that appearance dawns, then however many relatives, kinsmen, companions and loved ones one may have, what good do all of them do? They do no good at all. What good do loved ones and friends do? They do none whatever. It is not in their nature to fight back against the Lord of Death with an army of heroes and repay him in kind; nor can any place of petition be arranged by flattering and praising; nor is there any way of buying him off by setting out one's wealth and goods. And at that time, the one thing that will certainly help oneself is this: if in this life one had gone for refuge to the Three Jewels and accomplished virtue, and had gathered some genuinely pure merit — the sublime Dharma, the remedy that abandons wrongdoing — then that would actually have protected one. "Yet even that, in this very life, I did not cultivate in my being as I should have." Not knowing how to cultivate it, one's life has run out; and at the point when death arrives, while undergoing the fierce suffering of the final severing at death, such states as the delusive appearance of being seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death may also arise. Therefore, the meaning is: from now on, confess wrongdoing quickly.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_NKW_bo_segmented.md#^2-41]]

#### Main Teaching Points

1. **When one is seized at death by the messengers of the Lord of Death, no relative or friend is of any help.** — All the commentaries explain in one voice that at the time of being seized directly by the messengers of the Lord of Death, whatever relatives, kinsmen and loved ones one has are of no help at all.
2. **What certainly helps at that time is merit alone, previously gathered.** — Minyak Kunzang Sonam makes clear that the sublime Dharma alone — such as going for refuge and pure ethical discipline — is the supreme refuge. Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk likewise explains that if one had gone for refuge to the Three Jewels, accomplished virtue and gathered some genuinely pure merit, that would actually have protected one.
3. **Regret arises because that merit was not gathered and not cultivated before.** — Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen makes it clear: "This is regret at not having done virtue." Sabzang Mati Panchen and Minyak Kunzang Sonam likewise explain it as the way regret arises at not having gathered and cultivated merit before.
4. **As scripture shows, at the time of death no relative, wealth or power follows after one.** — Minyak Kunzang Sonam cites the *Sutra of Advice to the King*, the *Lalitavistara*, and the words of Vijayagatamitra to show that at the time of death there is no refuge other than the Dharma, and that nothing whatever — father and mother, children, wealth and goods, power and position — follows after one.
5. **Therefore one must confess wrongdoing quickly from now on and accomplish virtue.** — Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk makes clear the necessity of confessing wrongdoing quickly, since what helps at the time of death is only the merit gathered from now on.
