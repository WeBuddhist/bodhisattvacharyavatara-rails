## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the eighty-seventh and eighty-eighth verses of the fifth chapter. We think about when we must give up our own body, and when we must not. We also think about why we do not teach the Dharma to people holding umbrellas and staffs.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable quotation, which is easy to understand and encouraging. Master Aryadeva taught about the body in the *Chatuhshataka* as follows. "Although we see the body as an enemy, even so we must protect it. Living long with ethics creates great merit from it." Although this body is like an enemy because it brings harm, living long with ethics accomplishes great merit. For this reason, we protect this body.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, to benefit others, I will take good care of my own body by eating and resting well.

**Explanation:** *(Taming the mind)* Without pure compassion, we must not harm our own body. If we protect this body well, we can bring great benefit to others. Living a long life becomes a cause for gathering great merit.

<!-- sub:interlinear -->
#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

Without the compassionate intention — which engages without distinction toward all, friends and non-friends alike — being fully pure, this body should not be given away to others, for it is said: "The medicine tree called 'lovely to behold,' / its roots and so on fit for use, and its seed — / like the medicine tree of the perfect buddha, / the childish should guard it well from untimely use." "In any case, in this life and in others too, if one sees that it will become a cause for accomplishing the great aims of oneself and others, one gives it."

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

"...so it says." Thus, although it is said that one must train in actually giving beings one's body, enjoyments, and roots of virtue, the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* teaches the way to protect the body and enjoyments — for the time being, when one is unable to give, or when giving would serve no great purpose. It is pure when it is not tainted by the thought of one's own aims, and it must be increased further in the conditions for accomplishing others' aims; that is, one must practice giving, guarding, purifying, and increasing in four ways each, with body, enjoyments, and roots of virtue. "If, while the compassionate intention is not pure, one actually gives the body, one falls away from (ཉམས་འགྲཱུ་བས) great purposes." Once the compassionate intention is pure — when some great purpose of accomplishing others' aims is about to come — "if," in this life and the next and in all circumstances, it benefits, and the great accumulations of oneself and others are completed, and one arrives at the point of accomplishing the great purpose of others' aims, then at that time one must give without reluctance.

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Therefore a beginner whose compassionate intention is not pure — that is, until he has attained the first ground, Perfect Joy — should not actually give to others this body, the support for accomplishing the sacred Dharma, such as its head, hands, and feet; for it is uncertain that it would benefit others, one would regret it, and it could become an obstacle to virtue. The *Śikṣāsamuccaya* teaches that, for example, it must be guarded like a great medicine tree. "In any case, in this life and in other lives too" — if, as in the case of the god-lama Changchub Ö giving up his body, it becomes a cause for accomplishing "great aims" for the teachings and for beings, and will not become an obstacle to virtue, then "one gives it" — that is, one must give it.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-87]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Although a bodhisattva has, from the very beginning, given up body and so on to beings with firm intention, still, because the ascetic hardship of those who ask for flesh from the body and so on would make one disheartened, and because one would feel regret afterwards, one should not give this body — without the compassionate intention that cherishes others more than oneself (བདག་པས) being pure — for, as the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* extensively shows by citing a sutra, giving at the wrong time is taught to be Māra's work. When, from one's own side, one is free of obstacles to giving such as miserliness, and the great accumulations will be completed without fault, then, "in any case, in this life and in others too, when it becomes a cause for accomplishing great aims, one gives it."

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-87]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

"Without compassionate intention being pure" — that is, without having reached the ground of pure superior intention — one should not actually give this body; for it is uncertain that it would benefit others, and it could lead to regret, to the mind turning back, and to obstacles to virtue. The *Śikṣāsamuccaya* also says: "For example, the medicine tree, lovely to behold — / its roots and so on fit to be refined (སྦྱང་འོས), and its seed — / even when given, should be guarded from untimely use; / the medicine tree of the perfect buddha is likewise." Here, some texts read "when the intention is equal, one should give it," and Namnang Tso (རྣམ་སྣང་འཚོ) did not comment on that. If someone says, "If one does not give the body, doesn't that contradict the pledge?" — no: "in any case, in this life and in others too, for the sake of accomplishing others' great aims, one should give it in others' recollection (གཞན་གྱི་དྲན་དུ)," as the *Detailed Explanation* (རྣམ་བཤད) says. Then, when the intention is pure, one should give it as is fitting.

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Therefore a beginner whose compassionate intention is not pure, and who has not come to see self and others as equal, should not give this body — head and so on — as a gift; for conduct unconnected with the proper time would become an obstacle to benefiting self and others. If one does nonetheless give the body, then "in any case" means "certainly": "in this life and in others too, one gives it as a cause for accomplishing great benefit for self and others" — or else, as in the Indian original and its commentary: [quotation not carried in this block] — "as it says," it means that when one has attained a ground and one's superior intention has not declined, and great aims will be accomplished, one should give it. And in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* too: [quotation not carried in this block] — "as it says."

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

"...so it says"; and in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* too: [quotation not carried in this block] — "so it says." The manner of guarding the body and so on should be known in detail from that same text. That shows the way in which giving the body is, or is not, permissible with respect to timing. Moreover, it is taught that one should not give even to those who ask with an intent to challenge, such as the gods of Māra's retinue and those they have instigated, or to those who ask out of madness and the like; these are cases where giving is not permissible because of the one who asks. For those to whom, because of the purpose and so on, it is not permissible to give, not only is there no downfall if one does not give, but there is a downfall if one gives. In all situations other than these, one must give the body to one who asks for it. Two ways of giving are taught: giving permanently, selecting whichever major or minor limb is needed; and giving temporarily, for the sake of accomplishing actions in accord with the Dharma for others and the like, in the manner of going as a servant. As for the manner of gathering disciples through the gift of Dharma: the distinctions in the listener's conduct are —

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

"Impure compassionate intention" means: if one wonders, "When does the compassionate intention become pure?" — as Nāgārjuna said, "If one realizes from the depths of the mind / this primordially unborn nature, / compassion will arise spontaneously / for those sunk in the mire of samsara." From the first ground, where the truth of reality is directly realized, the compassionate intention becomes completely pure. Therefore, until that ground is reached, a beginner bodhisattva must guard this body and not give it, as the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* teaches: "The medicine tree called 'lovely to behold,' / its roots and so on fit for use, and its seed — / like the medicine tree of the perfect buddha, / the childish should guard it well from untimely use." Further: "in any case, in this life and in others, such as later lives" — if something like a cause for accomplishing great aims arises, that is, if one gives one's own body, for example being able to save another person's life, and that too with great benefit coming to five, or ten, or a hundred and so on; or else, as in the aims of the Buddha's teachings, whatever is fitting: if, in this life and in later births, a cause for accomplishing the great aims of self and others arises, "one gives it" — that is, giving is permitted; permission is granted to beginner bodhisattvas. For example, in the past, in order to invite Jowo Je Palden Atisha to Tibet, the Tibetan king, Lha Lama Yeshe Ö himself, gave his own body to the king of the borderland Garlog; in a case like that it is permissible to give. Such is the meaning.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** While the compassionate intention is not pure, actually giving the body would fall away from great purposes, and so it must not be given; yet when, in this life and the next, it becomes a cause for accomplishing the great aims of oneself and others, it must be given without reluctance.

**Key points.**

- If a beginner actually gives the body without pure superior intention and compassion, it is uncertain that it will benefit others, and it will lead to regret and to obstacles to virtue, or to a downfall.
- The compassionate intention becomes completely pure from the first ground, where the nature of reality is directly realized.
- Giving the body at the wrong time, before the mind that cherishes others more than oneself (བདག་པས) has grown, is Māra's work; so, like a medicine tree, it must be guarded from untimely use.
- Even if the body is seen as an enemy, it must be protected, as taught in the *Four Hundred* and the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*.
- As an example of accomplishing great aims: the Tibetan king Lha Lama Yeshe Ö gave his own body to the king of the borderland in order to bring Jowo Je Atisha to Tibet.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Those who lack respect, in intention and in conduct, toward the Dharma and the lama should not be taught the Dharma; for if one teaches them, the greatness of the Dharma is diminished, the listener gains no benefit, and moreover, through disrespect for the Dharma he will fall into the lower realms. Even where the intention is respectful, there is a distinction in conduct: those who, though not ill or sick, have the head wrapped with a headcloth or the like; those who wear a hat; those who hold an umbrella and a staff — that is, a hand-support — and those who hold weapons such as a sword or spear; those whose heads are covered with a blanket or the like; and those who sit on a large, high seat and so on — to these the Dharma should not be taught; it is not permissible to teach them. For the ill and so on there is no fault.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->

One should not teach the Dharma to those who are disrespectful toward the Dharma and the Dharma speaker. As for the distinction in conduct: those who, though not ill, have the head wrapped; those who hold an umbrella, a staff, or weapons in the hand; and those whose heads are covered with cloth and the like — to these too, the Dharma should not be taught.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

Moreover: (in the Jātakas:) [citation not carried in this block] — "as it says," [the Dharma is taught] to those endowed with respect; but to those who lack respect for the sacred Dharma and for the one who speaks it, one does not teach the profound Dharma, because if one teaches, that listener would fall into hell through the karma of abandoning the Dharma. Even where the intention is respectful, there is a distinction in conduct: those who, though not ill, have the head wrapped with a headcloth; those who wear a hat; those who hold an umbrella in the hand; those who hold a staff; those who hold weapons such as a sword; and those whose heads are covered with cloth and the like — to these too the Dharma should not be taught. For those afflicted by illness or heat and so on there is no fault.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

Because the profound sacred Dharma is hard to find and is worthy of offerings and veneration by all beings, together with the gods, one should not teach the Dharma to those who lack respect for the Dharma and the Dharma speaker. If one teaches them, the gods will mock and revile, and for that reason the Teacher forbade it; and the listener, lacking respect for the Dharma, will be born in hell through the karma of rejecting the Dharma, and so on.

> Standing, to one who is seated, one should not teach the Dharma. Seated, to one who is lying down, one should not teach the Dharma.

Such statements are also given as examples. And if the one teaching is like a monk, it is said that teaching such persons gives rise to an offense of wrongdoing each time; so it is taught that one must teach the Dharma without being stained by the twenty-six offenses of wrongdoing. It is said that there is no fault if a listener who is ill, or afflicted by heat and so on, has the head wrapped or holds an umbrella and so on; and likewise, there is no fault in the case of a sick person with respect to not teaching the Dharma while sitting to one who is lying down or while standing to one who is sitting, and so on.

In short, the listener of the Dharma must listen having generated respect and reverent service. From the *Sutra of the Essence of the Earth* (སའི་སྙིང་པོའི་མདོ):

> Listen to the Dharma with single-minded faith and respect;
> do not mock or disparage it.
> As for making offerings to the one who speaks the Dharma:
> cultivate toward him the perception that he is like a buddha.

So it says. And the *Bodhisattva Grounds* (བྱང་ས) explains that one must listen free of the two afflictions, pride and belittling, and without paying mind to five grounds for thinking, "I will not listen to this one": that he has broken his ethical discipline, that he is of low caste, that he is ugly, that his wording is poor, and that he speaks harshly and unpleasantly. As for distinctions of conduct and so on, the Jātakas also say:

> Sit on a very low seat;
> give rise to the splendor of discipline.

and so on. As for the actual manner of listening, it is taught that one must listen by abandoning the three faults of the vessel and relying on the six perceptions, and so on. As for the benefits of hearing the Dharma by listening respectfully in this way, many are taught: in the chapter on hearing (ཐོས་པའི་ཚོམས), "Through hearing, one comes to know the Dharmas," and so on; and in the Jātakas too, "Whoever, through hearing, has the mind become faithful," and so on.

As for the distinctions in the speaker's conduct and so on, from the *White Lotus of the Sacred Dharma*:

> In a clean and pleasing place,
> having arranged and spread a broad seat well...

thus, sitting on a fine seat in a clean place; and

> Having put on that clean Dharma robe,
> well dyed with fine colors...

and so on — wearing a clean Dharma robe, well dyed in color; and

> Food and drink, things to eat and chew,
> clothing, bedding, and Dharma robes...

and so on — with an intention free of seeking gain and honor; and

> The wise one, always without jealousy...

and so on — free of jealousy, laziness, thoughts of weariness, and the like; and

> Cultivate the power of love toward the retinue.

and, "Elsewhere, the wise, always themselves and these beings — may they attain buddhahood," and so on — one must teach the Dharma having brought forth a mind of love and the wish for oneself and others to attain buddhahood, as taught, as extensively cited in that same text and in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*. Likewise, in the sutra *Requested by Lodrö Gyatso* (བློ་གྲོས་རྒྱ་མཚོས་ཞུས་པ), it says, "The Dharma speaker should be clean, careful in all conduct, well washed, and well dressed." Setting up the five perceptions — oneself as a physician, the Dharma as medicine, the listener as a patient, the Tathāgata as the sublime person, and long abiding in the manner of the Dharma — and, it is taught in that same text, when one recites from memory and makes manifest the Māra-severing mantra words and speaks about the Dharma, then within a radius of a hundred leagues around the retinue, Māra and the gods of Māra's retinue will not come with an intent to challenge, or even if they come, will be unable to create obstacles. And from the *Sutra of the Essence of the Earth* too:

> To dispel the illness of all afflictions,
> urge others to listen to the medicine of the sacred Dharma;
> having abandoned profit and fame far away,
> teach and explain the sacred Dharma.

as it says. Moreover, one must teach having abandoned all motivations and applications such as making a show with the Dharma (ཆོས་ལ་དཔེ་མཁྱུད་བྱེད་པ), self-praise, and belittling others. As it is said in the *Vinaya Sutra*, "Do not [teach] unless requested," one does not teach without a request; and in making requests too, one examines the vessel. The way it is permissible to teach even without a request, if one knows the person to be a suitable vessel, is as in the *King of Concentration*:

> For the sake of the gift of Dharma,
> if they request you,
> first say these words:
> "I have not trained extensively."

— so it should be understood. As for the benefits of teaching the Dharma in this way: in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, in the section on increasing merit, after stating, "The gift of Dharma has no material goods, / and becomes a cause for merit to increase," it cites the *Sutra Encouraging Superior Intention*: "one is endowed with mindfulness, endowed with intelligence, endowed with wisdom, endowed with devotion, endowed with discernment," and so on — the twenty benefits of teaching the Dharma without regard for material things such as gain, honor, and fame; it also cites the benefits taught in the Perfection of Wisdom sutras, and shows extensively the benefits of giving the Dharma and how to give it. Likewise, many benefits are taught in the *Maitreya's Lion's Roar Sutra* and others.

##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

As for the distinction of the recipients to whom Dharma is, or is not, taught, in taking disciples under one's care through the Dharma: to those of a kind whose intention is disrespectful toward the lama who explains the Dharma and toward the Dharma to be explained, it is not permissible to teach the Dharma, and one does not teach it. If one asks, "Then by what means is that distinction recognized?" — it is this: if one is ill, there is no problem; but while not ill, one has the head wrapped with cloth or the like; or holds a rain-umbrella or the like in the hand; likewise, while not elderly or ill, holds a staff in the hand; likewise holds a weapon such as a sword in the hand; or covers the head by wrapping it. The Dharma should not be taught to such persons, for a disrespectful intention toward the Dharma shows outwardly.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** One must not teach the Dharma to those who are disrespectful, in intention and in conduct, toward the Dharma and the Dharma speaker. One must not teach the Dharma to those whose conduct shows disrespect — wrapping the head though not ill, holding an umbrella, a staff, or weapons, and so on.

**Key points.**

- If one teaches the Dharma to those who are disrespectful toward the Dharma and the lama, slander of the Dharma's greatness and the like arise.
- If one teaches the profound Dharma to those who are disrespectful toward the Dharma and the Dharma speaker, the listener incurs the fault of abandoning the Dharma.
- One does not teach the Dharma to those who, though not ill, have the head wrapped, or who hold umbrellas, staffs, or weapons, or who have the head covered.
- One does not teach the Dharma while standing to one who is sitting, or to one who is lying down; and if the speaker is a monk, teaching such a person gives rise to an offense of wrongdoing.
- The Dharma speaker must teach seated on a good seat in a clean place, without desire for gain and honor, jealousy, or laziness, and with a mind of love.
