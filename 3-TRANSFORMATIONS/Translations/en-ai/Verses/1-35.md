## Combined Commentary Summaries

The three classical commentators—Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen, Sazang Mati Panchen, and Ngulchu Thokme—demonstrate complete harmony in explaining Verse 1-35. They split their analysis of this verse into two main themes: first, how the Bodhisattva acts as the supreme field of virtue, where even a single moment of clear faith yields immense positive fruits; and second, how the Bodhisattva's mind remains completely unshakeable under great adversity, turning severe harm into a condition that naturally increases their virtue.

### 1. The Bodhisattva as the Supreme Field of Virtue (Faith and Devotion)
In contrast to the catastrophic consequences of anger explained in Verse 1-34, the commentators explain that generating faith and devotion toward a Bodhisattva creates boundless positive karma:
- **Consensus on the Superiority of Virtuous Fruit:** All three commentators agree that the positive fruit of generating clear faith and devotion toward a Bodhisattva vastly outshines the negative consequences of anger. They explain that the Bodhisattva is the "supreme field of virtue" (*དགེ་བའི་ཞིང་མཆོག*).
- **Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen on Fruits and Duration:** Gyaltsab Je states that for "far more countless eons than the moments of clear faith," the individual will experience "exceptional fruits" of happiness and high rebirth.
- **Sazang Mati Panchen on Devotion:** Sazang Mati Panchen emphasizes that if a fortunate person generates clear faith and devotion toward one who possesses the mind of enlightenment, the fruit of that faith increases far more than the fruit of any harmful thought.
- **Ngulchu Thokme's Scriptural Citation:** Ngulchu Thokme highlights this immense merit by quoting the **Sūtra of the Mudra of Entering the Definite and Indefinite** (*确定不确定入印经* / *ངེས་པ་དང་མ་ངེས་པར་འཇུག་པའི་ཕྱག་རྒྱའི་མདོ།*). The sūtra presents a dramatic comparison: if someone were to put out the eyes of all sentient beings in all the worlds, and another person with a loving mind were to miraculously restore their sight, that is an act of immense merit. Yet, a person who merely looks with a faithful, clear mind at a Bodhisattva devoted to the Mahāyāna generates far greater merit than that.

### 2. The Unshakeable Mind of the Bodhisattva Under Adversity
The second half of the verse explains that even when subjected to intense harm, abuse, or great adversity, Bodhisattvas do not react with anger, retaliation, or negativity; instead, their virtue naturally increases:
- **Gyaltsab Je's Psychological/Scholastic Analysis:** Gyaltsab Je provides a deep psychological explanation for why anger does not arise in a Bodhisattva. He explains that because anger and other mental afflictions "have no purpose in abiding in them," they do not arise in the continuum of these Heroes of Enlightenment, even under "great pressure" or "at the cost of their lives." Instead of committing negativity, their virtue increases naturally.
- **Sazang Mati Panchen on Love and Compassion:** Sazang Mati Panchen explains that even if others obstruct or make great efforts to hinder the profound Dharma of the heirs of the Buddha, "no negativity such as harm toward sentient beings ever arises in them." Instead, their virtues of love (*བྱམས་པ།*) and compassion (*སྙིང་རྗེ།*) naturally increase more and more under those very conditions.
- **Ngulchu Thokme on Patience:** Ngulchu Thokme frames this as "not being overcome by suffering." He explains that even when severe harm and suffering are inflicted upon the Bodhisattvas, they do not generate negative thoughts of anger; rather, by the power of those painful conditions, their virtue increases naturally.
- **Ngulchu Thokme's Citation of the *Compendium of Perfections*:** To support this, Ngulchu Thokme cites the **Compendium of Perfections** (*Pāramitāsamuccaya* / *སྤྱོད་པ་བསྡུས་པའི་སྒྲོན།*), which states that even when inflicted with "various fierce harms," the minds of these supreme beings do not change. Because they are "exceptionally stable through the power of patience," their devotion to accomplishing supreme enlightenment actually increases as a direct response to harm.
