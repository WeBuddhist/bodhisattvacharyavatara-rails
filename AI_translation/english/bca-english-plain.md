# Here is the Bodhisattva's Way of Life ^0

## 0. Prefatory Material ^I-0

In the language of India: Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra. ^I-1

In the language of Tibet: The Way of the Bodhisattva. ^I-2

Homage to all Buddhas and Bodhisattvas. ^I-3

## 1. Chapter One: Explaining the Benefits of Bodhicitta ^1-0

To the Sugatas endowed with the Dharma-body, together with their heirs,
and to all others worthy of homage, I bow down with reverence.
I will explain briefly, in keeping with the scriptures,
how to enter the vows of the Sugatas' heirs. ^1-1

There is nothing said here that has not been said before,
and I have no real skill in composition.
For this reason I have no intention of benefiting others;
I have written this only to familiarize my own mind. ^1-2

Through this, the strength of my faith
may for a while grow stronger;
and if another of fortune equal to mine
should also see this, it may prove worthwhile. ^1-3

This freedom and fortune is extremely hard to find;
having gained it, one has reached the chance to accomplish what matters.
If its benefit is not brought about now,
how could such an opportunity come again later? ^1-4

Just as a flash of lightning in the black night of a clouded sky
lights everything up for a single instant,
so too, through the power of the Buddha,
the world's virtuous, wise intentions arise only rarely, for a moment. ^1-5

Therefore virtue is always weak,
while the power of wrongdoing is great and truly terrible.
Except for the mind of perfect awakening,
what other virtue could ever overpower it? ^1-6

Having reflected for many eons,
the Lords of Sages saw that this alone is beneficial,
for by it the boundless multitudes of beings
easily attain the highest happiness. ^1-7

Those who wish to destroy the hundreds of sufferings of existence,
who wish to dispel the discomfort of beings,
and who wish to enjoy hundreds of kinds of happiness,
should never abandon the mind of awakening. ^1-8

The moment bodhicitta arises,
the wretched, bound in the prison of saṃsāra,
come to be called children of the Sugatas,
and become worthy of homage from the worlds of gods and men. ^1-9

Like the finest elixir that transmutes base metal into gold,
this impure body, once taken hold of by it,
is transformed into the priceless jewel of a Buddha's form.
So hold firmly to this thing called bodhicitta. ^1-10

Since the sole guide of all beings, with his immeasurable wisdom,
has examined it closely and found it precious,
all who wish to leave behind the states of wandering existence
should hold firmly to this precious bodhicitta. ^1-11

All other virtue, like the plantain tree,
withers away once it has borne its fruit.
But the tree of bodhicitta bears fruit
constantly, and only grows, without ever being exhausted. ^1-12

Even one who has committed the most terrible wrongs
can, by relying on a hero, quickly cross beyond great danger;
so why would the careful not rely
on that by which one is instantly set free? ^1-13

Like the fire at the end of an age,
it certainly burns up great evils in a single instant.
Its immeasurable benefits
were explained by the wise and loving protector Maitreya to Sudhana. ^1-14

This mind of awakening, in brief,
should be understood as two kinds:
the mind that aspires to awakening,
and the mind that engages in awakening. ^1-15

Just as one understands the difference
between wishing to go and actually going,
so the wise should understand, in due order,
the difference between these two. ^1-16

Although the aspiring mind of awakening
brings great fruit even while one is still in saṃsāra,
it does not, unlike the mind of engagement,
give rise to an unbroken stream of merit. ^1-17

From the moment one takes up that mind
with irreversible resolve,
for the sake of fully liberating
the boundless realms of sentient beings, ^1-18

from that time on, even while asleep
or otherwise heedless,
a force of merit, vast and unbroken,
will arise continually, equal to the sky. ^1-19

This teaching, together with its reasoning,
was taught by the Tathāgata himself
in response to the request of Subāhu,
for the sake of beings of lesser inclination. ^1-20

If, merely by thinking "I shall relieve
the headaches of sentient beings,"
one having such a beneficial intention
already possesses immeasurable merit, ^1-21

then how much more so for one who wishes
to dispel the immeasurable suffering of every single being,
and who wishes each and every one of them
to attain immeasurable good qualities? ^1-22

Does any father or mother
possess such a benevolent wish?
Do even the gods, the sages,
or Brahmā himself possess it? ^1-23

If these very beings have never, even in a dream,
had such a wish before, even for their own sake,
how could it possibly arise
for the sake of others? ^1-24

That precious and extraordinary mind,
which does not arise in others even for their own sake,
is a wonder unprecedented before,
born now for the sake of every being. ^1-25

How can one possibly measure
the merit of this precious mind,
the cause of joy for all who wander,
the very remedy for beings' suffering? ^1-26

If merely intending to benefit others
surpasses even the worship of the Buddhas,
how much more so the effort
to bring about the welfare of every single being without exception? ^1-27

Though beings wish to be free of suffering,
they run straight toward suffering itself.
Though they wish for happiness, out of delusion
they destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy. ^1-28

But one who fills with every happiness
those who are deprived of happiness
and full of suffering,
and who cuts through all their suffering, ^1-29

and who also dispels their delusion —
where could there be virtue equal to that?
Where could there be such a friend?
Where could there be such merit? ^1-30

If even one who repays a kindness received
is worthy of some praise,
what need is there to speak of the bodhisattva
who does good unasked? ^1-31

People praise as virtuous
one who gives a little plain food to a few beings,
feeding them for only a moment, one meal,
looking down on them as they do so, satisfying them for half a day. ^1-32

So what need is there to mention
one who constantly bestows, upon countless beings for a long time,
the unsurpassed happiness of the Sugatas,
fulfilling their every wish completely? ^1-33

The Sage has said that whoever generates
an evil thought against such a child of the Victors, a lord of generosity,
will dwell in hell
for as many eons as there were evil thoughts. ^1-34

But if one instead generates a mind of faith toward them,
the fruit of that will grow even greater still.
Even in the face of great adversity,
wrongdoing does not arise in the children of the Victors, and virtue increases naturally. ^1-35

I bow to the body of anyone
in whom that precious and sublime mind has arisen,
and I go for refuge to that source of happiness
who brings happiness even to those who harm them. ^1-36

This concludes the first chapter, entitled "Explaining the Benefits of Bodhicitta," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^1-a

## 2. Chapter Two: Confessing Wrongdoing ^2-0

In order to hold onto that precious mind,
I make excellent offering to the Tathāgatas,
to the sacred Dharma, the stainless Jewel,
and to the children of the Buddha, oceans of good qualities. ^2-1

Whatever flowers and fruits exist,
whatever kinds of medicine exist,
whatever jewels the world contains,
and whatever clear, pleasing water there is, ^2-2

mountains of precious substances, and likewise
forests, and places of solitude and delight,
trees adorned with blossoms,
and trees whose branches bend low with fine fruit, ^2-3

the fragrances found in the worlds of gods and others,
incense, wish-fulfilling trees, and jeweled trees,
harvests that grow without being sown,
and every other ornament worthy of offering, ^2-4

lakes and pools adorned with lotuses,
where wild geese cry out sweetly and pleasingly,
all things unclaimed by anyone,
extending to the very limits of boundless space — ^2-5

all these I take up in my mind and offer well
to the Sage, the supreme being, together with his heirs.
O sublime and greatly compassionate fields of merit,
think of me with love and accept these offerings of mine. ^2-6

I am destitute, without merit;
I have no other wealth to offer.
So may the Protector, who cares for the welfare of others,
accept this, by his own power, for my sake. ^2-7

To the Victor and his heirs
I offer my body entirely, forever.
Accept me fully, O supreme beings;
with reverence I will become your servant. ^2-8

Since you have taken me under your care completely,
I shall benefit beings, unafraid of existence.
I will thoroughly pass beyond my former wrongs,
and from now on will commit no further wrongdoing. ^2-9

In a bathing chamber fragrant and pleasant,
with a floor of crystal, clear and shimmering,
with beautiful pillars ablaze with jewels,
and a canopy glowing with radiant pearls, ^2-10

there, to the Tathāgatas and their heirs,
I offer a bath with many jeweled vases
filled well with fragrant, pleasing water,
accompanied by many songs and instruments. ^2-11

I dry their bodies with cloths
unequaled, clean, and perfumed with the finest scent,
and then I offer them garments
of the loveliest colors, supremely fragrant. ^2-12

With soft, fine garments of many kinds,
and with hundreds of the finest ornaments,
I adorn the noble Samantabhadra, Mañjughoṣa,
Avalokiteśvara, and all the rest. ^2-13

With the finest perfumes, whose fragrance
pervades the whole of the trichiliocosm,
I anoint the bodies of all the Lords of Sages,
radiant and blazing like well-refined, polished gold. ^2-14

To the Lords of Sages, supreme objects of offering,
I offer all pleasing flowers —
mandārava, lotus, utpala, and every fragrant kind —
arranged into beautiful, well-strung garlands. ^2-15

I offer them clouds of incense too,
their captivating fragrance spreading everywhere,
and I offer them feasts of food and drink,
every kind of divine nourishment. ^2-16

I offer rows of golden lotuses
set with precious lamps,
and on the ground anointed with perfume
I scatter pleasing flower petals. ^2-17

Immeasurable palaces, filled with pleasing songs of praise,
adorned with hanging pearls and jewels, beautiful and blazing,
ornaments that fill the whole of boundless space —
all these too I offer to those whose nature is compassion. ^2-18

Beautiful jeweled parasols with golden handles,
their rims adorned in pleasing patterns,
of fine shape and lovely to behold, held aloft —
these too I constantly offer to the Lords of Sages. ^2-19

And beyond these, may every further mass of offerings,
pleasing music and sweet melody,
gather like clouds that soothe
the suffering of every being, each in its own place. ^2-20

May a rain of jewels, flowers, and the like
fall without ceasing
upon all the sacred Dharma, the Three Jewels,
and upon stūpas and images. ^2-21

Just as Mañjughoṣa and others
made offerings to the Victors,
so too do I make offerings
to the Tathāgatas, the Protectors, together with their heirs. ^2-22

To these oceans of good qualities
I offer praise with oceans of verses.
May clouds of sweet praise
arise for them continually, without fail. ^2-23

To all the Buddhas of the three times,
to the Dharma, and to the supreme assembly,
with bodies as numerous as atoms in every field,
I bow down and pay homage. ^2-24

I bow to every foundation of bodhicitta,
and to every stūpa.
I bow to the preceptors, likewise to the masters,
and to those of supreme discipline. ^2-25

Until I reach the essence of awakening,
I go for refuge to the Buddhas;
likewise I go for refuge
to the Dharma and to the assembly of bodhisattvas. ^2-26

To the perfectly awakened Buddhas dwelling in every direction,
and to the bodhisattvas
who possess great compassion,
I join my palms and make this plea: ^2-27

Whatever wrongs I have committed
throughout beginningless saṃsāra,
in this life or in others,
whether unknowingly, or caused others to do, ^2-28

or rejoiced in, overcome
by the confusion of delusion —
seeing these faults for what they are,
I confess them from the depths of my heart to the Protectors. ^2-29

Whatever harm I have done
through afflicted body, speech,
and mind, to the Three Jewels,
to my parents, or to other teachers, ^2-30

whatever grave and unbearable wrongs
I, a sinner stained by many faults,
have committed —
all these I confess to all the Guides. ^2-31

Before I have purified this wrongdoing
I may well die first.
I pray that you protect me swiftly,
so that I may be certainly freed from it. ^2-32

This Lord of Death is not to be trusted;
he does not wait for tasks done or undone.
Whether sick or well, none of us
can rely on this fleeting life. ^2-33

Having to leave everything behind and depart,
I did not understand this,
and for the sake of friend and foe
committed many kinds of wrongdoing. ^2-34

My foes will cease to be,
and my friends too will cease to be;
I myself will cease to be;
likewise everything will cease to be. ^2-35

Like an experience in a dream,
whatever thing I have enjoyed
becomes merely a memory;
whatever has passed will not be seen again. ^2-36

Even in this present life,
many friends and foes have passed away,
yet the unbearable wrongs I did for their sake
remain, standing before me. ^2-37

Thus, not having realized
that I too am transient,
through delusion, attachment, and hatred
I have committed many kinds of wrongdoing. ^2-38

Never resting, day or night,
this life is constantly slipping away,
and no addition to it ever comes from elsewhere —
so how could one like me not die? ^2-39

Though I lie on my bed
surrounded on all sides by relatives and friends,
the feeling of my life-force being cut
I alone will have to experience. ^2-40

When seized by the messengers of the Lord of Death,
what use are relatives, what use are friends?
At that time only merit can protect me,
and that too I have not relied upon. ^2-41

O Protector, being heedless
and not recognizing such danger,
I have, for the sake of this impermanent life,
brought about many wrongs. ^2-42

Even one being led today to a place
where limbs are cut off turns pale with fear;
his mouth goes dry, his eyes grow wretched,
his whole appearance changes from before. ^2-43

How much more so when seized by the terrifying
messengers of the Lord of Death, of dreadful aspect,
and struck by the great terror of illness —
what need is there to speak of such utter misery? ^2-44

"Who will truly protect me
from this great terror?" — thinking so,
with eyes wide and bulging in terror,
I search in every direction for refuge. ^2-45

But seeing no refuge anywhere,
I fall into utter despair.
If there is no refuge to be found in that place,
what then am I to do? ^2-46

Therefore, from this very day,
I go for refuge to the Victor, the Protector of beings,
who strives for the sake of guarding all who wander,
of great power, dispelling every fear. ^2-47

Likewise I go for genuine refuge
to the Dharma he has realized in his heart,
which dispels the fears of saṃsāra,
and to the assembly of bodhisattvas. ^2-48

Trembling with fear,
I offer myself to Samantabhadra;
to Mañjughoṣa also
I offer my own body. ^2-49

To the Protector Avalokiteśvara,
whose compassionate conduct never falters,
I cry out in anguish, in a wretched voice:
"Protect me, a sinner, I pray." ^2-50

To noble Ākāśagarbha,
and to Kṣitigarbha,
and to all the Protectors of great compassion,
I cry out from my heart, seeking refuge. ^2-51

I go for refuge to the Vajra-Bearer,
at the sight of whom the hostile
messengers of the Lord of Death and the like
flee in terror in every direction. ^2-52

Before, I transgressed your command;
but now, seeing this great terror,
I go for refuge to you,
and pray that you swiftly dispel this fear. ^2-53

If one who fears an ordinary illness
must follow the physician's word,
how much more so when constantly afflicted
by the sickness of desire and a hundred other faults? ^2-54

If that alone destroys
all who dwell in this world,
and no other medicine to cure it
can be found anywhere, ^2-55

then to fail to heed the instructions
of the physician who knows all remedies,
who removes every kind of affliction,
is a matter of the most reprehensible delusion. ^2-56

If one must be careful
even at an ordinary small precipice,
how much more so before the precipice
of a thousand leagues' fall, lasting a very long time? ^2-57

It is not right to rest comfortably,
thinking, "I will surely not die today."
The time when I will cease to exist
will certainly come. ^2-58

Who can grant me freedom from fear?
How can I be certainly freed from this?
If it is certain that I will cease to exist,
how can my mind possibly be at ease? ^2-59

What is left for me now
of what I once enjoyed and which has since perished?
Yet clinging to it with strong attachment,
I acted against the words of my teacher. ^2-60

Likewise, in this present life,
I must one day leave behind friends and relatives
and depart alone to who knows where —
what use, then, is it to distinguish friend from foe? ^2-61

"How can I be certainly freed
from the suffering that comes from wrongdoing?"
This alone is what I should
think about constantly, day and night. ^2-62

Whatever wrongs I have committed
through not knowing, through delusion —
whether wrong by nature or wrong by prohibition —
whatever they may be, all of them, ^2-63

before the eyes of the Protector,
with palms joined, in fear of suffering,
bowing again and again,
I confess all of them completely. ^2-64

I pray that the Guides accept
my wrongdoing as it truly is;
since this is not good,
from now on I will never do it again. ^2-65

This concludes the second chapter, entitled "Confessing Wrongdoing," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^2-a

## 3. Chapter Three: Wholly Adopting the Mind of Awakening ^3-0

I rejoice with delight in the virtue
that brings rest from suffering to all beings in the lower realms,
and in the placing of those who suffer
into states of happiness. ^3-1

I rejoice in the virtue amassed
that becomes the cause of awakening.
I rejoice in the definite freedom
of embodied beings from the suffering of saṃsāra.
I rejoice also in the awakening of the Protectors,
and in the grounds attained by the children of the Victors. ^3-2

I rejoice with delight
in the ocean of virtue that is the generation of the mind
that brings happiness to all beings,
and in the deeds that benefit beings. ^3-3

To the Buddhas of all directions
I join my palms and make this plea:
please kindle the lamp of the Dharma
for beings lost in the darkness of suffering. ^3-4

To the Victor wishing to pass into nirvāṇa
I join my palms and make this plea:
please remain for countless eons,
and do not leave this world in blindness. ^3-5

Thus, having done all these things,
whatever virtue I have thereby amassed —
by that, may all the suffering
of every sentient being be dispelled. ^3-6

For as long as beings remain ill,
until their sickness is cured,
may I become the medicine, the physician,
and the one who nurses them. ^3-7

May I rain down food and drink
to dispel the suffering of hunger and thirst,
and in the eon of famine
may I myself become food and drink. ^3-8

For beings destitute and poor
may I become an inexhaustible treasure,
remaining before them
in the form of every kind of thing they need. ^3-9

My body, and likewise my possessions,
and all my virtue of the three times —
all these I give up without reserve
for the sake of accomplishing the welfare of all beings. ^3-10

Nirvāṇa is attained by giving up everything;
my mind seeks nirvāṇa.
If everything must be given up at once,
it is best that it be given to beings. ^3-11

I have already given this body
to all embodied beings, to use as they please.
Let them constantly kill it, revile it,
or beat it, as they wish. ^3-12

Even if they play with my body,
or make it an object of mockery and ridicule,
since I have already given this body away,
what point is there in cherishing it? ^3-13

Let them do to it whatever actions
do not cause them any harm.
When anyone thinks of me,
may it never be pointless for them, ever. ^3-14

Whether those who think of me
feel anger or feel faith,
may that itself always become
the cause that fulfills all their wishes. ^3-15

Whoever speaks ill of me,
or whoever else does me harm,
or likewise mocks and insults me —
may they all have the fortune to attain awakening. ^3-16

May I be a protector for those without protection,
a guide for those who travel the path,
and for those who wish to cross over,
may I be a boat, a ship, and a bridge. ^3-17

May I be an island for those seeking an island,
a lamp for those who desire a lamp,
a bed for those who desire a bed,
and may I become the servant of all beings who desire a servant. ^3-18

May I become a wish-fulfilling jewel, a good vase,
an accomplished mantra of knowledge, a great medicine,
a wish-fulfilling tree,
and a cow of abundance for embodied beings. ^3-19

Just as earth and the other great elements,
and space itself, remain forever,
may I too, in many different ways,
remain as a basis of life for boundless beings. ^3-20

Likewise, throughout the entire realm of beings
that extends to the very limits of space,
until all of them pass into nirvāṇa,
may I remain as a cause of their sustenance. ^3-21

Just as the Sugatas of the past
generated the mind of awakening,
and just as they, in due order,
abided by the trainings of bodhisattvas, ^3-22

so too, for the benefit of beings,
I will generate the mind of awakening,
and likewise, in due order,
I will train in those very trainings. ^3-23

Thus, having intelligently
taken up the mind of awakening with joy,
in order to increase it further still,
I praise it in this way: ^3-24

Today my life has borne fruit;
having well obtained this human existence,
today I am born into the family of the Buddhas,
and have now become a child of the Buddha. ^3-25

Now, whatever I do,
I must act in accordance with this family;
I must never bring stain
upon this flawless, noble lineage. ^3-26

Just as a blind man finds a jewel
in a heap of rubbish,
so too, by some strange fortune,
this mind of awakening has arisen in me. ^3-27

This is the supreme elixir
that destroys the Lord of Death who slays beings;
it is also the inexhaustible treasure
that eliminates the poverty of beings. ^3-28

It is also the supreme medicine
that thoroughly heals the sickness of beings;
it is the tree of rest
for beings exhausted from wandering the path of existence. ^3-29

It is the universal bridge
that carries all beings across from the lower realms;
it is the rising moon of the mind
that soothes the torment of beings' afflictions. ^3-30

It is the great sun
that dispels the cataracts of beings' ignorance;
it is the pure essence of butter
churned from the milk of the sacred Dharma. ^3-31

For travelers on the road of existence,
guests wishing to partake of the joys of happiness,
this remains close at hand as the supreme happiness,
satisfying the great guests, all sentient beings. ^3-32

Today, before the eyes of all the Protectors,
I have invited all beings to be guests of the Sugata's own happiness,
and to the happiness in between —
so let gods, asuras, and all the rest rejoice. ^3-33

This concludes the third chapter, entitled "Wholly Adopting the Mind of Awakening," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^3-a

## 4. Chapter Four: Teaching Conscientiousness ^4-0

Having thus firmly taken hold
of the mind of awakening, a child of the Victor
should always strive, without wavering,
never to transgress the trainings. ^4-1

Whatever is undertaken rashly,
or without proper examination,
even if committed to as a vow,
it is right to consider whether to do it or abandon it. ^4-2

But what the Buddhas and their heirs
have examined with great wisdom,
and what I myself have also examined again and again —
what reason could there be to turn back from that? ^4-3

If, having made such a vow,
I fail to carry it out in action,
having thereby deceived all those beings,
what kind of rebirth will I have? ^4-4

It is said that even one who merely intends in his mind
to give some small, ordinary thing,
and then fails to give it,
becomes a hungry ghost because of that. ^4-5

How much more so if, having earnestly invited
all beings as guests to the highest happiness,
I then deceive them all —
to what happy realm could I possibly go? ^4-6

The way in which even those
who abandon the mind of awakening
are nevertheless liberated
is inconceivable, known only to the Omniscient One. ^4-7

That, for a bodhisattva,
is among the heaviest of downfalls;
for if it were to occur,
it would harm the welfare of all beings. ^4-8

Whoever obstructs, even for a single instant,
the merit of this mind of awakening in another,
because they have harmed the welfare of beings,
their fall to the lower realms will be endless. ^4-9

If destroying the happiness of even a single being
brings about my own downfall,
how much more so the destroying of the happiness
of the boundless embodied beings filling all of space? ^4-10

Thus, moving back and forth between
the power of downfalls and the power of bodhicitta,
within saṃsāra, one is greatly delayed
in reaching the bodhisattva grounds. ^4-11

Therefore I must carry out with respect
exactly what I have vowed to do;
if I do not strive from this day on,
I will fall lower and lower still. ^4-12

Though countless Buddhas
who benefit all beings have already passed,
because of my own faults
I did not become an object of their healing care. ^4-13

If I continue to act in the same way now,
then again and again, in just the same manner,
I will experience the lower realms, sickness,
being bound, cut apart, and torn open, and the like. ^4-14

The arising of a Tathāgata,
the attainment of faith and a human body,
and being fit for training in virtue —
when such things are rare, when will they come again? ^4-15

Even a day like today, free of illness,
with food and no harm —
life is deceptive, lasting but an instant;
the body is like a thing borrowed for a moment. ^4-16

With conduct such as mine,
I will not even obtain a human body again;
and if I fail to obtain a human body,
there is only wrongdoing, no virtue. ^4-17

If, even when I have the fortune to practice virtue,
I still fail to act virtuously,
then when utterly bewildered by the suffering of the lower realms,
what will I be able to do then? ^4-18

If I do not perform virtuous deeds
and instead accumulate wrongdoing,
then even for a hundred million eons
I will not hear so much as the word "happy realm." ^4-19

For this very reason the Blessed One said
that it is extremely rare to obtain a human birth —
like a sea turtle's neck happening to pass
through the hole of a yoke tossed on a vast ocean. ^4-20

If a wrong committed in a single instant
causes one to dwell for an eon in the hell of no respite,
how much more so with the wrongs accumulated
throughout beginningless saṃsāra — how could one go to a happy realm? ^4-21

And having merely experienced that much suffering,
one is still not freed;
for even while experiencing it,
further wrongs continue to arise. ^4-22

Having found such an opportunity as this,
if I fail to practice virtue,
there is no greater deception than this,
and no greater folly than this. ^4-23

If, having understood this,
I again grow lax out of delusion,
then at the time of death
great anguish will arise in me. ^4-24

If my body is burned for a long time
in the unbearable fires of hell,
then unbearable regret will surely
torment my mind like a blazing fire. ^4-25

Having, by some rare chance,
found this beneficial state so hard to attain,
if I, even while possessing understanding,
am led back again to that hell, ^4-26

it is as if I had been bewildered by a spell;
I seem to have no mind of my own here at all.
I do not even know by what I am so deluded;
what is it that dwells within me? ^4-27

Enemies such as hatred and craving
have no limbs, no hands or feet;
they are neither brave nor clever —
so how have they made me their slave? ^4-28

While they dwell within my own mind,
gleefully doing me harm,
to bear this without anger
is patience misplaced, an object of reproach. ^4-29

Even if all the gods and demigods
were to rise up against me as enemies,
they would still be unable
to lead me into the fire of the hell of no respite. ^4-30

But this powerful enemy, the afflictions,
casts me into that very fire in a single instant,
a fire before which even Mount Meru
would be consumed without a trace, reduced to ash. ^4-31

No other enemy could possibly last
as long as my enemy, the afflictions,
which have neither beginning nor end,
persisting through beginningless, endless time. ^4-32

If treated well and served,
all others bring benefit and happiness;
but the afflictions, when served and relied upon,
only bring further suffering and harm. ^4-33

When such a lasting, constant enemy,
the sole cause of an ever-growing mass of harm,
remains firmly lodged within my own heart,
how could I possibly feel joy, unafraid, in saṃsāra? ^4-34

If the guardians of saṃsāra's prison,
these executioners in the hells and elsewhere,
were to dwell within the net of my clinging mind,
how could there be any happiness for me at all? ^4-35

Therefore, for as long as I have not truly
and utterly destroyed this enemy before my very eyes,
I will not abandon my effort here — just as one enraged
even by a small offense will not sleep until it is avenged. ^4-36

Those who, when arrayed for battle, are determined to destroy by force
the afflictions that by their very nature cause the suffering of death,
disregard the pain of being struck by arrows and spears,
and do not turn back and flee until their goal is achieved. ^4-37

How much more so should I, now determined
to destroy these afflictions, by nature always the enemy,
the very cause of all suffering —
not lose heart or grow discouraged, even amid a hundred sufferings? ^4-38

If even scars inflicted pointlessly by an enemy
are worn on the body like ornaments,
why should suffering harm me at all,
when I am genuinely striving to accomplish a great purpose? ^4-39

Fishermen, outcastes, farmers, and the like,
thinking only of their own livelihood,
endure the hardships of cold, heat, and so on —
so why should I not endure hardship for the happiness of beings? ^4-40

Having vowed to free
beings throughout the ten directions, to the very limits of space,
from their afflictions,
while I myself am not yet free from afflictions, ^4-41

not even knowing my own capacity,
would I not be speaking like a madman?
Therefore, in destroying the afflictions,
I must never turn back. ^4-42

To this task I will hold fast,
grasping it as a personal grudge, and do battle;
though this too is a kind of affliction,
it is one that only destroys the afflictions, and so is not to be excluded. ^4-43

Better that I be burned alive, or killed,
or even that my head be cut off,
than that I ever, in any way,
bow down to the enemy, the afflictions. ^4-44

An ordinary enemy, even if driven from the country,
settles elsewhere, gathers strength,
and then returns —
but the enemy of the afflictions is not like that. ^4-45

Once an affliction is abandoned by the eye of wisdom
and cast out from my mind, where could it go?
Where would it dwell, from which to return and harm me?
I simply lack the fortitude — my resolve is weak. ^4-46

The afflictions dwell neither in objects, nor in the senses, nor in between,
nor anywhere else — so where do they dwell, from which they harm all beings?
They are like an illusion; so let the heart set aside fear, and strive to understand this.
Why let them harm me pointlessly in the hells and elsewhere? ^4-47

Having thus reflected in this way,
I will strive diligently to accomplish the trainings just described.
If a patient does not heed the physician's word,
how can medicines ever bring about a cure? ^4-48

This concludes the fourth chapter, entitled "Teaching Conscientiousness," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^4-a

## 5. Chapter Five: Guarding Introspection ^5-0

Those who wish to guard the trainings
should thoroughly focus and guard the mind;
if this mind is not guarded,
it will be impossible to guard the trainings. ^5-1

Just as the mad elephant of the mind, if let loose,
causes harm as great as the hell of no respite,
no wild, untamed elephant here in this world
does harm to match that. ^5-2

But if the elephant of the mind is bound tightly
with the rope of ever-present mindfulness,
all fear will vanish completely,
and all virtue will come into one's hand. ^5-3

Tigers, lions, elephants, bears,
serpents, and every kind of enemy,
the guardians of the hells of sentient beings,
witches, and likewise rākṣasa demons — ^5-4

all of these are bound
simply by binding this one mind;
and by taming this one mind alone,
all of them are tamed. ^5-5

Thus it is that all fears,
and immeasurable sufferings too,
arise from the mind —
so the Truthful One himself has taught. ^5-6

The weapons in the hells of beings —
who deliberately made them?
Who made that ground of burning iron?
From where did those masses of fire arise? ^5-7

All such things, the Sage has taught,
arise from a mind of wrongdoing.
Thus, in all three worlds,
there is no other source of fear apart from the mind. ^5-8

If the perfection of generosity meant
ridding beings of their poverty,
then, since destitute beings still exist today,
how did the past Protectors ever perfect it? ^5-9

It is said that the perfection of generosity
is the intention to give away all one's possessions,
together with their fruits, to all beings —
therefore it is simply a matter of the mind. ^5-10

Where could fish and the like be sent
so that they would never be killed?
It is said that the perfection of discipline
consists in attaining the intention to refrain from harming. ^5-11

Unruly beings are as limitless as space;
they can never all be overcome.
But if this one mind of anger is overcome,
it is as if all those enemies were overcome. ^5-12

Where could enough leather be found
to cover the whole surface of the earth?
But with leather the size of just the soles of one's shoes,
it is as if the whole earth were covered. ^5-13

Likewise, external things
I cannot possibly turn back or avoid entirely;
but if I turn back this mind of mine,
what need is there to turn back anything else? ^5-14

Just as generating a single clear state of mind
can result in rebirth as Brahmā and the like,
physical and verbal actions
do not bear such fruit, since their power is weak without mind. ^5-15

Even if recitations and austerities of every kind
are practiced for a long time,
if done with the mind distracted elsewhere,
the wise say they are pointless. ^5-16

Whoever does not understand this secret of the mind,
the supreme essence of the Dharma,
though wishing to attain happiness and overcome suffering,
will wander pointlessly, achieving nothing. ^5-17

Therefore I must take hold of this mind of mine
and guard it well.
Apart from the discipline of guarding the mind,
what use are many other disciplines? ^5-18

Just as one takes great care of a wound
while dwelling amid a careless, unruly crowd,
so too, while dwelling among bad company,
one should always guard this wound that is the mind. ^5-19

If one is careful of a wound
even out of fear of its small pain,
then, fearing being crushed in hell between mountains or ground like flour,
why not guard this wound that is the mind? ^5-20

Living with such conduct as this,
whether dwelling among bad company
or even among women,
one's firm discipline will not decline. ^5-21

Let my gain and reputation,
my body, and even my livelihood be lost;
let other virtues too decline —
but never let this mind decline. ^5-22

To those who wish to guard the mind,
I say, with joined palms:
"Guard mindfulness and introspection
with all your effort." ^5-23

Just as people afflicted by illness
have no strength for any task,
so too, a mind disturbed by delusion
has no strength for any task. ^5-24

For one whose mind lacks introspection,
whatever is heard, reflected upon, or meditated on
will not remain in memory,
like water in a leaking pot. ^5-25

Even many who are learned, faithful,
and diligently zealous
become stained with the fault of lacking introspection,
and so fall into the impurity of transgression. ^5-26

The thief that is the lack of introspection
follows behind the loss of mindfulness;
though merit has been carefully amassed,
it is stolen away like by a thief, and one goes to a bad rebirth. ^5-27

This band of thieves, the afflictions,
is constantly searching for an opening;
finding one, it steals away virtue,
and destroys even the very life of a good rebirth. ^5-28

Therefore mindfulness should never be let go
from the gate of the mind, not for a moment;
if it should lapse, recalling
the harms of the lower realms, one should reinstate it at once. ^5-29

Mindfulness arises easily
for the fortunate who, through being in the company of a teacher,
following the guidance of a preceptor,
and out of fear, act with respect. ^5-30

"The Buddhas and bodhisattvas
see everything without obstruction;
I am always present
before all of them" — ^5-31

thinking in this way, one should cultivate
a sense of conscience, respect, and reverent fear as well;
and thus mindfulness of the Buddhas too
will arise again and again in that person. ^5-32

When mindfulness remains at the gate of the mind
for the purpose of guarding it,
then introspection too will come,
and even if it has lapsed, it will return. ^5-33

First, having recognized
that a mind like this is faulty,
I should then, like a piece of wood,
remain firm and unmoving. ^5-34

I should never let my gaze
wander pointlessly about;
with deliberate intention, I should always
look downward, with eyes cast down. ^5-35

To rest my gaze,
I may occasionally look about;
and if someone appears in view,
I should look at them and greet them kindly. ^5-36

To check for dangers on the road and elsewhere,
I should look repeatedly in all four directions;
and when resting, having looked ahead,
I should also look behind. ^5-37

Having checked both ahead and behind,
I should then go forward or turn back;
in this way, in every situation,
I should act after understanding what is needed. ^5-38

Having resolved, "I will hold my body this way,"
and undertaken some task,
I should from time to time check
how this body is actually positioned. ^5-39

With every effort, I should check
that the mad elephant of the mind
remains bound, without ever slipping loose,
to the great post of reflection on the Dharma. ^5-40

I should examine again and again, asking:
"Where is this mind of mine wandering?" —
so that the effort toward meditative concentration
does not slip away, even for a single instant. ^5-41

If, on occasions of danger or celebration,
it is not possible to maintain this vigilance, one may act as circumstances allow;
for it is taught that at the time of giving,
discipline may be set aside for the moment. ^5-42

Having deliberated and undertaken a task,
I should not think of anything else;
with my mind fixed single-pointedly on it,
I should accomplish that task for the time being. ^5-43

In this way, everything is done well;
otherwise, neither task is accomplished.
And the secondary affliction of lacking introspection
will not, in this way, be allowed to grow. ^5-44

When idle gossip of every kind,
and many marvelous spectacles arise,
drawing everyone's attention,
I should abandon any attachment to them. ^5-45

If I find myself pointlessly digging the earth, cutting grass,
drawing lines in the ground, and the like,
recalling the Sugata's teachings,
I should stop at once, out of fear of wrongdoing. ^5-46

Whenever I wish to move,
or wish to speak,
I should first examine my own mind,
and act appropriately, with steady awareness. ^5-47

Whenever I notice my own mind
inclined toward attachment or anger,
I should neither act nor speak,
but remain still, like a piece of wood. ^5-48

Whenever my mind becomes agitated with excitement or mockery,
or filled with pride and arrogance,
or intent on exposing others' faults,
or deceitful and evasive — ^5-49

whenever I am eager for self-praise,
or intent on belittling others,
or given to abuse and quarrelsome speech —
at such times I should remain still, like a piece of wood. ^5-50

Whenever I desire gain, honor, or fame,
or wish to seek out servants and a retinue,
or when my mind desires to be served —
at such times I should remain still, like a piece of wood. ^5-51

Whenever a mind arises that neglects others' welfare
and seeks only my own,
or that is eager to speak up selfishly —
at that time I should remain still, like a piece of wood. ^5-52

Whenever intolerance, laziness, fearfulness,
brazen and reckless talk,
or partiality toward my own side arise in my mind —
at such times I should remain still, like a piece of wood. ^5-53

Thus, having examined my mind and found it
afflicted throughout, or striving pointlessly,
I, like a hero, should then firmly
hold it in check with the appropriate remedy. ^5-54

With utter conviction, deep faith,
steadiness, respect, and courtesy,
a sense of conscience, and reverent fear,
I should strive, calm, to bring joy to others. ^5-55

I should not become discouraged
by the conflicting desires of childish beings,
but, understanding that such thoughts arise in them
because of the afflictions that have arisen, feel compassion for them. ^5-56

Acting for the sake of myself and other beings,
in ways that are blameless,
I should always hold this mind
free of self-importance, like a magical illusion. ^5-57

Reflecting again and again
on having, after a long time, finally obtained this supreme freedom and fortune,
I should hold this mind firm and unshakable,
like Mount Meru. ^5-58

O mind, if, when vultures, attached to flesh,
tug and drag this body about on every side,
you would not be displeased,
then why now do you cherish it so? ^5-59

O mind, why do you hold this body
as your own and guard it so?
Since you and it are truly separate,
what use is it to you? ^5-60

O deluded mind, why do you not instead
take hold of a clean form made of wood?
What use is there in guarding this decaying machine,
this mere collection of impure things? ^5-61

First, mentally separate away,
layer by layer, this covering of skin;
then, with the sword of wisdom,
separate the flesh from the network of bones. ^5-62

Even the bones should be separated,
and examined down to the very marrow,
asking yourself, "What essence is there in this?"
and investigate for yourself. ^5-63

If, having searched diligently in this way,
you find no essence there at all,
then why, O mind, do you still
guard this body so, out of attachment? ^5-64

You cannot eat its impurities,
nor can you drink its blood,
nor can you suck out its intestines —
so what use is this body to you? ^5-65

It would make more sense
for jackals and vultures to guard it for their food;
this human body
is fit only to be put to use. ^5-66

Even if you guard it in this way,
when the merciless Lord of Death
seizes it and gives it to the birds and dogs,
what will you be able to do then? ^5-67

If a servant is not fit for work,
one does not give him clothing and the like;
so since this body, though nourished, will pass to others in the end,
why do you exhaust yourself feeding it? ^5-68

Having paid it its wages,
let it now work for your own purpose;
but do not give it everything
when there is no benefit in doing so. ^5-69

Regard the body merely as a vessel
for coming and going;
and, for the sake of accomplishing the welfare of beings,
transform it into a wish-fulfilling body. ^5-70

Having thus gained self-mastery,
always keep a smiling countenance;
give up frowning and scowling entirely,
and be an open, sincere friend to all beings. ^5-71

Do not carelessly set down
chairs and the like with a loud noise;
nor open doors forcefully —
always take delight in being gentle and restrained. ^5-72

The heron, the cat, and the thief
move about silently and stealthily
to accomplish what they desire;
the sage should always act in the same way, with quiet care. ^5-73

Skillfully accepting, with respect and a bow of the head,
the advice of those skilled in guiding others,
and words of benefit offered unasked,
I should always be a student to everyone. ^5-74

To everything well said,
I should respond, "Well spoken."
And when I see someone performing an act of merit,
I should praise them and rejoice wholeheartedly. ^5-75

I should speak of others' good qualities behind their backs;
and when their qualities are spoken of by others, I should agree.
When my own qualities are spoken of,
I should recognize that this is simply the recognition of good qualities. ^5-76

All undertakings are done for the sake of happiness,
yet such happiness is rarely found even if bought with wealth;
so I should enjoy the happiness of rejoicing
in the good qualities and deeds accomplished by others. ^5-77

In this way, I lose nothing here,
and in future lives there is great happiness;
whereas through faults, there is only displeasure and suffering here,
and great suffering in future lives as well. ^5-78

When speaking, I should speak coherently and to the point,
clearly, in a way that is pleasing,
free from attachment and aversion,
gently, and in a fitting, measured way. ^5-79

When looking upon beings with my eyes,
I should think, "It is in dependence on these very beings
that I shall attain buddhahood,"
and look upon them directly, with a loving gaze. ^5-80

Whether motivated constantly by strong attachment to virtue,
or motivated by an antidote applied deliberately,
in either case, virtuous action brings good qualities and benefit;
but it becomes especially great when done for those who suffer. ^5-81

Acting skillfully and with enthusiasm,
I should always carry out my tasks myself;
in all undertakings,
I should not depend on anyone else. ^5-82

The perfections, such as generosity and the rest,
should be practiced in ascending order of importance;
I should not give up something greater for the sake of something lesser —
above all, I should consider the welfare of others. ^5-83

Understanding this, I should remain
always diligent in the welfare of others;
the far-seeing, compassionate ones
have even permitted, for this purpose, what is otherwise forbidden. ^5-84

One should share one's food with those who have fallen into wrongdoing,
those without protection, and those observing ascetic discipline,
eating only what is sufficient for oneself,
and give away everything except the three Dharma robes. ^5-85

This body, which practices the sacred Dharma,
should not be harmed for trivial reasons;
acting in this way, the aspirations of beings
will swiftly be fulfilled. ^5-86

Without a pure motivation of compassion,
one should not give away this body;
rather, in this life and in future lives,
it should be given for the sake of accomplishing some great purpose. ^5-87

The Dharma should not be taught to those without respect,
nor to those, though not ill, who wrap cloth around their heads,
nor to those holding parasols, staffs, or weapons,
nor to those with covered heads. ^5-88

The profound and vast teachings should not be given to those of lesser capacity,
nor should teachings be given to women without a male present;
lesser and higher teachings alike
should be treated with equal respect. ^5-89

One who is a vessel for the vast teachings
should not be directed toward lesser teachings;
one should never entirely abandon ethical conduct,
nor mislead others by citing sūtras and mantras out of context. ^5-90

Used tooth-cleaning sticks and spit
should be covered when discarded;
and it is reprehensible to relieve oneself
in water or on ground that is used by others. ^5-91

One should not eat with a full mouth, noisily,
or with the mouth wide open;
one should not sit with legs stretched out,
nor rub both hands together at once, impolitely. ^5-92

One should not be alone with another man's wife
in a vehicle, on a bed, or in a private place;
and one should observe and inquire, then avoid,
anything that would cause others in the world to lose faith. ^5-93

One should not gesture with a single finger;
rather, respectfully, with the whole right hand,
one should indicate directions
and point out the way in this manner. ^5-94

One should not wave the arms about excessively;
a slight gesture, together with a soft sound,
and small signals such as a snap of the fingers, are enough —
otherwise one becomes undisciplined. ^5-95

One should lie down to sleep in the position desired,
like the reclining posture of the Protector at his parinirvāṇa,
and from the very outset, resolve firmly
to rise again quickly and mindfully. ^5-96

Although the conduct of bodhisattvas
is said to be boundless,
one should definitely practice, to that same extent,
the conduct that trains the mind as described here. ^5-97

Three times during the day and three times at night,
one should recite the Threefold Sūtra of Confession,
and by relying on the Victors and the mind of awakening,
pacify whatever downfalls still remain. ^5-98

Whether the circumstance concerns oneself or others,
whatever the situation and whatever one does,
in whatever situation the trainings apply,
one should diligently train in them. ^5-99

There is nothing whatsoever
that a child of the Victors need not train in;
for the wise one who abides in this way,
there is nothing that does not become a source of merit. ^5-100

Whether directly or indirectly,
one should do nothing other than for the benefit of beings;
and solely for the sake of beings,
one should dedicate everything toward awakening. ^5-101

One should never, even at the cost of one's life,
forsake a spiritual friend
who is always skilled in the meaning of the Great Vehicle,
and who upholds the supreme discipline of the bodhisattva. ^5-102

One should train in how to rely on a teacher
according to the account in the Life of Śrī Sambhava;
this and other instructions taught by the Buddha
should be learned by reading the sūtras. ^5-103

The trainings are found within the sūtras,
so the sūtras should be read;
the Ākāśagarbha Sūtra in particular
should be consulted first. ^5-104

Because that text explains at length
what must constantly be practiced,
the Compendium of Trainings too
should definitely be consulted again and again. ^5-105

Or else, for now, one may consult
the briefer Compendium of Sūtras;
both of these, composed by
the noble Nāgārjuna, should be studied diligently. ^5-106

Whatever is not prohibited in one text
but taught in another should indeed be practiced;
and in order to protect the minds of ordinary people,
one should act correctly, having studied the trainings. ^5-107

To examine again and again
the state of one's body and mind —
this alone, in brief,
is the very definition of guarding introspection. ^5-108

These things must be put into practice with the body;
what is accomplished by mere words?
Does simply reading a medical text
ever actually help the sick? ^5-109

This concludes the fifth chapter, entitled "Guarding Introspection," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^5-a

## 6. Chapter Six: Teaching Patience ^6-0

Good deeds gathered over a thousand eons —
generosity, offerings to the Blissful Ones, and the like —
all of it, every bit,
a single burst of anger will destroy. ^6-1

There is no wrongdoing like hatred,
and no discipline like patience.
So I should devote myself with effort
to cultivating patience in every way. ^6-2

If I hold in mind the sharp sting of hatred,
my mind will never know peace;
I will find no joy, no happiness,
sleep will not come, and I will have no steadiness. ^6-3

Even those who depend on someone
for wealth and kindness shown to them
will rise up to kill
a lord who is filled with hatred. ^6-4

Because of it, friends and family grow tired of him;
though he is generous, they will not stay near him.
In short, there is simply no one
who lives happily while angry. ^6-5

From the enemy that is anger
come sufferings just like these;
but whoever strives and conquers anger
finds happiness now and later. ^6-6

Finding, in what was done against my wish
or in what blocked what I wanted,
the fuel for an unhappy mind,
anger flares up and destroys me. ^6-7

Therefore I must utterly destroy
the fuel of this enemy;
for this enemy has no other task
than to bring me harm. ^6-8

Whatever befalls me,
I should not let it disturb my joyful mind;
being unhappy does not accomplish what I want,
and it only makes my virtues decline. ^6-9

If there is a remedy, what use
is there in being unhappy about it?
And if there is no remedy at all,
what use is being unhappy about that either? ^6-10

For myself, or for my friends,
I do not want suffering, contempt, harsh words,
or unpleasant speech —
yet for my enemies I want the opposite. ^6-11

The causes of happiness arise now and then,
but the causes of suffering are very many.
Without suffering there is no true release from saṃsāra —
so, mind, be firm and steady. ^6-12

Fire-walkers and followers of Karṇapa endure
burning, cutting, and such, without any real purpose,
merely for some imagined freedom —
so why am I such a coward, when my purpose is true freedom? ^6-13

There is nothing at all
that does not grow easier through habituation.
So, by growing used to small harms,
I should learn to bear even great harm. ^6-14

Have I not seen how the bites of snakes and flies,
the pangs of hunger and thirst,
and afflictions like rashes and the like
are all endured, though they serve no purpose whatsoever? ^6-15

Heat, cold, rain, and wind,
sickness, captivity, being struck, and the like —
I should not be too delicate about these;
for behaving that way only makes the harm grow. ^6-16

Some, on seeing their own blood,
grow even more courageous and resolute,
while others, on seeing someone else's blood,
grow faint and lose their senses. ^6-17

This comes from firmness or weakness of mind,
a difference shaped by habituation.
So I should disregard harm
and remain unshaken by suffering. ^6-18

Even when suffering strikes, the wise
do not let the clarity of their mind be disturbed;
for in the battle against the afflictions
there is naturally much harm in the fray. ^6-19

Those who disregard all suffering
and conquer enemies such as hatred —
they are the true victors, the heroes;
the rest merely slay corpses. ^6-20

Furthermore, suffering has this good quality:
through disillusionment it dispels pride,
it brings compassion for those in saṃsāra,
caution about wrongdoing, and joy in virtue. ^6-21

One does not get angry at bile and the like,
the great source of suffering that they are;
so why be angry at beings with minds,
since they too are all driven by conditions? ^6-22

Just as sickness arises
though it is not wanted,
so too, though unwanted,
the afflictions forcefully arise. ^6-23

People do not think, "I shall now grow angry,"
and yet they grow angry;
and anger, in just the same way, arises
without ever having thought, "I shall now produce it." ^6-24

Every kind of wrong there is,
and every variety of misdeed,
arises through the force of conditions;
none of it has any independence. ^6-25

Nor do the assembled conditions themselves
intend to produce it,
nor does what they produce
think, "I am the one produced." ^6-26

Whatever is imagined as a "primal substance,"
and whatever is conceived as a "self" —
these do not arise
through any deliberate wish, "I shall arise." ^6-27

If it did not exist before it arose,
then what is it that would wish to arise?
And it could not be something
always occupied with objects and then ceasing. ^6-28

If, instead, that self were permanent,
it would clearly be inactive, like space;
and even meeting other conditions,
being unchanging, what could it possibly do? ^6-29

And if, even while acting, it stayed just as before,
what did its "acting" actually accomplish?
What connection could there be
by which one could say, "This is its action"? ^6-30

So everything is under the power of something else,
and that in turn is under the power of yet another — nothing is independent.
Understanding this, one will not grow angry
at things, which are like magical illusions. ^6-31

"But if there is no one doing the preventing,
and preventing itself makes no sense" — one might object —
"then it would be unreasonable
to hope that suffering could ever be brought to an end that way." ^6-32

So, when I see an enemy or a friend
behaving badly,
I should think, "This arose from such conditions,"
and rest at ease in that understanding. ^6-33

If things happened just as one wished,
then, since no embodied being wishes for suffering,
suffering would never arise
for anyone at all. ^6-34

Yet through carelessness, people harm even themselves
with thorns and the like;
and driven to win a woman or the like,
they grow desperate and even refuse food. ^6-35

Some hang themselves, or leap from cliffs,
eat poison or spoiled food,
and through unwholesome conduct
bring harm upon themselves. ^6-36

If, under the power of the afflictions,
people even kill themselves, whom they love most,
how then could they possibly hold back
from harming the bodies of others? ^6-37

Since the afflictions give rise to conduct
that leads people even to kill themselves,
if I cannot muster compassion for them,
what sense is there in growing angry instead? ^6-38

If it is simply the nature of childish beings
to cause harm to others,
then being angry at them makes no more sense
than being angry at fire for its nature of burning. ^6-39

And if the fault is merely incidental,
while the nature of beings is actually sound,
then anger is likewise unreasonable —
like being angry at the sky when smoke happens to fill it. ^6-40

If, struck by a stick or the like,
I grow angry at the one who wields it,
that person too is driven by hatred —
so it makes more sense to be angry at the hatred itself. ^6-41

I myself, in the past, have done
this same kind of harm to other beings;
so it is fitting that this harm
should now come to me, who has harmed sentient beings. ^6-42

Both his weapon and my body
are causes of this suffering;
he produced the weapon, and I produced this body —
so which of the two should I be angry at? ^6-43

This human form is like an open sore,
unbearable to the touch, full of suffering;
blinded by my own craving, I took hold of it —
so when it is hurt, at whom should I be angry? ^6-44

Childish beings do not want suffering,
yet they crave its very causes;
so, harmed by their own fault,
why should they resent others? ^6-45

It is like the guardians of hell,
or a forest of leaf-bladed swords —
this too was produced by my own actions,
so at whom should I be angry? ^6-46

Driven by my own past actions,
those who now harm me have appeared;
and because of this, they will go to the hells —
have I not, in this way, destroyed them myself? ^6-47

By relying on these very enemies,
and through my patience, much of my wrongdoing is purified;
but because of me, they instead
go to hell and suffer there a long time. ^6-48

I am the one who harms them,
while they, in fact, benefit me —
so why, O twisted and unruly mind,
do you grow angry in such a backward way? ^6-49

If I had the virtue of a good intention,
I would not go to hell;
but if I protect myself this way,
what becomes of them then? ^6-50

And if I retaliate and harm them in return,
they will not be protected by that,
and my own conduct will be spoiled as well,
so that my discipline will be ruined. ^6-51

The mind has no physical form,
so it cannot be destroyed by anyone in any way;
but because of its intense clinging to the body,
the body is harmed by suffering. ^6-52

Contempt, harsh words,
and unpleasant speech
do not actually harm the body —
so why, mind, do you grow so furiously angry? ^6-53

If others' displeasure with me
does not, in this life or the next,
consume me,
why then do I not want it? ^6-54

If I do not want it
because it blocks my gain,
my gain will be lost in this life regardless,
while my wrongdoing will remain firmly fixed. ^6-55

Better that I die today
than live long by wrongful means;
for even if someone like me lives long,
the suffering of death is the same in the end. ^6-56

Suppose one man dreams a hundred years of happiness
and then wakes;
and another experiences happiness
for only a moment, and then wakes — ^6-57

for both of them, once awake,
that happiness will never come back again.
Whether a life is long or short,
at the moment of death it ends in just the same way. ^6-58

Even if one gains great wealth
and enjoys happiness for a long time,
one leaves empty-handed and stripped bare,
as if robbed by a thief. ^6-59

If one says, "By gaining wealth I can live,
exhaust my wrongdoing, and do merit,"
but then grows angry for the sake of wealth,
will one not instead exhaust one's merit and pile up wrong? ^6-60

If the very purpose for which I live
is itself corrupted this way,
what use is such a life at all,
spent doing nothing but wrong? ^6-61

If I grow angry at those who speak ill of me
because it makes beings lose faith,
then why do I not grow equally angry
when unpleasant things are said of others? ^6-62

If I can tolerate others' lack of faith,
since it depends on something else,
why can I not tolerate unpleasant speech about myself,
since it too depends on the arising of afflictions? ^6-63

Even toward those who destroy and deface
images, stūpas, and the sacred Dharma,
it is not right for me to feel hatred,
since the Buddhas and such are not truly harmed by it. ^6-64

And toward those who harm
my teachers, relatives, and friends,
I should, as before, see it as arising from conditions,
and turn my anger back. ^6-65

Since embodied beings are harmed
both by things with minds and things without,
why single out those with minds for resentment?
So I should bear such harm with patience. ^6-66

Some do wrong out of delusion,
and some, out of delusion, grow angry;
which of them is without fault?
Who among them is entirely blameless? ^6-67

Why did that person, in the past,
do the deed that now leads them to harm others?
Since everything depends on past actions,
why should I resent this at all? ^6-68

Seeing things in this way,
I should strive with effort
to accomplish merit, so that
all beings come to feel loving-kindness for one another. ^6-69

For example, when a fire breaks out in a house
and spreads toward another house,
whatever fuel — straw and the like — feeds it
should be cleared away and thrown out. ^6-70

Likewise, whatever the mind clings to
that lets the fire of hatred spread
should, for fear that it will burn up my merit,
be thrown out that very instant. ^6-71

If a man condemned to die
is freed after losing only a hand, is that not fortunate?
If, through the sufferings of a human life,
one is freed from the hells, is that not fortunate? ^6-72

If I cannot even bear
this small suffering of the present,
then why do I not turn back the anger
that is the cause of the suffering of hell? ^6-73

For the sake of desires I have already endured
thousands of burnings and the like in the hells,
yet I have accomplished
neither my own good nor that of others. ^6-74

This present suffering is nowhere near as severe,
and besides, it serves a great purpose;
so I should feel nothing but joy
at this suffering that clears away harm from all beings. ^6-75

If, when others are praised for their good qualities,
I would feel joy and happiness at that,
then why, mind, do you not
feel that same joy when you are praised? ^6-76

This happiness of joy that you feel
is a source of happiness free of any fault;
it is endorsed by those with good qualities,
and it is also the finest way to draw others near. ^6-77

If you say you do not want this happiness,
this "may others too find happiness in this way,"
then you should also give up paying wages and the like,
and you will lose out, both seen and unseen. ^6-78

When my own good qualities are spoken of,
I want others to feel glad;
so when the good qualities of others are spoken of,
why do I not want to feel glad myself? ^6-79

Since I have generated the wish for awakening
out of a wish for the happiness of all beings,
why then, when beings find happiness on their own,
should I grow angry at that? ^6-80

If I claim to wish that beings become buddhas,
honored by the three worlds,
then why am I so tormented
at seeing them receive some small mark of respect? ^6-81

If a relative you support and care for
finds a livelihood through someone else,
would you not be glad, but instead
grow angry all over again? ^6-82

If you would not even wish that for beings,
then what sort of wish for their awakening do you have?
How can there be any awakening mind at all
in one who grows angry when another prospers? ^6-83

Whether that gain stays with you
or remains in the giver's own house,
either way it was never yours to begin with —
so what does it matter whether it is given to you or not? ^6-84

Why would you throw away your own merit, faith,
and good qualities?
Tell me, who does not get angry
at their own lack of self-restraint? ^6-85

Not only do you feel no remorse
for the wrongs you yourself have done,
but you even wish to compete
with others who have done good deeds!
[This marks the end of the second section.] ^6-86

Even if your enemy is unhappy,
what is there in that for you to be glad about?
Your mere wish for it
will not become a cause of harm to him. ^6-87

And even if, through your wish, his suffering
did come about, what would you have to be glad about?
If you say, "I would be satisfied" —
is there any greater ruin than that? ^6-88

That hook, cast by the fisherman of the afflictions,
is truly unbearably sharp;
once caught by it, I will be boiled
by the guardians of hell in a cauldron there. ^6-89

Being honored, praised, and attended upon
brings no increase in merit, no increase in lifespan,
no increase in strength, no freedom from illness,
and no bodily comfort either. ^6-90

If I truly understood my own real good,
what benefit would there be in it for me?
If all I want is a pleasant feeling of mind,
I might as well take up gambling and drink. ^6-91

For the sake of reputation, people squander their wealth,
and even take their own lives;
but what use are mere words?
Who could find comfort in them after death? ^6-92

When a sandcastle collapses,
children cry out in distress;
in just the same way, when praise and fame decline,
my own mind behaves just like a child's. ^6-93

Since a mere sound has no mind of its own,
it cannot possibly intend to praise me;
one might say what pleases me is the thought
that others feel joy toward me — supposedly the cause of my joy. ^6-94

But whether that joy belongs to someone else
or even to me, what benefit is it to me
if another feels joy? That joy and pleasure
belong entirely to that other person; I get no share of it. ^6-95

If I were happy because that other person is happy,
I should feel that way about everyone equally;
so why, when others feel joy in someone else,
do I myself not feel happy? ^6-96

So the joy I feel
merely because someone says "I am praised" —
this too, being unreasonable in just this way,
is nothing more than childish behavior. ^6-97

Praise and the like distract me,
and they also destroy my disillusionment with saṃsāra;
they breed envy toward those with good qualities,
and they destroy excellence as well. ^6-98

So are not those who stand ready
to destroy my praise and reputation
in fact acting to protect me
from falling into the lower realms? ^6-99

I, who seek liberation,
have no need to be bound by gain and honor;
so how could I possibly be angry
at those who free me from such bonds? ^6-100

As if by the Buddha's own blessing,
those who bar the door
against my being led into paths of suffering —
how could I possibly be angry at them? ^6-101

"But this person is an obstacle to my merit" —
even so, it is not right to be angry at them;
for there is no discipline like patience,
so should I not rely on them instead? ^6-102

And if it is through my own fault
that I fail to be patient here,
then it is I myself, and not this person,
who obstructs the very cause of merit. ^6-103

Whatever, in the absence of something, does not arise,
but arises when that thing is present,
is that thing's real cause —
so how could it be called an obstacle? ^6-104

A beggar who arrives at the right time
is no obstacle to generosity;
and one who ordains a person
cannot be called an obstacle to their ordination. ^6-105

Beggars in this world are common enough,
but those who cause harm are rare;
for if I have done no harm to others,
no one, in turn, harms me. ^6-106

So, just as an unearned treasure
appearing in one's house is a joy without effort,
I should rejoice in my enemy,
since he has become a helper in my practice of awakened conduct. ^6-107

Since it is through this enemy and myself together
that this is accomplished,
the fruit of patience should rightly be offered to him first,
since he is, in this way, the true cause of my patience. ^6-108

"But since he had no intention of helping me develop patience,
he does not deserve honor" — one might say.
But then why do we honor the sacred Dharma,
which is likewise merely a fitting cause for accomplishment? ^6-109

"If he intended to harm me,
he does not deserve honor" — one might say.
But if he instead strove to help me, like a doctor,
how then could my patience ever be accomplished? ^6-110

So, since patience arises
in dependence on a mind intensely hostile,
that hostile mind itself is the true cause of patience,
and so deserves honor like the sacred Dharma. ^6-111

For this reason the Sage has said
that sentient beings are a field, just as the Victors are a field;
many have reached the perfection of excellence
by pleasing these very beings. ^6-112

Since the qualities of a buddha arise equally
from sentient beings and from the Victors,
what principle is there
by which the Victors are honored but not sentient beings? ^6-113

The greatness of a good intention lies
not in itself, but in its result;
sentient beings too share this same quality,
and so, in that respect, the two are equal. ^6-114

Whatever offering is made to those who hold loving-kindness
is itself the greatness of sentient beings;
and whatever merit comes from faith in the Buddha
is likewise the greatness of the Buddha. ^6-115

Since sentient beings share in bringing about
the qualities of a buddha, they are held equal in that respect;
yet none are truly equal
to the Buddhas, whose oceans of qualities have no bounds. ^6-116

Even if only a small portion
of that single, supreme mass of qualities
appears in something, an offering of the three worlds
would still be too small to honor it properly. ^6-117

Since sentient beings hold a portion
of what gives rise to the Buddha's supreme qualities,
it is fitting, in proportion to that portion alone,
that they too be honored as worthy of offering. ^6-118

Furthermore, toward those who are true, guileless friends
and who bring immeasurable benefit,
what other way is there to repay them
than by bringing joy to sentient beings? ^6-119

Since they repay the kindness of those
who even give up their bodies and enter the hell of no respite for their sake,
therefore, even if these beings do great harm,
I should treat all of it as something entirely good. ^6-120

Since even my own Lord
does not spare even his own body for their sake,
how could I, so deluded toward them,
act with pride, instead of acting as their servant? ^6-121

Since the Sages rejoice when these beings are happy,
and are displeased when they are harmed,
pleasing these beings pleases all the Sages,
and harming them harms the Sages as well. ^6-122

Just as, when the whole body is ablaze with fire,
no desired object could bring the mind pleasure,
so too, when sentient beings are harmed,
there is no way to please the greatly compassionate ones. ^6-123

So, for whatever harm I have done to beings
that has displeased all the greatly compassionate ones,
today I confess each such wrong, one by one,
and pray the Sage forgive me for all I have done to displease him. ^6-124

From now on, to please the Tathāgatas,
I will become the servant of the world;
though the multitudes trample on my head
or even kill me, I will not retaliate — may the Protector of the world be pleased. ^6-125

There is no doubt that the compassionate nature
of these Protectors regards all these beings as their very self;
so why would I not show respect
to these beings, seen in the very form of the Protector himself? ^6-126

This alone is what pleases the Tathāgatas;
this alone truly accomplishes my own good;
this alone dispels the suffering of the world —
so I should always do this. ^6-127

For example, though a single official of the king
may cause harm to many people,
far-sighted subjects,
even if capable, do not retaliate against him, ^6-128

for he is not acting alone —
the king's own power stands behind him.
Likewise, I should never look down on
any weak being who causes me harm, ^6-129

for behind them stand
the guardians of hell and the compassionate ones;
so, as common people appease a fierce king,
I should strive to please all sentient beings. ^6-130

Even if a king, when angered,
were to inflict the sufferings of hell
on those who have displeased him,
could he really do that? ^6-131

But even if a king, when pleased,
gave everything he could,
could he ever grant what is attained
by pleasing sentient beings — buddhahood itself? No, that is impossible. ^6-132

Setting aside even the future attainment of buddhahood
that comes from pleasing beings,
have I not seen how, in this very life,
it brings great glory, fame, and happiness? ^6-133

Through patience, in future lives one gains beauty and the like,
freedom from illness and good repute,
an extremely long life,
and the vast happiness of a universal monarch. ^6-134

This concludes the sixth chapter, entitled "Teaching Patience," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^6-a

## 7. Chapter Seven: Teaching Diligence ^7-0

With patience gained in this way, I should take up diligence;
for awakening depends on diligence.
Just as there is no motion without wind,
there is no merit without diligence. ^7-1

Diligence means delighting in what is good.
Its opposites should now be explained:
clinging to what is bad, laziness,
discouragement, and self-contempt. ^7-2

From savoring the comfort of idleness,
from craving attached to sleep,
and from feeling no disillusionment
with the suffering of saṃsāra, laziness arises. ^7-3

Caught up by the net of the afflictions,
fallen into the trap of rebirth,
having already entered the very mouth of the Lord of Death —
do you still not understand, even now? ^7-4

Have you not seen your own kind
killed off, one after another?
And still you go on sleeping,
like a water-buffalo lulled by a butcher. ^7-5

With every road sealed off
and the Lord of Death watching you,
how can you take pleasure in eating,
and how can you take pleasure in sleep? ^7-6

Since death comes swiftly,
I should gather the accumulations while I still can.
Even if I gave up laziness only then,
what use would that be — it would already be too late. ^7-7

Thinking, "This I have not yet begun,"
or "This I have only half finished,"
when the Lord of Death arrives without warning,
one will think, "Alas, I am ruined." ^7-8

With eyes swollen from the force of grief,
face streaked with tears,
seeing relatives whose hope has run out,
and beholding the faces of Death's messengers — ^7-9

tormented by the memory of one's own wrongs,
hearing the sounds of hell,
terrified, body soiled with filth —
gone mad, what is there left to do? ^7-10

If you are already this fearful in this life,
like a living fish being gutted,
what need is there to speak
of the unbearable sufferings of hell that follow from wrongdoing? ^7-11

Even the mere touch of hot water
is unbearable to tender young skin —
so, having done deeds that lead to hell,
why do you remain so much at ease? ^7-12

Wanting results without effort,
being too delicate, suffering greatly,
like a god yet already gripped by death —
alas, such a person is destroyed by suffering! ^7-13

Relying on the boat of a human birth,
one may cross the great river of suffering.
Since this boat is hard to find again later,
fool, do not sleep — now is not the time. ^7-14

Why do you delight in distraction, in restlessness,
and in things that are the very causes of suffering,
having cast aside the supreme joy
of the boundless, sacred Dharma? ^7-15

Freedom from discouragement, and strength,
enthusiasm, and self-mastery,
equalizing myself with others,
and exchanging myself for others — ^7-16

I should never grow discouraged, thinking,
"How could someone like me attain awakening?"
For the Tathāgata, who speaks only truth,
has himself spoken this truth: ^7-17

even creatures such as bees, flies,
mosquitoes, and worms,
if they generate the strength of diligence,
can attain the unsurpassed awakening, hard as it is to reach. ^7-18

So, being born human, of a capable kind,
able to tell benefit from harm,
if I do not abandon the conduct that leads to awakening,
why should I not attain it myself? ^7-19

"But I am afraid, since I would have to give up
my hands, feet, and the like," one might say.
Without weighing what is heavy against what is light,
this is nothing but the fear of the deluded. ^7-20

For countless millions of eons,
I have been cut apart, pierced,
burned, and flayed again and again,
yet have never thereby attained awakening. ^7-21

But this suffering that goes into accomplishing awakening
has a definite limit;
it is like the pain of a surgical cut
made to remove an internal illness and clear away harm. ^7-22

All physicians cure
the discomfort of sickness through treatment;
so, in order to destroy many great sufferings,
I should bear this small discomfort. ^7-23

Yet the Supreme Physician does not
use such an ordinary method at all —
by the gentlest of means
he cures immeasurable, terrible ills. ^7-24

The Guide begins by having his students give
vegetables and such in acts of generosity;
and once accustomed to that, later on,
they gradually come to give even their own flesh. ^7-25

Once the understanding has arisen
that one's own body is like a vegetable,
what difficulty is there
in giving away one's own flesh and the like? ^7-26

By giving up wrongdoing, one is free of suffering;
through wisdom, one is free of unhappiness.
It is wrong views and wrongdoing
that harm the mind and the body alike. ^7-27

Through merit, the body is at ease;
through wisdom, the mind is at ease —
so why should the compassionate ever grow weary,
even while remaining in saṃsāra for the sake of others? ^7-28

By the strength of this mind of awakening,
past wrongs are exhausted,
and an ocean of merit is gathered;
this is why it is taught to surpass the path of the hearers. ^7-29

So, mounted on the horse of bodhicitta,
which clears away all weariness and fatigue,
and moving from joy to joy —
what person of understanding would ever grow discouraged? ^7-30

To accomplish the welfare of beings, the forces needed
are aspiration, steadfastness, joy, and letting go;
aspiration is generated by reflecting
on the sufferings and fears of saṃsāra, and the benefits of its remedy. ^7-31

So, having abandoned what runs counter to this,
and cultivating aspiration, confidence, joy, and letting go,
I should make effort to increase diligence
through the strength of enthusiasm and self-mastery. ^7-32

The faults of myself and of others
are without measure, and I must destroy them;
yet for each single fault,
an ocean of eons would be needed to exhaust it. ^7-33

If I have not even begun
to wear away a fraction of my own faults,
why does my heart not break,
facing this place of immeasurable suffering? ^7-34

The good qualities of myself and of others
are many, and I must accomplish them;
yet for each single quality,
an ocean of eons of habituation would be needed. ^7-35

Yet I have never even begun
to grow accustomed to a fraction of such qualities;
it is astonishing that I have wasted
this precious chance, so hard to find, for nothing. ^7-36

I have made no offerings to the Blessed One,
given no feast of celebration and joy,
performed no service for the teaching,
and fulfilled no wish of the destitute. ^7-37

I have given no fearlessness to the fearful,
no ease to the wretched;
I have produced nothing at all
but the pain of my mother's womb, and suffering alone. ^7-38

Because, both in the past and now,
I have lacked aspiration for the Dharma,
such destitution as this has come upon me —
who, then, would ever give up aspiration for the Dharma? ^7-39

The Sage has said that aspiration
is the root of every kind of virtue;
and the root of that, in turn,
is constantly meditating on the ripening of results. ^7-40

Suffering, an unhappy mind,
fears of every kind,
and being kept from what one desires —
all of it arises from wrongful conduct. ^7-41

Through virtue done with genuine intention,
wherever one goes,
in that very place one will be honored
with the rewards that are its fruit. ^7-42

But one who does wrong, though wishing for happiness,
wherever they go,
in that very place their wrongdoing
will cut them down with the weapon of suffering. ^7-43

Dwelling in the heart of a vast, fragrant, cooling lotus,
nourished and given splendor by the sweet sound of the Victor's speech,
one takes birth, supreme in form, from a lotus opened by the Sage's light,
becoming, through virtue, a child of the Blissful One in the Victor's very presence. ^7-44

But one whose skin is entirely flayed by the Lord of Death's messenger,
utterly wretched, body drenched in molten, blazing copper,
pierced by burning swords and spears into a hundred pieces of flesh,
falls upon ground of blazing iron — the fruit of much unvirtuous action. ^7-45

So I should hold aspiration for virtue,
and cultivate it with genuine reverence;
and, in the manner of the Vajra Banner rite,
I should build confidence in my ability to accomplish it. ^7-46

First one should examine one's own capacity,
and either begin the task or not;
it is better not to begin at all
than to begin and then turn back. ^7-47

For that habit will carry over into another life,
and wrongdoing and suffering will only grow;
and in that other life, the time for the result too
will shrink, and even that task will not be accomplished. ^7-48

One should have confidence in three things:
in action, in the afflictions, and in one's own capacity —
thinking, "I alone will accomplish this" —
this is confidence regarding action. ^7-49

This world, powerless under the afflictions,
is unable to accomplish its own good;
so beings are not capable as I am,
and therefore I myself must accomplish this. ^7-50

When others take on lowly tasks,
how could I possibly stand by, idle?
Let this not be done out of mere pride —
rather, freedom from pride is itself supreme. ^7-51

When a crow comes upon a dead snake,
it acts as bold as an eagle;
likewise, if I myself am weak,
even a small fault can bring me harm. ^7-52

For one who gives up effort, discouraged,
is there any way out of destitution?
But one who generates confidence together with effort
is hard to overcome, even for the mighty. ^7-53

So, with a firm mind,
I should overcome my downfalls;
for if I am defeated by mere downfalls,
my wish to conquer the three worlds becomes a laughingstock. ^7-54

I must be victorious over everything;
I must never be defeated by anything.
As a child of the Victors, the Lion,
I should stand firmly in just this confidence. ^7-55

Beings overcome by pride
are not the same as those with confidence over the afflictions;
those with pride fall under the power of an enemy;
these instead overpower the enemy that is pride itself. ^7-56

Puffed up by the pride of the afflictions,
one is led by that pride down to the lower realms,
stripped of the joys of human life,
and reduced to eating the scraps of others as a slave — ^7-57

dull, unattractive, and weak,
one becomes despised everywhere.
Even the ascetic puffed up with pride —
if that too counts as pride —
tell me, what could be more lowly than that? ^7-58

But whoever holds firm confidence
in order to conquer the enemy that is pride —
that one truly has confidence, a hero of complete victory,
who brings the fruit of victory to all beings, just as wished. ^7-59

Living in the very midst of the afflictions,
I should stand firm against them in a thousand ways,
like a lion among jackals,
untouched by the whole host of afflictions. ^7-60

Just as a person, even in the greatest danger,
guards their eyes above all else,
so too, even amid the greatest danger,
I should never fall under the power of the afflictions. ^7-61

Just as one grows attached to a game
for the pleasure its outcome brings,
so too should I grow attached
to whatever task is at hand, never wearied, taking joy in it. ^7-62

Even when acting for the sake of happiness,
it is uncertain whether happiness will follow;
but how could there be no happiness
in the very action that is itself the cause of happiness? ^7-63

If desires, like honey smeared on a razor's edge,
never satisfy,
then how could the ripened happiness
of peaceful merit ever fail to satisfy? ^7-64

So, to bring a task through to its end,
like an elephant scorched by the midday sun
plunging into a lake it has found,
I should throw myself fully into that task. ^7-65

When strength gives out and connection with the task is lost,
I should set it aside for a while, to take it up again later;
and once a task is properly completed,
I should let it go, looking ahead to the next. ^7-66

As in battle against a seasoned enemy,
one wards off the blade that comes at oneself,
so I should evade the weapon of the afflictions,
and press on to destroy the enemy that is the afflictions. ^7-67

If a sword falls to the ground in battle,
one snatches it up again in fear;
just so, if the weapon of mindfulness slips away,
I should quickly retrieve it, recalling the terrors of hell. ^7-68

Just as poison, carried along by the blood,
spreads through the whole body,
so too, once it finds an opening,
wrongdoing spreads through the mind. ^7-69

Like one carrying a vessel filled to the brim with mustard oil,
made to walk with care, threatened with death
by a man standing by with a sword should a drop be spilled —
just so should one of disciplined conduct hold themselves in check. ^7-70

So, just as one leaps up at once
if a snake falls into one's lap,
in just the same way, when sleep or laziness arrives,
I should drive it off without delay. ^7-71

Whenever a fault occurs,
I should reproach myself for it,
and reflect at length on how
to make sure it never happens again. ^7-72

Thinking, "How can mindfulness
become a habit in situations like these?" —
I should seek out the company that gives rise to it,
or whatever activity is fitting for it. ^7-73

So that I have strength before I take up any task,
recalling the teaching on conscientiousness,
I should make myself
light and ready to act. ^7-74

Just as a wisp of cotton is carried
by the coming and going of the wind,
so too should I be carried by enthusiasm;
only in that way will anything be accomplished. ^7-75

This concludes the seventh chapter, entitled "Teaching Diligence," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^7-a

## 8. Chapter Eight: Teaching Meditative Concentration ^8-0

Having generated diligence in this way,
I should settle the mind in meditative concentration;
for a person whose mind is scattered and distracted
lives caught between the fangs of the afflictions. ^8-1

Through the solitude of body and mind,
distraction simply does not arise.
So I should give up worldly life,
and set aside discursive thought entirely. ^8-2

Worldly life is not given up
because of attachment and craving for gain and the like;
so, in order to give these up completely,
the wise should reflect in the following way. ^8-3

Understanding that special insight, joined
with calm abiding, thoroughly destroys the afflictions,
I should first seek calm abiding,
and that in turn is accomplished through a joyful lack of attachment to the world. ^8-4

Who could rightly grow attached
to what is impermanent, when they themselves are impermanent too?
Because of that, for a thousand lifetimes
one will fail to see one's beloved again. ^8-5

If I do not see them, I find no joy,
and my mind cannot settle in meditation;
and even seeing them, I am never satisfied,
so I continue to be tormented by craving, just as before. ^8-6

By growing attached to sentient beings,
I become entirely blocked from seeing reality as it is;
my very disillusionment with saṃsāra is destroyed,
and in the end I am left tormented by grief. ^8-7

By thinking only of that one I love,
this life passes by, spent for nothing;
and because of impermanent friends and companions,
even the everlasting Dharma comes to be destroyed. ^8-8

By sharing the fortune of childish people,
one is bound to go to the lower realms;
for they lead one to states unequal to oneself —
so what use is there in associating with the childish? ^8-9

In a single instant they turn into friends,
and in a moment they turn into enemies;
they grow angry even at what should bring joy —
ordinary people truly are hard to please. ^8-10

If told something for their own good, they grow angry,
and even turn me away from doing good;
and if their own words go unheeded,
they grow angry and head toward the lower realms. ^8-11

Toward superiors, envy; toward equals, rivalry;
toward inferiors, arrogance; at praise, conceit;
at unpleasant words, anger arises —
when could any good ever come from the childish? ^8-12

In the company of the childish,
self-praise, criticizing others,
and talk of the pleasures of saṃsāra and the like
are bound to lead to some kind of wrongdoing. ^8-13

So, associating with others in this way
only leads to ruin on both sides;
they gain nothing of benefit from me,
and I gain nothing of benefit from them. ^8-14

I should therefore stay far from the childish;
but if we do meet, I should please them with a friendly manner,
without forming any close attachment,
keeping things at the level of ordinary courtesy. ^8-15

Like a bee that takes only the nectar of a flower,
I should take only what serves the Dharma from others,
and remain as though I had never known them before,
without forming any close bond. ^8-16

"I have much wealth and honor,
and many people are fond of me" —
if I hold on to conceit like this,
terror will arise once I am dead. ^8-17

So, whatever this deluded mind
becomes attached to,
in that same measure a thousandfold suffering
will arise to meet it. ^8-18

So the wise should not grow attached;
for from attachment, fear is born.
Since these things are, by their very nature, bound to be lost,
I should stay firm and understand this fully. ^8-19

Even if abundant gain has come to me,
along with fame and praise as well,
none of it — the gain, the fame, all of it —
will go with me to wherever I must go next. ^8-20

If someone else disparages me,
what joy is there for me in being praised?
And if someone else praises me,
what displeasure is there for me in being blamed? ^8-21

Since sentient beings have such varied inclinations
that even the Victors cannot please them all,
what need is there to speak of someone as lowly as me?
So I should give up worrying about the opinions of the world. ^8-22

People blame those with no wealth,
and speak ill even of those who have it;
these people are, by nature, hard to get along with —
how could any joy ever come from them? ^8-23

Since the childish never find joy
apart from their own self-interest,
the Tathāgatas have said
that no childish person is ever a true friend. ^8-24

The deer, birds, and trees of the forest
never speak unpleasant words;
when will I come to dwell
with such pleasant companions as these? ^8-25

Dwelling in a cave, an empty temple,
or at the foot of a tree,
when will I be free
never to look back, without any attachment? ^8-26

When will I come to dwell,
free and unattached,
in vast places that belong to no one,
open and unclaimed by nature? ^8-27

When will I come to dwell without fear,
carrying only a begging bowl and the like,
wearing clothes no one else wants,
with no need to hide this body at all? ^8-28

When will I go to a charnel ground
and, seeing the bones of others there
next to my own body — both subject to the same decay —
come to see them as equal, without distinction? ^8-29

For even this body of mine
will come to such a state
that its very smell will drive away
even the jackals who would otherwise come near it. ^8-30

Even this single body, though it arose all at once,
made of flesh and bone born together,
will decay and fall apart, each part going its own way —
what need is there, then, to speak of other, outside companions? ^8-31

At birth, one is born alone,
and at death, that very same person dies alone;
since no one else shares in that suffering,
what use are companions who only get in the way of practice? ^8-32

Just as travelers on a road
take up lodging along the way,
so too do travelers on the road of existence
take up dwelling in the various states of birth. ^8-33

Until such a person is carried off by four bearers,
mourned by the whole world,
one should go to the forest
before that time arrives. ^8-34

Without friend and without foe,
dwelling alone in solitude,
having already counted oneself as good as dead,
one feels no grief even when death actually comes. ^8-35

For no one nearby to grieve
would cause any disturbance,
and so nothing would pull one away
from mindfulness of the Buddha and the like. ^8-36

So, in a forest grove,
bright and joyful, needing little effort, pleasant and at ease,
where all distraction is naturally stilled,
I should dwell alone. ^8-37

Having set aside every other concern,
and with a mind fixed only on myself,
I should make effort
to settle the mind in equipoise and tame it. ^8-38

In both this world and the next,
desires bring nothing but misfortune;
here they lead to killing, binding, and cutting,
and hereafter to the hells and the like. ^8-39

For the sake of which so many pleas were once made
through go-betweens, both men and women,
and for whose sake one shrank back
from neither wrongdoing nor disgrace — ^8-40

for whose sake one even walked into danger,
and used up all one's wealth,
those very things one clings to so tightly
become the greatest source of joy — ^8-41

yet in the end they are nothing but a skeleton,
with no independence, no self of their own;
so why, wanting and clinging to them so completely,
does one not go toward release instead? ^8-42

One who at first, with great effort, lifts the veil,
looks down out of shyness;
whether it has been seen before or not,
one covers the face with a cloth. ^8-43

That very face of yours, so afflicted,
now uncovered as if for the first time,
seen laid bare by vultures pulling it away —
why then do you flee from it in terror now? ^8-44

If, when even the eyes of others merely rest on it,
you guard it so jealously,
while it is being devoured by that very thing all along —
why, O miser, do you not guard it then? ^8-45

Seeing this heap of flesh,
if vultures and others come to eat it,
why, then, when it is offered as food to others,
does one deck it out with garlands of flowers and sandalwood? ^8-46

If you are frightened even to see it
as a mere motionless skeleton,
why are you not just as afraid
when it is stirred and moved about like a walking corpse? ^8-47

From food that is all of one single kind
both excrement and saliva arise;
so why is it that you take no pleasure
in the excrement, yet delight in the saliva? ^8-48

One who is attached, confused about what is impure,
takes no pleasure in a soft pillow
of cotton, gentle to the touch,
simply because it gives off no foul smell. ^8-49

If you are so attached to this body clothed,
why would you not want it unclothed as well?
And if you have no need of it unclothed,
why then do you embrace it when it is clothed? ^8-50

If you are not attached to what is impure,
why then do you take in your arms
another person — a mere cage of bones bound by sinews,
plastered over with a mud of flesh? ^8-51

You yourself are made of much that is impure;
you constantly make use of that very impurity,
and, craving still more impurity,
you desire other bags of impurity as well. ^8-52

"I take pleasure in this flesh," you say,
wanting to touch it and look at it —
but tell me, how can you desire flesh
that is, by its very nature, entirely without mind? ^8-53

The mind that you desire
cannot itself be touched or seen;
and what can be touched has no capacity for knowing —
so what is the point of this pointless embrace? ^8-54

It is not so strange that one fails to see
the impure nature of another's body;
what is truly astonishing
is failing to see that very same impurity in oneself. ^8-55

Having set aside the youthful lotus,
opened by the sun's rays beneath a cloudless sky,
why, with a mind attached to what is impure,
does one instead delight in a cage of impurity? ^8-56

If you are unwilling to touch
ground that is soiled with filth,
how can you wish to touch a body
from which that very filth comes? ^8-57

If you are not attached to what is impure,
why then do you take in your arms
another person, born from an impure field,
and produced by that very impure seed? ^8-58

You have no desire even for a small worm
born from what is impure,
yet you desire a body impure through and through by nature,
one that itself arose from an impure birth. ^8-59

Not only do you not despise
your own impurity,
but, craving impurity,
you desire other bags of impurity as well. ^8-60

Even pleasant things like camphor,
rice, or vegetables,
once put in the mouth and then expelled,
turn the very ground filthy and impure. ^8-61

If such impurity as this,
even when plainly evident, still leaves you doubting,
then go and look at other bodies too,
cast away, impure, in the charnel grounds. ^8-62

Knowing that when the skin is peeled back
great terror arises,
how is it that, knowing this,
one still comes to feel fresh delight in that very thing? ^8-63

As for the scent applied to the body,
that too comes from sandalwood and the like, not from the body itself;
so why does one grow attached to another
because of a fragrance that actually belongs to something else? ^8-64

If it is good not to be attached to this body
precisely because of its own foul natural smell,
then why do people in the world, pointlessly infatuated,
smear it with sweet-smelling perfumes? ^8-65

But if that sweet fragrance
belongs to sandalwood, what does that have to do with the body?
Why then does one grow attached to another
because of a scent belonging to something else entirely? ^8-66

If, with long hair and nails,
yellowed, foul-smelling teeth,
and stains of filth worked into it,
the body's true, naked nature is indeed something dreadful, ^8-67

then why does one still labor so hard
grooming it, as though tending a weapon meant to harm oneself?
Through such deluded effort spent on this self,
this whole world has become maddened and thrown into turmoil. ^8-68

If seeing merely a heap of bones
already brings disenchantment in a charnel ground,
could one still delight in a city
filled with moving skeletons? ^8-69

Furthermore, such an impure thing as this
is never obtained without cost;
by striving to acquire it, one wears oneself out,
and comes to harm even in the hells and elsewhere. ^8-70

A child cannot yet earn wealth,
so how could there be any pleasure once he comes of age?
And if one measures out one's life while gathering wealth,
what use is desire once one has grown old? ^8-71

Some, base in their desire,
utterly worn out from a day's labor,
come home and lie down,
their exhausted bodies like corpses. ^8-72

Some, suffering the hardships of travel abroad,
suffering from being far from home,
though longing to see wife and children,
do not see them for years on end. ^8-73

Utterly deluded, seeking their own benefit,
some sell themselves for the very thing they want,
yet never attain it, and even achieving nothing,
are driven along by the winds of others' demands. ^8-74

Some sell their own bodies,
made powerless, forced to serve others;
and their wives, even when giving birth,
must do so beneath a tree or out in the wilderness. ^8-75

Fools, deceived by desire,
thinking, "I must make a living to survive,"
go into battle risking their very lives,
and become slaves merely for the sake of profit. ^8-76

Some, driven by desire, even have their bodies cut apart;
some are impaled upon stakes;
some are pierced through with spears;
and some can be seen being burned alive. ^8-77

Know that wealth brings boundless misfortune —
in gathering it, in guarding it, and in losing it;
those distracted by attachment to wealth
have no chance to be freed from the suffering of existence. ^8-78

For those attached to desire, faults like these
are many, and the reward is small —
like a beast of burden pulling a cart,
allowed only a mouthful of grass along the way. ^8-79

For the sake of that small reward,
which even beasts can get without difficulty,
this precious human life, so hard to find,
is destroyed by those tormented by their own actions. ^8-80

Desire is bound to end in ruin,
and leads one down to the hells and elsewhere;
since it is not something truly great,
the exhausting toil it demands at every turn — ^8-81

if even a fraction, a ten-millionth part,
of that same toil accomplished buddhahood,
then those who pursue desire suffer far more,
yet gain no awakening at all. ^8-82

Considering the sufferings of hell and the like,
one sees that desires cannot even be likened
to weapons, poison, fire, precipices,
or enemies — they are far worse than any of these. ^8-83

So, having grown disillusioned with desire,
I should cultivate delight in solitude,
in tranquil forests
free of conflict and affliction. ^8-84

In the cool of a fortunate moon's light and sandalwood,
in a broad, delightful mansion of flat stone,
stirred by the silent, peaceful mountain breeze,
one walks about, reflecting on how to benefit others. ^8-85

Dwelling as long as one wishes
in an empty house, at the foot of a tree, or in a cave,
having given up the suffering of guarding and clinging to possessions,
one acts freely, without worry, without dependence. ^8-86

To live freely, unattached,
tied to nothing at all,
content and at ease —
even the gods find this hard to attain. ^8-87

Having reflected in this and other ways
on the good qualities of solitude,
I should thoroughly still discursive thought,
and cultivate the mind of awakening. ^8-88

First of all, I should make effort
to cultivate the equality of myself and others;
since happiness and suffering are the same for all,
I should protect everyone as I protect myself. ^8-89

Just as the hand and other limbs,
though many and different, are protected as one body,
so too should all beings, different as they are,
in their wish for happiness and freedom from suffering, be held as one, the same as myself. ^8-90

Although my own suffering
does not harm anyone else's body,
that suffering is still unbearable to me,
simply because of my attachment to myself. ^8-91

Likewise, though the sufferings of others
do not actually befall me,
still, those sufferings of that other person
are hard for me to accept, if I hold a sense of self toward them too. ^8-92

I should eliminate the suffering of others
simply because it is suffering, just like my own;
and I should benefit others
simply because they are sentient beings, just like my own body. ^8-93

Since I and others alike
equally wish for happiness,
what makes me so special
that I strive for my own happiness alone? ^8-94

Since I and others alike
equally do not wish for suffering,
what makes me so special
that I protect myself but not others? ^8-95

If I do not protect against another's suffering
on the grounds that it does not harm me,
why then do I protect against my own future suffering,
which likewise does not harm me now? ^8-96

The thought "I will be the one to experience it"
is itself mistaken;
for it is one person who dies,
and an entirely different person who is born. ^8-97

If it is held that whoever experiences a suffering
should be the one to guard against it,
then the suffering of the foot is not the hand's concern —
so why does the hand protect the foot? ^8-98

"Though this may not be reasonable,
it comes about through grasping at a self," one might object.
But whatever is unreasonable, whether concerning self or other,
should equally be given up, as far as one is able. ^8-99

What is called a "mental stream" or an "aggregate"
is false, like a rosary or an army;
since there is no real owner of suffering,
who is there to have power over it? ^8-100

Since suffering has no owner,
there is no distinction of any kind among instances of it;
it should be dispelled simply because it is suffering —
what use is there in drawing boundaries here? ^8-101

"Why should the suffering of all be prevented?" —
this should not even be asked;
if it is to be prevented, then all of it should be prevented;
if not, then this holds for myself as well, just as for any other being. ^8-102

"Why generate compassion so intensely,
when it brings so much suffering with it?" one might ask.
But when one truly considers the suffering of all beings,
how could the suffering involved in compassion ever be too much? ^8-103

If it were true that the suffering of others
could be ended by taking on a single suffering oneself,
then one filled with loving compassion
should indeed bring about that suffering, for both self and other. ^8-104

So Supuṣpacandra, though he knew
the harm the king would inflict,
did not turn away his own suffering,
so that the suffering of many might come to an end. ^8-105

So, one whose mind has grown accustomed
to delighting in calming the suffering of others,
will enter even the hell of no respite,
like a wild goose entering a lotus pool. ^8-106

When sentient beings are set free,
what ocean of joy could compare to that?
Is that alone not enough?
What use is there in seeking freedom for oneself alone? ^8-107

So, even while acting for the benefit of others,
I should feel no pride or amazement about it;
since I take joy solely in benefiting others,
I have no hope of any reward in return. ^8-108

So, just as I now guard myself
even from the smallest unpleasant thing,
so too should I cultivate a protective mind toward others,
and cultivate compassion in just the same way. ^8-109

Through habituation, one comes to think
of a mere drop of semen and blood belonging to others
as "mine," even though
it has no real substance of its own. ^8-110

In just the same way, why should I not
regard the body of another as my own?
That my own body should likewise
be given over to others is no harder than this. ^8-111

Having seen myself as full of faults,
and others as an ocean of good qualities,
I should completely let go of self-grasping,
and cultivate the practice of taking up others in its place. ^8-112

Just as the hand and other limbs
are accepted as parts of the body,
why should embodied beings, likewise,
not be accepted as parts of the world? ^8-113

Just as, through habituation,
a sense of "I" arose toward this body, which has no true self,
so too, through habituation
toward other beings, why should a sense of "I" not arise as well? ^8-114

In this way, even while acting for the benefit of others,
no pride or astonishment arises;
just as when I feed myself,
I have no hope of a reward in return. ^8-115

So, just as I now guard myself
even from the smallest unpleasant thing,
so too should I cultivate a protective mind toward beings,
and grow accustomed to a mind of compassion. ^8-116

For this reason, the Protector Avalokiteśvara,
out of great compassion,
in order to dispel the fears of the multitudes of beings,
blessed even his own name with this very power. ^8-117

One should not turn back from hardship;
for through the strength of such habituation,
even something whose mere name once inspired fear
will, without it, bring no joy at all. ^8-118

Whoever wishes to swiftly protect
both themselves and others
should practice that most sacred secret:
exchanging self and other. ^8-119

Because of attachment to one's own body,
even a small danger gives rise to great fear;
who would not hate, as an enemy,
this very body that gives rise to such fear? ^8-120

Wishing to cure the ills of hunger, thirst,
and the like that afflict the body,
people kill birds, fish, and deer,
and lie in wait for them along the paths. ^8-121

For the sake of profit and standing,
some even kill their own father and mother,
and steal the wealth of the Three Jewels;
because of this they will be burned in the hell of no respite. ^8-122

What wise person would want
this body, guard it, and honor it,
rather than treat it
as an enemy, and hold it in contempt? ^8-123

"If I give this away, what will I have to use?" —
such self-concerned thinking is the way of a hungry ghost.
"If I use it, what will I have to give?" —
such thinking for the sake of others is the way of the gods. ^8-124

If I harm others for my own sake,
I will be tormented in the hells and elsewhere;
but if I harm myself for the sake of others,
I will attain every kind of excellence. ^8-125

Through wishing to raise myself up,
one falls into wretched, foolish lower realms;
but by directing that very wish toward others,
one attains a good rebirth and receives honor. ^8-126

If I make use of others for my own sake,
I will experience servitude and the like;
but if I make use of myself for the sake of others,
I will experience mastery and the like. ^8-127

Whatever happiness there is in the world
all comes from wishing for the happiness of others;
whatever suffering there is in the world
all comes from wishing for one's own happiness. ^8-128

What need is there to say more?
Just observe this distinction:
the childish act for their own benefit,
while the Sage acts for the benefit of others. ^8-129

If I do not genuinely exchange
my own happiness for the suffering of others,
buddhahood will never be accomplished,
and there will be no happiness even in saṃsāra. ^8-130

Setting aside the concerns of future lives,
even the purpose of this present life will not be accomplished
if a servant does not do their work,
and a master does not pay the wages due. ^8-131

Giving up entirely the excellent happiness
that would come from benefiting both seen and unseen,
and, through causing suffering to others,
the deluded reap unbearable suffering instead. ^8-132

Whatever harm exists in this world,
and whatever fear and suffering there is,
all of it arises from grasping at a self —
so what use is this great demon to me? ^8-133

Unless I give up this self entirely,
I will not be able to give up suffering,
just as, unless one gives up fire,
one cannot avoid being burned. ^8-134

So, in order to calm my own harm,
and to calm the suffering of others,
I should give myself over to others,
and hold others as dear as myself. ^8-135

O mind, you must know for certain
that you now belong to others;
from now on, you must think of nothing else
but the welfare of all sentient beings. ^8-136

It is not right to use what belongs to another —
the eyes and so forth — for one's own purpose;
nor is it right to turn the eyes and so forth,
now devoted to that other purpose, wrongly back toward oneself. ^8-137

So, holding sentient beings as foremost,
whatever I see in this body of mine
that could be of use,
I should take it, whatever it may be, and use it to benefit others. ^8-138

Having made those lesser than myself into "myself,"
and having made myself into "another,"
with a mind free of discursive thought,
I should cultivate envy, rivalry, and pride. ^8-139

"This one is honored, I am not;
I have not received as much as this one;
this one is praised, I am blamed;
this one is happy, I am the one who suffers." ^8-140

"I am the one who does all the work,
while this one simply stays at ease;
this one is regarded as great in the world,
while I am said to be lowly, without any good qualities." ^8-141

"What can be done, since I have no good qualities?
I am actually endowed with every good quality —
compared to some, this one is inferior;
compared to others, I myself am superior." ^8-142

"My discipline, my view, and the rest have declined
not through my own power, but through the force of the afflictions;
I must be helped in whatever way possible —
even harm done to me, I should willingly accept." ^8-143

"But if this one is not to be helped,
why is he treating me with contempt?
What use to me are his good qualities?
He is the one with good qualities, not I." ^8-144

"Standing at the very brink of the wretched, terrible lower realms,
utterly without compassion for beings,
this one, boasting outwardly of good qualities,
wishes to rival even the wise." ^8-145

"Regarding myself as equal to others,
in order to raise myself above them,
I will certainly secure my own gain and honor,
even through contention and dispute if necessary." ^8-146

"How can my own good qualities
be made known to the whole world?
And how can it be arranged that no one, ever,
hears of this other one's good qualities at all?" ^8-147

"My own faults should be concealed;
I, not he, should be the one worshipped.
I should obtain excellent gains,
and I, not he, should be the one honored." ^8-148

"I will take pleasure for a long time
in watching this one meet with misfortune,
becoming a laughingstock to the whole world,
and an object of contempt from every side." ^8-149

This self-cherishing mind even claims
to rival me directly —
does it possess learning and wisdom equal to mine?
Does it equal me in beauty, family, or wealth? ^8-150

Hearing my own good qualities
proclaimed to everyone in this way,
I will thrill with delight, hair standing on end,
and fully enjoy such happiness. ^8-151

Even if this one has some wealth,
if he must work for me,
I should give him just enough to live on,
and take the rest by my own power. ^8-152

I should strip this one of his happiness,
and constantly inflict harm upon him;
for it is he who, in a hundred ways,
has done me harm throughout saṃsāra. ^8-153

O mind, though you have spent countless eons
wishing only to benefit yourself,
all that great exertion of yours
has brought about nothing but suffering. ^8-154

So, now resolve firmly
to devote yourself entirely to the welfare of others;
the words of the Sage are never false —
you will come to see the benefit of this in time. ^8-155

If you had undertaken this task
at an earlier time,
you would never have found yourself
in a state lacking the perfect happiness of buddhahood. ^8-156

So, just as you have taken up
a mere drop of another's semen and blood
and grasped it as "I,"
so too should you become habituated to doing the same for others. ^8-157

Having become a close observer of others,
whatever you find useful in your own body,
take that very thing
and use it to benefit others. ^8-158

"I am happy, this other is unhappy;
I am elevated, this other is lowly;
I am the one who helps, this other does not" —
why does this not provoke envy toward myself? ^8-159

"I should give up my own happiness,
and take on the suffering of others" —
whenever this thought arises,
I should examine it as if it were my own fault. ^8-160

Even a wrong committed by someone else
should be turned into a fault of my own;
and even a small fault of my own
should be openly confessed before many people. ^8-161

I should overshadow my own reputation
by proclaiming the reputation of others;
I should treat myself as the lowest of servants,
made to work for the benefit of all. ^8-162

This self is by nature full of faults;
I should not praise it for any incidental good quality;
in every way possible,
I should ensure that no one ever comes to know of its good qualities at all. ^8-163

In short, whatever harm you, self-cherishing mind,
have done to others for your own sake,
let all of that harm instead
fall upon myself, for the sake of all sentient beings. ^8-164

This mind should not be roused
into becoming harsh and unruly;
rather, like a newly wedded bride,
it should be held in check with modesty and caution. ^8-165

"Act like this; remain like that;
do not act like this" —
in this way, this mind should be kept under control,
and punished if it steps outside these bounds. ^8-166

But if, even after being instructed in this way,
you, O mind, still refuse to comply,
then, since you are the source of every fault,
it is you alone who must be punished. ^8-167

"That earlier time when you destroyed me
is now gone, a thing of the past;
I see you now — where can you go?
I will crush all your arrogance." ^8-168

"Give up now, once and for all,
the thought that I still have some purpose of my own;
I have already sold you to others —
do not grieve, but offer yourself in service without complaint." ^8-169

"If, through carelessness,
I do not hand you over to sentient beings,
you will surely be handed over instead
to the guardians of hell." ^8-170

"For you have betrayed me like this before,
and I have suffered long because of it;
now, recalling all these old grievances,
I will crush your self-serving intentions once and for all." ^8-171

If, then, I wish for my own happiness,
I should not seek happiness for myself alone;
and if I wish to protect myself,
I should constantly protect others instead. ^8-172

The more and more
this body is pampered and indulged,
the more and more
it falls into ever greater delicacy and fragility. ^8-173

And once it has fallen into such a state,
not even the whole of this earth
has the power to satisfy its wants —
so who could possibly satisfy them? ^8-174

For one whose wants cannot be fulfilled,
the afflictions and a degraded resolve will only increase;
but one who expects nothing from anything
will never run short of excellence. ^8-175

So, one should not indulge
the body's desires by giving them free rein;
whoever does not cling to what is pleasing
possesses the finest thing there is. ^8-176

In the end reduced to nothing but ashes,
inert, to be moved only by another —
why should I hold to a sense of self
in this unbearable, impure form? ^8-177

Whether it is alive or already as good as dead,
what use is this mechanical contraption to me?
What difference is there between it and a clod of earth?
Alas, why can I not dispel this pride? ^8-178

Through such coddling of the body,
I have needlessly piled up great suffering;
what use is there in attachment or anger
toward something no different from a piece of wood? ^8-179

Whether I nurture it in this way
or it is instead devoured by vultures,
it feels neither attachment nor aversion —
so why then do I feel attachment toward it? ^8-180

If it feels no anger when reviled,
and no gratification when praised,
then, since it has no such awareness at all,
for whose sake have I worn myself out caring for it? ^8-181

"Whoever desires this body
is my friend," one might say.
But since everyone desires their own body,
why should I not feel affection for all beings equally? ^8-182

So, without any attachment,
I should give up this body for the benefit of beings;
though it has many faults,
I should keep it only as a tool for accomplishing this task. ^8-183

So, enough of childish conduct —
I should follow in the footsteps of the wise;
recalling the teaching on conscientiousness,
I should drive away sleep and mental dullness. ^8-184

So, in order to remove obscurations,
I should turn my mind back from wrong paths,
and constantly settle it in meditative equipoise
upon its proper object. ^8-185

This concludes the eighth chapter, entitled "Teaching Meditative Concentration," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^8-a

## 9. Chapter Nine: The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0

All these branches of practice
were taught by the Sage for the sake of wisdom.
So those who wish
to calm suffering should give rise to wisdom. ^9-1

Relative and ultimate —
these are held to be the two truths.
The ultimate is not within the reach of the intellect;
the intellect itself is said to be the relative. ^9-2

In this regard, the world is seen as twofold:
that of the yogin and that of the ordinary person.
Of these, the world of the ordinary person
is overturned by the world of the yogin; ^9-3

and yogins too, through differences of insight,
are overturned by those higher still,
by means of examples both sides accept,
without examining the actual point at issue. ^9-4

The ordinary person sees things as real entities,
and takes them to be ultimately so,
not like an illusion —
this is the very point on which the yogin and the world dispute. ^9-5

Even what is directly seen — form and the rest —
is established by common agreement, not by valid means of knowing;
it is false, just as what is impure
is commonly agreed to be pure, and so on. ^9-6

"In order to lead the world toward the truth,
the Protector taught things
as existing, though they do not
even last a single instant — is this not a contradiction, even conventionally?" ^9-7

There is no fault in the yogin's conventional view;
compared to the world, they see what is real.
Otherwise, the world would refute
the yogin's seeing of a woman as impure. ^9-8

"Merit comes from a Victor who is like an illusion,
just as it would from one who truly exists.
But if a sentient being is like an illusion,
how can it be reborn after dying?" ^9-9

For as long as conditions come together,
for that long even an illusion will occur.
How, simply because a continuum lasts longer,
could a sentient being be said to truly exist? ^9-10

Killing an illusory person and the like
involves no wrong, since it has no mind;
but merit and wrong do arise
toward one who possesses an illusion-like mind. ^9-11

Since mantras and the like have no power to produce a mind,
an illusory mind does not arise from them;
an illusion produced from many different conditions
is itself, correspondingly, of many kinds. ^9-12

There is no single condition
anywhere with the power to produce everything.
"If ultimately there is nirvāṇa,
and conventionally there is saṃsāra, ^9-13

then even the Buddha would belong to saṃsāra —
so what use would the conduct of a bodhisattva be?"
As long as the continuum of conditions is unbroken,
even an illusion will not cease; ^9-14

but once the continuum of conditions is broken,
it will not arise even conventionally.
"When even the confusion that sustains it is gone,
by what could the illusion then be apprehended?" ^9-15

"When you yourself have no illusion at all,
what, then, is there to apprehend?
And if that illusion is something other than mind,
then its very form is nothing but mind itself." ^9-16

"When mind itself is illusion-like,
then who sees what, by what?"
The Protector of the World himself has said
that mind cannot see mind. ^9-17

Just as a sword's edge cannot
cut itself, so too with the mind.
"But just as a lamp truly
lights up its own nature —" ^9-18

a lamp does not, in that sense, illuminate itself,
since it was never obscured by darkness to begin with.
Blue, like a crystal, does not depend
on anything else in order to be blue. ^9-19

Likewise, some things are seen
to depend on something else, and some not.
What is not itself blue
does not turn itself blue by its own doing. ^9-20

"A lamp illuminates" — this
is known and said by a knowing mind;
but "mind itself is luminous" —
by what is this known, that one could say such a thing? ^9-21

Since no one at all ever sees it,
whether it is luminous or not luminous
is as pointless to argue about
as the charms of a barren woman's daughter. ^9-22

"If there were no self-awareness,
how could consciousness be remembered?"
Memory arises through a connection
with something experienced elsewhere, like the poison from a rat's bite. ^9-23

"Since it is seen directly, in conjunction
with other conditions, it is self-evidently luminous."
But applying an effective eye-salve
does not make the salve itself visible, even though the vase becomes visible. ^9-24

Whatever is seen, heard, or known
is not what is to be refuted here;
what is to be refuted here is holding it
to be truly existent, since that is the cause of suffering. ^9-25

"Illusion is not other than mind,
yet it is also not held to be not-other.
If it were truly real, how could it be not-other than mind?
If it is not other, then it cannot be a true entity." ^9-26

Just as an illusion is not real,
yet still perceived — so too the mind that perceives it.
"If saṃsāra had some real basis,
then, being other than it, it would be like space." ^9-27

How could something unreal
have the capacity to act, in dependence on something real?
Then your mind would have to exist
entirely without any companion, all on its own. ^9-28

"When mind is free of any object grasped,
then all beings would be Tathāgatas.
Even so, what benefit is there
in positing mind-only as the ultimate?" ^9-29

"Even understanding it as like an illusion,
how would that reverse the afflictions?
For even the maker of an illusion
can grow attached to an illusory woman he himself has conjured." ^9-30

This is because the illusion-maker
has not abandoned the habitual imprint of affliction toward the object known;
so when he sees her,
his habitual imprint of emptiness is still weak. ^9-31

By growing habituated to the imprint of emptiness,
the habitual imprint of belief in real entities is abandoned;
and by growing habituated to "there is nothing at all,"
that too, later on, comes to be abandoned. ^9-32

When the thing to be examined, thought not to exist,
is no longer found even as a non-existent,
how could that non-entity, with no support at all,
remain before the mind? ^9-33

When neither an entity nor a non-entity
remains before the mind,
then, since there is no other alternative,
having no object at all, the mind grows utterly still. ^9-34

Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-fulfilling tree
fulfills every wish,
so too, through the power of the aspirations of those to be guided,
the form of the Victor appears. ^9-35

For example, a shrine built
to a garuḍa, though the garuḍa has long since passed away,
continues, even long after,
to calm poison and the like. ^9-36

Likewise, a shrine built to the Victor
in keeping with the conduct of a bodhisattva
continues to accomplish every purpose,
even after the bodhisattva has passed into nirvāṇa. ^9-37

"How can offerings made to something without mind
bear any fruit at all?"
Because it is taught that offerings made to a Buddha,
whether present or passed into nirvāṇa, are said to be the same. ^9-38

Whether conventionally or ultimately,
the offering bears fruit because scripture speaks of a resulting fruit —
just as, for example, scripture speaks
of a fruit arising from a truly existing Buddha. ^9-39

"One is freed by seeing the truths;
what use, then, is there in seeing emptiness?"
Because scripture states that without this path
there is no awakening at all. ^9-40

"If the Great Vehicle is not established as authentic,
then how is your own scripture established?"
Because this scripture is accepted as valid by both sides,
while the Great Vehicle is not accepted by you from the start. ^9-41

Whatever grounds you have for trusting your scripture
apply equally to the Great Vehicle.
If something were true simply because two parties agree on it,
then the Vedas and the like would have to be true as well. ^9-42

"The Great Vehicle is disputed" — if that is the objection,
then, since your own scriptures are disputed by non-Buddhists,
and other scriptures are disputed
even among your own schools, they too should be rejected. ^9-43

"The root of the teaching is the ordained monk himself" —
yet even being a true monk is hard to establish;
for minds that still have an object of grasping
find even nirvāṇa hard to attain. ^9-44

"If liberation comes through abandoning the afflictions,
then liberation should follow right away."
Yet even without the afflictions,
the power of past actions is still seen at work in such beings. ^9-45

"For now, it is certain
that craving, the condition for grasping rebirth, is absent in an arhat."
Even if that craving is not itself an affliction,
why should it not be present, like delusion in general? ^9-46

Craving arises from the condition of feeling,
and such feeling is indeed still present in them;
a mind that still has an object to grasp
will continue to remain, in some form, in such beings. ^9-47

A mind without the realization of emptiness,
even once it has ceased, will arise again,
just as in the meditative absorption without perception —
so emptiness must be cultivated. ^9-48

"If you hold that whatever teaching enters the sūtras
is accepted as the Buddha's word,
then why do you not likewise accept
that most of the Great Vehicle resembles your own sūtras?" ^9-49

"If the whole is faulted because one part is missing,"
then, since a part resembling the sūtras is found in the Great Vehicle,
why should not the whole of it
be accepted as spoken by the Victor? ^9-50

Whatever teaching was not fathomed
even by Mahākāśyapa and others,
who are you to reject it
simply because you yourself have not understood it? ^9-51

For the sake of beings tormented by delusion and suffering,
freedom from the extremes of attachment and fear,
and even the accomplishment of remaining in saṃsāra for their benefit —
this is the very fruit of emptiness. ^9-52

So no valid objection at all
can be raised against the position of emptiness.
So, without any hesitation,
one should meditate on emptiness. ^9-53

Emptiness is the remedy
for the darkness of the obscurations of the afflictions and of knowledge;
so how could one who wishes to swiftly attain omniscience
fail to cultivate it? ^9-54

If fear arises from whatever entity
gives rise to suffering,
how could fear ever arise
from emptiness, which calms suffering? ^9-55

If there were some kind of self,
then fear of anything at all might arise.
But since there is no self whatsoever,
who is there to feel fear? ^9-56

Teeth, hair, and nails are not the self;
the self is not bone or blood;
it is not mucus, nor phlegm;
nor is it lymph or pus. ^9-57

The self is not fat or sweat;
nor are the lungs or liver the self;
the other internal organs too are not the self;
the self is not excrement or urine. ^9-58

Flesh and skin are not the self;
warmth and breath are also not the self;
the cavities of the body are not the self;
and in every way, the six consciousnesses too are not the self. ^9-59

If the awareness of sound were permanent,
it would apprehend sound at all times.
If there is no object to know, what is aware?
By what could it then be called "a knowing"? ^9-60

If something without knowing were called knowing,
then even wood would absurdly become a knower.
So it is certain that, without the presence
of an object to be known, there is no knowing. ^9-61

If that same consciousness could know form,
why would it not also, at that time, be hearing?
"Because sound is not present to it" —
then, in that case, its knowing of sound too would be absent. ^9-62

How could something whose very nature
is to apprehend sound come to apprehend form?
One continuum is imagined as father and son,
but this is not ultimately true; ^9-63

for indeed, resolve, dust,
and darkness are neither son nor father;
and that consciousness which apprehends sound
is not seen to have such a nature either. ^9-64

Like an actor who appears in various guises,
that same thing is not permanent across those roles.
If it were truly one thing appearing in different modes,
this oneness has never before been seen in such a case. ^9-65

If the different modes are not themselves ultimately real,
then state what its own true nature actually is.
If you say it is simply "knowing itself,"
then it would absurdly follow that all beings are one and the same. ^9-66

Even things with and without mind
would become one, since mere existence is common to all;
and if the specific differences are illusory,
what, then, would be the common ground for their being alike? ^9-67

What is without mind is not the self,
since it lacks mind, like a vase and so on.
"But it is the self because it possesses mind" —
if so, then upon becoming aware, it would absurdly cease to be unaware. ^9-68

If the self undergoes no change even while thinking,
what did the thought actually do to it?
So that which lacks knowing and lacks any activity at all
would be turned into a "self" just like space. ^9-69

"If there is no self, then it is unreasonable
for actions and their results to be connected;
for once an action has been done and has ceased,
whose action would it then be?" ^9-70

Since the basis of an action and the basis of its result differ,
and since there is no inherently existing self performing the action either,
this point is established for both of us alike —
so is this not, then, a pointless dispute? ^9-71

It is not possible to observe a cause
as possessing its effect at the very same moment;
it is only in dependence on a single continuum
that something is called "agent" and "experiencer." ^9-72

A past or future mind is not the self,
since it does not exist at present.
And if the mind that has just arisen were the self,
then, once it has ceased, that self too would cease to exist. ^9-73

Just as, for example, when a banana tree
is cut apart piece by piece, nothing essential is found within it,
so too, when searched for through careful analysis,
no self is found to be ultimately real. ^9-74

"If sentient beings do not ultimately exist,
then for whom is compassion to be cultivated?"
It is for the sake of the result, accepted as a working premise,
that beings are posited, through a deluded but useful designation. ^9-75

"If there are no sentient beings, then whose result would it be?"
True — yet even so, it is accepted, through that same conventional delusion,
for the sake of calming suffering;
the delusion regarding the result should not be dispelled. ^9-76

The pride that is the cause of suffering
grows through delusion about a self.
"If there is no reversing this" —
even so, meditation on selflessness is the supreme remedy for it. ^9-77

The body is not the shin,
nor is it the thigh or the waist;
the belly and back are not the body;
the chest and shoulders are not the body either. ^9-78

the ribs and hands are not the body;
the armpits and shoulder blades are not the body;
the internal organs, too, are not it,
and the head and neck are not the body —
so, among all these, what actually is the body? ^9-79

If this body is held to exist
in part, distributed throughout all of these,
then the parts would simply reside among the parts —
so where would the body itself, as a whole, actually reside? ^9-80

If this body, as a single whole, resides
in the hands and other limbs,
then there would be as many bodies
as there are hands and other such parts. ^9-81

If there is no body either inside or outside,
how could a body exist in the hands and the rest?
And since there is nothing apart from the hands and the rest,
how could such a body exist at all? ^9-82

So the mistaken notion of "body"
arises, through delusion, toward the hands and other parts, which have no body of their own —
just as, because of the particular shape given to a heap of stones,
the mind mistakes it for a person. ^9-83

For as long as the conditions come together,
the body appears like a person;
likewise, for as long as the hands and other parts remain present,
the body appears to exist in relation to them. ^9-84

In the same way, the hand too, being a gathering of fingers,
what, in truth, is it?
It too is a gathering of joints,
and each joint can itself be divided into its own parts. ^9-85

Those parts, too, can be divided into particles,
and each particle can be divided further, into directional parts,
and even a directional part, having no parts of its own,
is, like space, in the end without existence either. ^9-86

So who among the discerning
would grow attached to a form that is like a dream?
And since, in this way, there is no ultimately real body at all,
what, then, is male, and what is female? ^9-87

If suffering itself truly existed as such,
why would it not harm even the utmost joy?
And if pleasure truly existed as such,
why would delicious food and the like not bring joy even to one overwhelmed by grief? ^9-88

"It is not felt because it is overpowered
by something more powerful" —
then that which has no capacity to be felt at that moment —
how could it still be called a feeling at all? ^9-89

"It exists in a subtle form even then, only its coarse form having been removed;
what remains is merely a form of subtle pleasure" —
if that is claimed, that subtlety too belongs to that very pleasure or suffering itself,
and the same analysis applies to it. ^9-90

"If its opposing condition has arisen,
then suffering has not truly arisen" —
is this not simply an admission
that what is taken as "feeling" is merely a mistaken conceptual overlay? ^9-91

For this very reason, one should cultivate
this discerning analysis as its remedy;
the meditative concentration born from such analysis
is indeed the very food of the yogin. ^9-92

"If there is a gap between the sense faculty and its object,
where could the two of them ever meet?
And if there is no gap, they would be one and the same —
so what, then, would meet with what?" ^9-93

One particle cannot enter another,
since they are of equal size, with no room for one within the other;
without entering, there can be no mixing,
and without mixing, there can be no meeting. ^9-94

How can it even make sense to speak
of "meeting" for something that has no parts at all?
If you have seen a case of meeting
between two things without parts, please show me. ^9-95

It is simply not reasonable for consciousness,
which has no physical form, to "meet" with anything;
nor can an aggregate meet, since it too has no true existence,
just as was analyzed before regarding the body. ^9-96

If, in this way, there is no such thing as contact,
then from what could feeling possibly arise?
What, then, is the point of all this effort,
and what could possibly harm what? ^9-97

Since there is no truly existing experiencer,
and no truly existing feeling either,
then, having seen this true state of affairs,
why should craving not be turned back? ^9-98

What is seen, and what is touched,
are themselves, like a dream or an illusion, mere appearances;
since they arise together with the mind that perceives them,
they are not truly seen by that feeling as something separate. ^9-99

Because the perceiving mind arises either before or after its object,
it can only be remembered, never truly experienced at the same time;
it does not experience itself directly,
nor is it experienced by anything else either. ^9-100

There is no truly existing experiencer of any kind at all;
so that feeling itself is not ultimately real either.
So how could this gathering, which has no self,
possibly be harmed by such a feeling? ^9-101

Mind does not reside in the sense faculties,
nor in form and the like, nor in between;
it is not found within as the mind itself, nor without,
nor anywhere else either. ^9-102

What is not the body, not other than it,
not mixed with it, nor separately anywhere else —
what is, in this sense, nothing whatsoever —
this itself is the very nature of sentient beings: nirvāṇa itself. ^9-103

"If knowing exists prior to its object of knowledge,
in dependence on what does it arise?
And if knowing and its object arise together,
in dependence on what does it arise?" ^9-104

"And if knowing arises after its object,
then from what would that knowing arise?"
In this way, the arising
of all phenomena cannot be established as truly occurring. ^9-105

"If it is like this, then there is no conventional reality at all —
so how, then, could there be two truths?
And if beings exist merely through another's convention,
how could a sentient being ever pass into nirvāṇa?" ^9-106

This is merely a conceptual construction in another's mind;
it is not that being's own conventional reality.
Later, once it is confirmed within their own experience, it will be said to exist for them;
if not, it simply remains without conventional existence for them. ^9-107

Conceptual thought and its object of analysis
are mutually dependent on one another;
just as, in dependence on common convention,
every kind of philosophical analysis is expressed. ^9-108

But if one were to analyze
the analyzing mind itself through further analysis,
then that analysis too would need yet another analysis,
and so there would be no end to it. ^9-109

Once the object to be analyzed has been fully analyzed,
there remains no further basis for the analyzing mind itself;
and since there is no basis, it does not arise;
this itself, it is said, is nirvāṇa. ^9-110

Determining, on whose view, these two — an object and its knowing — are ultimately real
is an extremely difficult matter.
"If the matter is established by the power of a knowing mind,
then what basis is there for that mind's own existence?" ^9-111

"And if the existence of the knowing mind is established by its object,
then what basis is there for the object's own existence?
If the two exist through mutual dependence,"
then, when analyzed, both would turn out not to exist at all. ^9-112

"If there is no son, there can be no father;
then from what would the very notion of 'son' arise?"
Just as, without a son, there is no father,
so too, in just this way, neither of the two ultimately exists. ^9-113

"A sprout arises from a seed,
and by that very sprout the seed is inferred to have existed;
so why, in just the same way, could the existence of an object
not be inferred from a cognition that arises from it?" ^9-114

"It is by a different cognition, apart from the sprout,
that the existence of the seed is inferred;
so by what, then, is it inferred
that the very cognition which knows the object actually exists?" ^9-115

For now, ordinary people directly perceive, through the senses,
all such causes as seed and sprout;
the various forms of, for example, a lotus stalk
are produced by a corresponding variety of causes. ^9-116

"What produced the variety in the cause?"
It comes from an earlier variety of cause in turn.
"How is a cause capable of producing an effect?"
Through the power of an even earlier cause. ^9-117

"If Īśvara were the cause of the world,
then first tell me, what exactly is Īśvara?"
"The elements," you say — granted, that may be so,
but then why exhaust yourself over a mere name? ^9-118

Even so, earth and the other elements are many,
impermanent, inert, not divine,
and are things to be trodden underfoot, and impure —
so they cannot be Īśvara. ^9-119

Īśvara is not space, since space is inert;
nor is he the self, since that has already been refuted above.
And as for an agent whose nature is inconceivable,
what use is it to speak of something inconceivable? ^9-120

What, then, is it that he is said to have created?
The self, along with the elements and so on?
Is not Īśvara's own nature permanent, and thus incapable of change?
Knowing arises from its object, ^9-121

and happiness and suffering arise, without beginning, from past actions —
so tell me, what, then, did Īśvara actually produce?
If a cause has no beginning,
how could its effect have a beginning either? ^9-122

Why does he not create constantly,
since he depends on nothing else in order to act?
If there is nothing at all besides him that he has not already made,
then on what could he possibly depend to act at all? ^9-123

"If he depends on a coming-together of conditions,
then it is that combination of conditions which is the real cause, not Īśvara."
If he depends on conditions, he has no power to withhold creating when they are present;
and without them, he has no power to create at all. ^9-124

If Īśvara creates while not wishing to,
then he would be under the power of something else;
and if he creates because he wishes to, he depends on that wish —
so in what sense, then, could he be called sovereign, "Īśvara," at all? ^9-125

Those who claim that partless particles are permanent
have likewise already been refuted above.
The Sāṃkhyas hold that a permanent "primal nature"
is the cause of the world; ^9-126

the qualities called sattva, rajas, and tamas,
existing in a state of balance,
are said to be called "primal nature";
when that balance is disturbed, the world is said to arise. ^9-127

It is not reasonable for a single thing
to have three natures at once; so it does not exist as a single, unitary primal nature.
Likewise, the guṇas themselves do not ultimately exist,
since each of the three is itself further threefold. ^9-128

And if the guṇas do not exist,
then the existence of sound and the rest becomes utterly implausible too.
It is also impossible for something without mind, like cloth,
to possess qualities such as pleasure. ^9-129

"Things share the very nature of their cause" —
but has this not already been thoroughly analyzed and refuted?
If you say your cause is itself pleasure and the rest,
then it is not really true that things like wool and cloth could arise from that. ^9-130

Pleasure and the rest, you claim, arise from things like wool;
but without the guṇas being separately real, there can be no pleasure and the rest.
And pleasure and the rest are never observed
to be permanent in any case. ^9-131

If pleasure and the rest truly, manifestly exist as claimed,
why is that experience of them never actually grasped as such?
"It has become subtle," you say —
but then how can it be both coarse and subtle at once? ^9-132

Having given up its coarse form and become subtle,
that coarseness and subtlety themselves show impermanence;
so why, in just the same way, should one not accept
that all things are impermanent? ^9-133

If the coarse form is not other than the pleasure itself,
then it follows that pleasure, manifestly, is impermanent.
"If you claim that something utterly non-existent
cannot arise, since it is simply non-existent," ^9-134

then you must accept that things arise even without first being manifest in latent form,
which you do not wish to accept, yet its remaining unmanifest within the cause is exactly what you assert.
If the effect already resides fully within the cause,
then one who eats food would be eating something impure. ^9-135

With the price of cloth, buy instead
the seed of cotton, and wear that.
"The deluded world simply fails to see this,"
you say — but that is only because they lack your kind of special wisdom. ^9-136

That kind of special wisdom is also present in ordinary people —
so why, then, do they not see it too?
If ordinary perception is not a valid means of knowing,
then what it perceives manifestly cannot be true either. ^9-137

If ordinary valid cognition is not truly valid,
then would not what it measures also be false?
For this same reason, then, meditation on emptiness
would, by your own logic, likewise be unreasonable to reject. ^9-138

Without first coming into contact with the imputed thing to be negated,
its non-existence cannot be apprehended.
So whatever false entity is first conventionally posited —
the non-existence of that entity is, in just the same way, conventionally false as well. ^9-139

So, when a woman dreams that her son has died,
the thought "he does not exist"
is itself an obstacle to the thought
that he does exist — and that very thought, too, is false. ^9-140

So, through analysis in this way,
nothing whatsoever is found to exist without a cause,
nor does anything reside
in its conditions, whether taken singly or gathered together. ^9-141

Nor has it come from anywhere else,
nor does it remain anywhere, nor does it go anywhere.
What difference is there between this,
which the deluded take to be real, and a mere illusion? ^9-142

Whatever is conjured by an illusionist,
and whatever is conjured by ordinary causes —
one should ask where such things
have come from, and where they go. ^9-143

Whatever is seen only in dependence
on the nearness of something else, and not otherwise,
is like a reflection in a mirror —
how could that possibly be said to be truly real? ^9-144

For something that already exists as an entity,
what need is there for a cause at all?
And for something that does not exist,
what use, again, is a cause? ^9-145

Not even a hundred million causes
can turn a non-entity into an entity;
for how could something in that state be an entity?
And what else, then, could possibly become an entity? ^9-146

If an entity cannot exist at the time of non-existence,
when, then, could it ever come to exist?
For as long as the entity has not yet arisen,
that non-entity does not depart to make way for it. ^9-147

And if the non-entity has not departed,
there is no opportunity for the entity to arise;
nor can the entity itself become non-existent while remaining what it is,
for that would mean it absurdly had two natures at once. ^9-148

So there is, in truth, no cessation,
and likewise no true entity either;
so all these beings
are, at all times, unborn and unceasing. ^9-149

Beings are like a dream;
when examined, they are like a banana tree, with nothing at its core.
In their true nature, there is no difference at all
between those who have passed into nirvāṇa and those who have not. ^9-150

Given that all things are empty in this way,
what is there to gain, and what is there to lose?
Who is there to be honored,
and who is there to be treated with contempt? ^9-151

From what source could pleasure or pain arise?
What is there to be displeased about, and what to delight in?
If one searches for its true nature,
who is it that craves, and for what does it crave? ^9-152

When examined, in this world of the living,
who is it that will die here?
What is yet to come, and what has already come to pass?
Who, indeed, are one's relatives, and who are one's friends? ^9-153

May all of us, like myself,
come to grasp that everything is like space.
Those who wish for their own happiness,
because of the causes of quarreling and revelry, ^9-154

become thoroughly agitated or overjoyed,
and, through anguish, striving, and conflict,
through cutting and wounding one another,
live a life of great hardship, weighed down by wrongdoing. ^9-155

Coming again and again to fortunate realms,
and enjoying much happiness there,
they nevertheless fall, after death, into the unbearable
and long-lasting sufferings of the lower realms. ^9-156

In saṃsāra there are many precipices;
in this regard, the truth of things is not as it appears;
and there, too, views of what is real contradict one another,
so that in existence, nothing of this kind is truly so. ^9-157

Furthermore, that condition is an ocean of suffering,
incomparable and unbearable, beyond all limit;
and within it, one's strength is so slight,
and one's life so short as well. ^9-158

There too, even to sustain life and health,
one endures the exhaustion of hunger and toil,
sleep, and injury, and likewise
the pointlessness of associating with the childish. ^9-159

Life passes by swiftly and to no purpose,
while genuine insight is extremely hard to attain;
and there, too, what means is there
to reverse the deeply ingrained habit of distraction? ^9-160

There, too, Māra strives diligently
to cast beings down into the great lower realms;
there, too, wrong paths abound,
and it is hard to cross beyond doubt. ^9-161

Freedom and fortune, once lost, are hard to find again,
and the arising of a Buddha is exceedingly rare to encounter;
the flood of the afflictions is hard to give up —
alas, how suffering follows upon suffering, without end! ^9-162

Even amid such intense suffering as this,
these beings, immersed in a river of suffering,
fail to see their own suffering for what it is —
alas, how truly worthy of grief they are! ^9-163

Just as someone might repeatedly bathe,
and then repeatedly throw themselves into fire,
so too, dwelling in a state of such intense suffering,
they nonetheless imagine themselves to be happy. ^9-164

So, acting as though free from old age and death,
those who dwell in such states
are, from the very outset, already condemned,
soon to fall into the unbearable depths of the lower realms. ^9-165

When, for those scorched by the fire of such suffering,
will I bring relief
through a rain of my own accumulated happiness,
born well from the cloud of merit? ^9-166

When will I, with reverence, gather the accumulation of merit
in a manner free of any conceptual reference point,
and teach emptiness
to those who are ruined by clinging to reference points? ^9-167

This concludes the ninth chapter, the Perfection of Wisdom, from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^9-a

## 10. Chapter Ten: Dedication ^10-0

By the virtue I have gained
through composing this work on entering
the conduct of a bodhisattva,
may all beings enter into the conduct of awakening. ^10-1

May all those, in every direction,
suffering in body and mind,
find, through my merit,
oceans of joy and happiness. ^10-2

For as long as they remain in saṃsāra,
may their happiness never fail;
may all beings ceaselessly find
the unbroken happiness of the bodhisattvas. ^10-3

Whatever hells there are
throughout the realms of the world,
may the beings there
find joy in the happiness of the blissful realms. ^10-4

May those worn down by cold find warmth;
may those worn down by heat be cooled
by the boundless rivers that pour forth
from the great clouds of the bodhisattvas. ^10-5

May the forest of leaf-bladed swords
become for them a dense grove of sandalwood;
may the trees of the śālmalī forest
grow instead as wish-fulfilling trees. ^10-6

May those regions of hell become lovely,
rich with lakes fragrant with lotus,
made beautiful by the sweet calls
of ducks, geese, and swans. ^10-7

May those heaps of burning coals turn into heaps of jewels;
may the scorched ground turn into a floor of crystal;
may the crushing mountains of hell turn
into mansions of offering, filled with the Blissful Ones. ^10-8

May the rain of embers, stones, and weapons,
from this moment on, become a rain of flowers;
and may the striking of one another with weapons,
from this moment on, become a playful scattering of flowers. ^10-9

May those sunk in the bottomless river, as in a pit of coals,
their flesh all consumed, their bones the color of the kunda flower,
through the strength of my virtue attain bodies like the gods',
and dwell at ease within the river, together with goddesses. ^10-10

"Why is it that here the men of the Lord of Death, and the crows and terrible vultures, no longer bring fear?
Whose is this fine power that clears away all darkness and gives rise to joy and happiness?" —
looking up, may they see, blazing in the midst of the sky, Vajrapāṇi himself,
and, filled with great joy, freed from wrongdoing, may they come to join him. ^10-11

Seeing a rain of flowers fall, mingled with scented water,
quenching with a hiss the burning coals of hell,
and thinking, "What is this sudden relief of happiness?" —
may the beings in the hells of the mind then behold Padmapāṇi. ^10-12

"Friends, cast off your fear, and come quickly, this way, to me!" —
by whose power all suffering is cleared away and the force of joy arrives,
who protects all beings everywhere, in whom bodhicitta and loving-kindness have arisen —
who has come, this blazing youth with the topknot, dispelling all fear? ^10-13

"Look — his lotus feet honored by the crowns of a hundred gods,
his eyes moistened with compassion, a rain of many flowers falling on his head,
in a delightful many-storied palace, praised in the songs of a thousand goddesses" —
seeing such a Mañjughoṣa, may the beings in the mental hells now cry out in joy. ^10-14

So, seeing the roots of my virtue arise as a cloud,
unobstructed, cool, fragrant, and pleasing,
raining down like Samantabhadra and the other bodhisattvas,
may the beings in the hells rejoice greatly. ^10-15

May animals be freed
from the fear of devouring one another;
may the hungry ghosts become as happy
as the people of the northern continent. ^10-16

May the hungry ghosts be satisfied,
and always cooled and refreshed by bathing,
in the stream of milk
flowing from the hand of noble Avalokiteśvara. ^10-17

May the blind come to see forms,
and the deaf always hear sounds;
may pregnant women give birth without harm,
just as the goddess Māyā did. ^10-18

May the naked find clothing;
may the hungry find food;
may the thirsty find water
and other pleasant drinks. ^10-19

May the destitute find wealth;
may the grief-stricken find joy;
may the despairing find new heart,
and steady resolve, and every excellence. ^10-20

May all sick beings
be swiftly freed from their sickness;
may every illness that afflicts beings,
without exception, never arise again. ^10-21

May the frightened become fearless;
may the bound be set free;
may the powerless gain strength;
may all hearts turn kindly toward one another. ^10-22

May all travelers, in every direction,
find happiness on their way;
and whatever purpose they travel for,
may it be accomplished without effort. ^10-23

May those who set out in boats and great ships
have their hearts' wishes fulfilled;
may they reach the shore in safety,
and rejoice there together with their kin. ^10-24

May those lost on desolate roads
come upon fellow travelers,
and, free from fear of bandits, tigers, and the like,
travel on unwearied, at ease. ^10-25

In wastelands and other fearsome, trackless places,
may children, the elderly, and the unprotected,
and those overcome by sleep or drunkenness,
be watched over by the gods. ^10-26

May all be freed from every hardship,
endowed with faith, wisdom, and loving-kindness,
with excellent food and conduct,
and may they always remember their past lives. ^10-27

May everyone possess wealth without end,
like a treasury as vast as the sky,
and, free of quarrel and harm,
enjoy it freely, without constraint. ^10-28

May those beings of little splendor
come to have great splendor;
may ascetics of unpleasant form
become excellent and beautiful in body. ^10-29

May all the women of the world
come to have the state of men;
may the lowly rise to high station,
yet remain free of pride. ^10-30

By this merit of mine,
may every sentient being, without exception,
having given up all wrongdoing,
constantly do what is virtuous. ^10-31

May they never be parted from bodhicitta,
and stay devoted to the conduct of awakening;
may they be fully embraced by the Buddhas,
and give up all the deeds of Māra. ^10-32

May the lifespan of all these beings
be immeasurably long;
may they always live in happiness,
and may the very sound of the word "death" disappear. ^10-33

May every direction everywhere
be filled with groves of wish-fulfilling trees,
resounding with the sweet Dharma
proclaimed by Buddhas and their bodhisattva heirs. ^10-34

May the ground everywhere
be free of gravel and the like,
smooth as the palm of a hand,
and of the very nature of lapis lazuli. ^10-35

May the great assemblies of bodhisattvas,
seated in their ninefold maṇḍalas,
adorn the surface of the earth
with their own excellence, and remain there. ^10-36

May every embodied being ceaselessly hear
the sound of the Dharma
from birds, from trees, from every ray of light,
and even from the sky itself. ^10-37

May they always come to meet
with Buddhas and their bodhisattva heirs,
and, with boundless clouds of offering,
honor the guides of all beings. ^10-38

May the gods send rain in its proper season,
and may harvests flourish in abundance;
may rulers govern in keeping with the Dharma,
and may the world everywhere prosper. ^10-39

May medicines be potent,
and the chanting of secret mantras effective;
may ḍākinīs, rākṣasas, and the like
be filled with hearts of compassion. ^10-40

May no sentient being ever suffer,
do wrong, fall ill,
be despised, or lose heart. ^10-41

May monasteries flourish, well adorned
with study and recitation;
may the saṅgha always dwell in harmony,
and may the purposes of the saṅgha be accomplished. ^10-42

May monks who wish to train
find the solitude they seek;
having given up all distraction,
may their minds grow workable and settle in meditation. ^10-43

May nuns have what they need,
and be free of conflict and harm;
and likewise may all who have gone forth
never decline in their discipline. ^10-44

May those distressed over broken discipline
constantly exhaust their wrongdoing;
and may they attain a fortunate rebirth,
without their vows ever declining even there. ^10-45

May the learned be honored,
and always receive alms with ease;
may their minds be perfectly pure,
and their good name spread in every direction. ^10-46

Without ever undergoing the suffering of the lower realms,
and without having to endure hardship,
may all beings, with bodies surpassing even the gods',
swiftly attain buddhahood. ^10-47

May all sentient beings, again and again,
make offerings to all the Buddhas,
and, through the inconceivable happiness of the Buddhas,
may they always dwell in happiness. ^10-48

May the bodhisattvas' intentions
for the welfare of beings be fulfilled;
and whatever the Protectors intend for beings,
may that come to pass for them. ^10-49

Likewise, may the solitary buddhas
and the hearers also come to happiness. ^10-50

And may I too, through the kindness of Mañjughoṣa,
until I reach the ground of Great Joy,
always remember my past lives,
and attain the life of a renunciant. ^10-51

May I live sustained
by even the simplest food,
and in every life
find the perfect conditions for a life of solitude. ^10-52

Whenever I wish to see him,
or even to ask some small question,
may I behold the Protector Mañjughoṣa himself,
without any obstruction at all. ^10-53

In order to accomplish the welfare
of all beings throughout the ten directions, to the very limits of space,
just as Mañjughoṣa acts,
may my own conduct be just the same. ^10-54

For as long as space endures,
and for as long as the world remains,
so long may I too remain,
dispelling the sufferings of the world. ^10-55

Whatever suffering there is for beings,
may all of it ripen upon me;
and through the community of bodhisattvas,
may the world enjoy happiness. ^10-56

May the teachings, the sole medicine for beings' suffering,
the very source from which all happiness arises,
long endure,
provided with support and honor. ^10-57

I bow to Mañjughoṣa,
through whose kindness virtuous thought has arisen in me;
and I bow also to my spiritual friend,
through whose kindness I have flourished. ^10-58

This concludes the tenth chapter, entitled "Dedication," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^10-a
