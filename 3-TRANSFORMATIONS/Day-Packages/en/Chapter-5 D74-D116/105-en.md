## 1. Today's Challenge (from the practice-plan track)

<!-- challenge:opening -->
### Opening

Today's practice is based on the seventy-ninth, eightieth, and eighty-first verses of the fifth chapter. We train to look at others with loving eyes and to speak gentle words.

<!-- challenge:tradition -->
### From the Tradition

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable example, which is easy to understand and encouraging. For example, a person tormented by thirst feels cooled by drinking cold water. In the same way, we think with an honest mind, "Relying on these very beings, I will become a buddha." Like a mother who sees her child, we look with love. We must not look down on others, belittle them, or despise them. We must look in an honest and loving way.

<!-- challenge:practice -->
### Today's Practice

**Practice:** Today, every being I see, I will look at them with loving and honest eyes.

**Explanation:** *(Doing good)* Relying on these beings, we ourselves will become buddhas. So we must not belittle anyone. We must look at them with loving eyes, as a mother looks at her child. If we do this, great loving-kindness will arise in our mind.

#### Interlinear Gloss (Khenpo Zhenga's annotation commentary)

When speaking, one should speak so that one is heartfelt and one's words hang together; so that doubts are cut and the meaning is clear; so that it is pleasing because it satisfies the mind; having abandoned attachment and hatred; and, being free of harsh words, gently, and in a way that is suitable, neither too much nor too little.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

As for how to practice speech: when speaking to other beings, one must speak in a heartfelt way, from the depths of the heart; with the meaning connected from earlier to later; connected to the occasion; clear, so that the meaning is easy to understand; pleasing to the other person and reasonable; having abandoned bad motivations such as one's own attachment and hatred; gentle in speech, and suitably detailed or brief. As for the loudness or softness of the voice, one must speak at a suitable volume, unless the other person has a hearing impairment.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-79]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Also when others ask questions and one answers, or at any other time of speaking, one should speak with alertness, in a heartfelt way, that is, so as to win the other's mind, in accord with whichever of virtue or non-virtue one is inclined toward; trustworthy, so that one's earlier and later words do not contradict each other, and thus connected; not giving rise to doubt, so that the meaning is clear; and, because the other person's mind is glad, pleasing; abandoning talk of attachment to one's own side and talk that displeases through hatred toward the other side, and the like; and, with a gentle tone of voice and manner of speaking, speaking in a suitable amount, neither too much nor too little. This one must do.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-79]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Also when speaking with others, one should speak in a way that is heartfelt from the depths of the heart; connected, with earlier and later [words] and so on in relation; easy to understand and clear in meaning, so that it settles in the mind and is therefore pleasing; with a motivation that has abandoned attachment and hatred; and with gentle words, suited to the time and in a suitable amount — exactly as taught in the Sutra on the Ten Grounds.

##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

When speaking with others too, one should speak words that are heartfelt from the depths of the heart on both sides — trustworthy, or worthy of trust. These too should be connected: earlier and later words not contradicting each other, or related to the context; easy to understand and dispelling doubt, so that the meaning is clear; agreeable to the mind and settling in it, and so pleasing; and, at the time of speaking, the motivation should be without any fault of attachment or hatred. ##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

When others ask questions about the Dharma, worldly matters, and so on, and on the occasions of speaking, such as giving answers, one should likewise speak with what is called "beneficial speech endowed with six branches." In brief: speaking in a heartfelt, settled, and unhurried way; being connected, through such means as having no repetition or fault in earlier and later words; with the subject matter extremely clear, and pleasing — good speech that is agreeable in nature; likewise abandoning words of attachment to one's own side and hatred toward the other side; with the manner of expression extremely gentle; and in quantity not too much and not too little, but speaking just a suitable amount.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** The commentators agree that when speaking to others one must speak in a way that is trustworthy and connected between earlier and later words; clear in meaning and pleasing; having abandoned the bad motivations of attachment and hatred; and gentle, with a suitable amount of words and a suitable strength of voice.

**Key points.**

- One must speak from the depths of the heart, in a way that is trustworthy, with earlier and later words not contradicting each other and connected to the context.
- One must speak so that the meaning is clear, by dispelling doubts and making it easy to understand, and so that it is pleasing and agreeable to the listener's mind.
- One must utterly abandon the bad motivations of attachment to one's own side and hatred toward the other side.
- Free of harsh words, one must speak gently and tenderly, with a suitable amount of words — neither too much nor too little — and with a suitable loudness of voice.
- One should rely on the words "like honey" and "like a flower" taught in the *Letter to a Friend*, and abandon words "like filth."

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus, likewise, taking one's physical expressions as an example: when looking at other beings with the eyes, too, without looking down on them, belittling them, or despising them, thinking, "Relying on these objects, I myself will become a buddha, and I will attain the state of buddhahood," one must look in an upright and loving way. In connection with that, whatever virtue it may be, great power can arise through the force of the distinctions of field, of motivation, and of time.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC20_TG_bo.toc.md#^5-80]]

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

When looking with the eyes at any beings — high or low, strong or weak — "Relying on these very beings, I will first generate bodhicitta, then, in between, engage in the bodhisattva's conduct of the two accumulations, the six perfections and so on, and in the end attain the state of buddhahood." Knowing them in this way as companions and spiritual friends for the bodhisattva's conduct, one must look, without making hateful eyes and the like, as one looks at one's child — without deceit, not crooked but upright, in a manner endowed with the motivation of love.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC19_KKP_bo_segmented.md#^5-80]]

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

When looking at beings with the eyes too, just as one tormented by thirst is cooled by drinking cold water, thinking, "Relying on these very beings, I will become a buddha," with an upright attitude and with thoughts of love for beings, one should look.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_GDR_bo_segmented.md#^5-80]]

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

When looking at beings with the eyes, thinking, "Relying on these very beings, I will become a buddha," one does not look at their faults or with crookedness, and so, being upright, one should look at them in a loving way.

Sources: [[1-SOURCES/Commentaries/Transcluded/BCAC14_NTS_bo_segmented.md#^5-123]]

<!-- cm:sabzang -->
##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

When looking with the eyes at beings who have come into view, thinking, "Relying on benefiting these beings and gathering the accumulations, I will become the fruit, buddhahood," one should look without deceit and without crookedness, and so uprightly, with a motivation endowed with love, having abandoned thoughts of the three poisons.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** When looking at beings with the eyes, one should look in an upright and loving way, without looking down on them and without deceit, with the thought, "Relying on these beings, I myself will attain the state of buddhahood."

**Key points.**

- Whichever beings one looks at, one must think of them as those who benefit one: "Relying on these, I will first generate the mind of awakening, in between train in the six perfections, and in the end attain buddhahood."
- One must look having abandoned the thoughts of the three poisons, pride, and jealousy, without looking down on them, belittling them, or despising them.
- As when looking at one's child, one must look with an upright, uncrooked, loving motivation.
- One should look with a supremely joyful mind, just as one tormented by thirst is cooled by cold water.
- If one abides in such conduct of speech and sight, one becomes able to accomplish the welfare of others as well, by oneself.

#### Commentary Explanations

<!-- cm:tenzin-gyatso -->
##### His Holiness the Dalai Lama (Teaching on Entering the Bodhisattva's Way of Life)

Thus: the distinction of time means being diligent constantly, not only once or twice. The distinction of motivation means being prompted by the thought of strong fixation, of intense aspiration, or being prompted by a powerful motivation such as the roots of virtue of non-attachment, non-hatred, and non-ignorance. The distinction of field means the field of qualities, such as the Three Jewels; the field of benefactors, such as one's parents in this life; and the field of the suffering, such as the destitute. If one accomplishes virtue focusing on these, that virtue brings great power.

Respect for parents and elders is not only a worldly custom but something very important. For that to come about, first, in proportion to how much the parents have cared for the child with love, as if it were the nature of things, the child comes to show reverence and to repay their kindness. Likewise, the elders, without looking down on the young, should pay attention to those young people, whose intelligence is bright, and care for them with love; and if the young try to take up the experience the elders have, good mutual relations can come about.

Then, helping the field of the suffering — the destitute and the sick — is extremely important. In the future, when we have come together and settled as a community, it would be good if the Tibetan monastics, men and women, like the monks and nuns of Jesus, took care of hospitals, schools, and the elderly and infirm within society. This would be done within the sangha, as a division of labor made on the basis of "རབ་ལ་ཚེ་དང་སྒྲུབ་པ་མཉམ་པ". Beyond that, one group would take on maintaining and upholding the Buddha's teaching from generation to generation within the monasteries and monastic colleges, and another group would have to take on responsibility for education in the schools and provide service there. Moreover, however pure the quality in which the Buddha's teaching abides within the monasteries, if few in society know about it and take an interest, it will decline to that extent. So if someone appears who, in the Buddhist way, bearing the signs and dress of ordination, is able to give explanations, then to that extent definite benefit can come. Then, if another group can provide help and care for the elderly, the infirm, the sick, and the helpless, then, if what is said in the books — "great virtue arises toward those who suffer" — can be put into practice, that is good.

*(Editorial note: the phrase རབ་ལ་ཚེ་དང་སྒྲུབ་པ་མཉམ་པ in this block is unclear in the rail and is left untranslated.)*

<!-- cm:kunpal -->
##### Khenpo Kunzang Pelden (Nectar Drops)

Whatever virtue one accomplishes, with the distinction of time — constantly, that is, unbroken; the distinction of motivation — prompted by the strong force of intense aspiration such as faith and compassion, by fixation, or prompted by the antidote; the distinction of the antidote — for instance, in giving, by giving what one oneself most cherishes and loves, it is certain to become the antidote to miserliness; likewise in ethics, by abandoning whatever one is most attached to, and so on, it is certain to become the antidote to the factors opposed to the six perfections; and the distinction of the field — when giving and so on is done toward the object endowed with qualities, the field of the Three Jewels; toward the field of benefactors, one's own parents; and toward the field of those who suffer, far-travelling guests, those long ill, and so on — the virtue becomes greater than giving to others. The same holds for non-virtue.

<!-- cm:gyaltsab -->
##### Gyaltsab Darma Rinchen (Gateway for Bodhisattvas)

Doing virtue constantly and continuously; being prompted by fixation, that is, doing it with intense aspiration; being prompted by an antidote to the opposing factors, such as non-attachment; and, toward the supreme field — the field endowed with qualities, the Three Jewels and so on — and toward the field of benefactors, parents and so on, and toward the field of the suffering, the destitute and so on, giving: the virtue becomes great, so one should make an effort.

<!-- cm:ngulchu-thogmed -->
##### Ngulchu Thogme (Ocean of Good Explanations)

Thus: "constantly" is the time, that is, continuous; "motivation, fixation" is intense aspiration; "prompted by the antidote" is an antidote able to crush its opposing factors; "field" is the field of qualities, buddhas and so on, the field of benefactors, parents and so on, and the field of the suffering, the sick and so on. Relying on these, virtue becomes great, and non-virtue is likewise. In the *Letter to a Friend*: "Constant, with strong fixation, with no antidote, / and an action arising from a basis chiefly endowed with qualities — / virtue and non-virtue are great in these five ways; / therefore strive to practice virtue." So he taught.

##### Sabzang Mati Panchen (Clear Illumination)

The distinction of time: doing it constantly, without interruption. The distinction of motivation: being prompted by aspiration and by intense fixation; or the distinction of the antidote: through the great power of non-attachment and of meditating on emptiness and so on — the training itself —; the distinction of the field: toward those endowed with qualities, buddhas and so on, and toward parents and so on as the field of benefactors, and focusing on the sick and so on as the field of the suffering, the virtue gathered by giving and so on, even if small, comes to have a great and immeasurable fruit; and non-virtue is the same in manner. <!-- cm:minyak-kunzang-sonam -->
##### Minyak Kunzang Sonam (Inexhaustible Treasury Vase)

The distinction of time: doing it constantly and continuously. The distinction of motivation: thinking, "I must do this," repeatedly making it one's own commitment (yidam), being prompted by the strong force of an aspiration prompted by fixation, or again being prompted by an antidote — for attachment, non-attachment as the antidote, and for grasping at true existence, meditation on emptiness and so on. The distinction of the object: toward the special field of qualities, the Three Jewels and so on; the field of benefactors, parents and Dharma teachers and so on; and the field of the suffering, the sick, the destitute, and so on — toward these, the roots of virtue gathered through offering, giving, and so on, though done with little material and in a short time, are virtue that becomes the cause of vast results arising over a long time. Virtue endowed with such distinctions becomes a root of virtue that is especially superior to virtue lacking those distinctions, one that is exceedingly powerful. And since it is said that non-virtue is likewise, one should abandon every side of non-virtue and strive to gather powerful virtue, and
So it was said. The meaning of that is as follows. Doing that action constantly is the distinction of application. Doing it with strong fixation, through faith and so on, or attachment and so on, is the distinction of motivation. The absence of an antidote that would destroy that action is the distinction of the antidote. The basis of benefit, parents and so on, and the basis chiefly endowed with qualities, such as the Three Jewels — these are the two distinctions of basis. An action endowed with these five distinguishing factors, whether virtuous or non-virtuous, is called a "great action," because it certainly brings about its result and brings about many. Thus, of virtue and non-virtue, one should abandon great non-virtue and apply oneself to great virtue — that is the meaning.

<!-- cm:khenpo-kunga -->
##### Khenpo Kunga Wangchuk (Commentary on the Bodhicaryāvatāra)

When one accomplishes virtue oneself, in order for it to become great virtue: in the distinction of time, "constantly" means practicing the virtuous Dharma every single day, without break. In the distinction of motivation, it is virtue prompted by faith, compassion, and so on, such as a great fixation of intense wish to accomplish that very virtue. Or again, in the distinction of the antidote: for the antidote to miserliness, such as giving to others one's own cherished possessions; then, for the antidote to immorality, such as undertaking to abandon whatever is hardest to give up — virtue prompted in this way. In the distinction of the field: in terms of the field of qualities, it is such as the root of virtue relying on the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha; as the field of benefactors, such as repaying the kindness of one's father and mother; as that which is the field of both qualities and benefaction, such as the virtue of relying on the khenpo, the teacher, the lama, the virtuous spiritual friend; and as the field of the suffering, such as a traveler on a long journey, one long ill, or a helpless pauper. Whatever virtue is done well, with a good motivation, toward these, he taught, becomes great virtue. And regarding non-virtue too, it is likewise: constantly committing non-virtues such as killing, being prompted by a strong fixation of wishing to kill, and so on, becomes so, he taught.

<!-- sub:synthesis -->
#### Verse Synthesis (overview)

**Brief introduction.** All the commentators agree that virtuous actions accomplished through the distinctions of time, motivation, antidote, and field, though small in substance, become powerful virtue that brings vast results.

**Key points.**

- **The distinction of time.** Virtuous deeds are to be done not once or twice but constantly, every single day, without break and without interruption.
- **The distinction of motivation.** One acts prompted by a motivation of intense strength: aspiration such as faith and compassion, and fixation.
- **The distinction of the antidote.** One acts prompted by an antidote, such as non-attachment and the meditation on emptiness, that destroys opposing factors such as miserliness and immorality.
- **The distinction of the field.** One accomplishes virtue relying on the field of qualities (the Three Jewels), the field of benefactors (parents and so on), and the field of the suffering (the sick, the destitute, and so on).
- **The distinction of the result.** Virtue endowed with these distinctions, though done with little material and in a short time, brings vast results; and non-virtue is the same in manner.
