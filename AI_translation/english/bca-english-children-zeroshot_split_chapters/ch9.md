## 9. Chapter Nine: The Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0


The Buddha taught all of these practices
for the sake of wisdom.
So, if we truly want suffering to end,
we must develop wisdom too. ^9-1


There are said to be two ways of understanding truth:
the ordinary, everyday way, and the deepest, ultimate way.
The deepest truth cannot be reached by ordinary thinking;
ordinary thinking belongs only to the everyday way. ^9-2


In this regard, there are two kinds of people in the world:
those who practice deep meditation, and ordinary people.
Ordinary understanding
is corrected by the understanding of meditators, ^9-3


and meditators themselves, because their understanding differs in depth,
are corrected in turn by those with even deeper insight.
Both groups actually agree on many things,
by using shared everyday examples, without needing to examine the deepest truth for those. ^9-4


Ordinary people see things
and immediately assume they are exactly as they appear.
They do not think of them as being like a magical illusion.
This is exactly where meditators and ordinary people disagree. ^9-5


Even things we see with our own eyes, like colors and shapes,
are only accepted as real because everyone agrees on them, not because they have been carefully proven true.
It is like how something unclean
gets called "clean" simply because everyone agrees to call it that — that is not the same as it truly being so. ^9-6


To help ordinary people begin to understand,
the Buddha, our protector, taught certain things
as if they truly existed just as they appear.
Someone might object: doesn't this contradict the deeper teaching that all things arise and pass in an instant, and even the ordinary teaching itself? ^9-7


There is nothing wrong with how meditators understand the everyday world;
compared to ordinary people, they see the true nature of things more clearly.
Otherwise, if we always trusted ordinary perception completely,
even something like seeing a woman's body as beautiful, when it is truly full of unclean things, would have to be considered correct — and ordinary understanding would end up disagreeing with meditators here too. ^9-8


"But how can goodness come from something merely illusion-like,
in the same way it would come from something truly, solidly real?
And if beings are just like illusions,
how can someone who has died be reborn again?" someone might ask. ^9-9


For as long as the right conditions come together,
even something illusion-like can still appear.
Why would something have to last a very long time
in order to be considered a real, living being? ^9-10


Killing an illusion-like person causes no real wrongdoing,
because that illusion has no mind of its own.
But when someone has an actual, illusion-like mind,
both goodness and wrongdoing can genuinely arise from it. ^9-11


Spells and other such things cannot actually create
an illusion-like mind at all.
An illusion arising from many different conditions
naturally takes on many different forms. ^9-12


There is no single condition anywhere
that has the power to create absolutely everything by itself.
Someone might ask: if ultimate truth is like true liberation from suffering,
while everyday reality is like the ordinary cycle of rebirth, ^9-13


then even a Buddha would still be caught in that same cycle —
so what real use would the bodhisattva path be?
But no — as long as the conditions for it continue uninterrupted,
even something illusion-like will keep appearing. ^9-14


Only once the flow of conditions is truly cut off
does even everyday appearance stop arising at all.
And when even ordinary confusion no longer exists,
what could an illusion possibly appear to, or be seen by? ^9-15


If, in ultimate truth, there really is no illusion for you at all,
then what exactly is there left to observe?
If you say something else still remains at that point,
that "something else" would just be your own mind. ^9-16


But if the mind itself is also illusion-like,
then what could possibly see what?
The Buddha, protector of the world, himself taught
that mind cannot truly see mind. ^9-17


Just as the edge of a sword
cannot cut itself,
the mind cannot truly see itself either.
Someone might say a lamp illuminates itself, the way it illuminates other things — ^9-18


but that is not quite right, because a lamp
is not something that was previously hidden by darkness needing illumination.
Something blue is simply blue by its very nature,
the way a crystal or sapphire is — it doesn't depend on anything else to make it blue. ^9-19


In the same way, we sometimes observe
things depending on other things, and sometimes not.
Something that is not blue does not become blue
just because it turns blue — it isn't "making itself" blue in that sense. ^9-20


When we say "the lamp illuminates,"
that is something we know and describe through our own awareness.
But who is it that first knows the mind itself is clear and knowing,
in order to describe it that way? ^9-21


If no one at all can actually see the mind,
then whether it is called "clear" or "not clear"
is really as meaningless to discuss
as praising the graceful walk of a woman who was never born. ^9-22


Someone might ask: "If the mind cannot know itself directly,
how could we ever remember our own past experiences?"
But memory can arise simply through connection with something else that was experienced,
just as the memory of a mouse's poisonous bite might return later, once its effect appears. ^9-23


Someone might say: "The mind clearly perceives itself
because it appears vividly, along with the right conditions."
But that is like saying that because eye medicine allows someone to see a treasure vase clearly,
the medicine itself must therefore also be seen just as clearly — which does not follow. ^9-24


We are not trying to deny
ordinary seeing, hearing, or knowing here at all.
What we are trying to correct
is the deeply held belief that these things are truly, solidly real, since that belief is the real cause of suffering. ^9-25


An illusion is not something separate from the mind,
yet it is also not exactly the same as the mind, since the mind is not permanent either.
So how could it be truly real, if it is neither the same as the mind nor different from it?
If it were truly different, it would have to exist as something independent, and it does not. ^9-26


Just as an illusion is not something truly, solidly real,
even though it can still be seen,
in the same way, the mind that perceives it is not truly real either.
If the ordinary cycle of rebirth were something truly solid to rely on,
it would have to be permanent, like the sky, and clearly it isn't. ^9-27


How could something not truly real
ever depend on something else that is truly real, and actually function?
If that were the case,
your very own mind would end up completely alone, without any real partner or support. ^9-28


When the mind is free of any real, solid object it perceives,
that is what all the Buddhas have realized.
But if that is so, what real value is there
in describing everything as "mind only"? ^9-29


Even understanding that things are like illusions,
how does that actually stop disturbing emotions from arising?
After all, even a magician who created an illusory woman
could still feel desire for his own illusion. ^9-30


That happens because the magician himself
has not yet let go of the deep habitual tendency toward disturbing emotions
regarding the object he is looking at.
So when he looks at it, his habitual tendency toward emptiness is still too weak to counter it. ^9-31


By becoming deeply familiar with the habit of seeing emptiness,
we gradually let go of the habit of seeing things as solidly real.
And by growing accustomed to the understanding that nothing at all is truly, solidly there,
we eventually let go of even that idea too, in time. ^9-32


When we examine something closely and find
that even the idea "it does not exist" cannot be pinned down as something solid either,
then how could "non-existence," with nothing left to rely on,
possibly remain fixed in the mind at all? ^9-33


When neither "it exists" nor "it does not exist"
remains fixed in the mind any longer,
then, since there is no other possible way to think of it,
the mind rests in perfect peace, free of any fixed reference point at all. ^9-34


Just as a wish-fulfilling jewel or a wish-granting tree
fulfills every hope placed in it,
in the same way, the Buddha appears
in whatever form helps beings, according to their own wishes and prayers. ^9-35


For example, someone once built a shrine
in honor of the mythical garuḍa bird, and then passed away.
Yet long after that person was gone,
the shrine still continued to calm poison and other dangers. ^9-36


In just the same way, a shrine built in accordance
with the practice of a bodhisattva, in honor of the Buddha,
continues to accomplish every kind of benefit for beings,
even after the bodhisattva has passed beyond suffering. ^9-37


Someone might ask: "How can making offerings to something without a mind of its own
actually produce any real result?"
But it is taught that making offerings brings the same result
whether the Buddha is still present or has already passed beyond suffering — the benefit is the same either way. ^9-38


Whether understood on the everyday level or on the level of ultimate truth,
this benefit comes about because the teachings themselves say so,
just as, according to those same teachings,
making offerings to a real, living Buddha also brings a result. ^9-39


Someone might say: "Liberation comes from directly seeing the four noble truths —
so what is the point of realizing emptiness as well?"
But the teachings themselves say
that without this path of wisdom, there is no true awakening at all. ^9-40


Someone might object: "But the Great Vehicle teachings are not even accepted as valid."
Then how can you accept your own scriptures as valid either?
Since both traditions equally rely on scripture to be considered valid,
this argument does not actually work against the Great Vehicle specifically. ^9-41


Whatever reason makes you trust your own scriptures
applies equally well to trusting the Great Vehicle scriptures too.
And if something is considered true just because two groups happen to agree on it,
then even non-Buddhist scriptures, agreed upon by their followers, would have to be considered true as well. ^9-42


Someone might say: "The Great Vehicle teachings are disputed, so they can't be trusted."
But other scriptures are disputed too, by non-Buddhists and even by other Buddhist schools among themselves —
so by that same reasoning, we would have to reject those scriptures as well. ^9-43


The very foundation of the teachings rests on the ordained community,
and even the true nature of that community is difficult to pin down.
Since the mind still grasping at solid objects
finds it difficult to reach the peace of true liberation, ^9-44


someone might say: "But if being free of disturbing emotions is what liberation means,
then it should follow immediately once those emotions end."
Yet even without disturbing emotions currently active,
we still see the power of past actions continuing to have an effect. ^9-45


Someone might say: "It is certain that craving,
the driving force that leads to further rebirth, is absent in such a person."
But even craving that is not obviously tied to strong disturbing emotions
can still exist quietly, in a confused way, why not? ^9-46


Craving arises based on feeling,
and feeling can still exist in such a person.
The mind that grasps at objects
can still remain present in some form. ^9-47


A mind that has not yet realized emptiness
can cease for a time, but will arise again later,
just as it does even in deep states of meditation without full realization.
That is why meditating on emptiness is so essential. ^9-48


Someone might say: "If the words used to enter these sūtras
are accepted as the Buddha's own words, then most of the Great Vehicle sūtras that you accept
should also be considered valid in the very same way, since they follow the same reasoning." ^9-49


If just one shared feature makes all these texts equally acceptable,
and yet you claim there's a fault in the Great Vehicle specifically,
then why not also say that all your own accepted sūtras
were, by that same shared feature, spoken by the Buddha too? ^9-50


Whatever the great disciple Kāśyapa and others like him
were unable to fully fathom the depths of —
just because you have not understood something either,
why should that mean it must be rejected? ^9-51


Freeing beings troubled by confusion
from the extremes of attachment and fear
by helping them remain in the world for others' benefit —
that itself is exactly the fruit of realizing emptiness. ^9-52


So there is really no good reason
to argue against the teaching of emptiness.
Therefore, without any doubt or hesitation,
we should meditate on emptiness. ^9-53


Since emptiness is the very remedy
for the darkness that clouds our understanding, caused by both disturbing emotions and ordinary ignorance,
and since we wish to become fully awakened, wise and compassionate, as quickly as possible,
why would we not meditate on it? ^9-54


Whatever thing causes real fear
naturally makes us afraid of it.
But emptiness is what actually calms suffering —
so how could it possibly be something to fear? ^9-55


If there really were some fixed, solid "self,"
it might make sense to fear the loss of it.
But since there has never actually been any such fixed self at all,
who exactly is there left to feel afraid? ^9-56


Teeth, hair, and nails are not the self.
The self is not bones or blood.
It is not mucus or bodily fluids,
and it is not lymph or pus either. ^9-57


The self is not fat or sweat.
It is not the lungs or the liver.
It is not any other internal organ,
and it is not waste or urine either. ^9-58


The self is not flesh or skin.
It is not body heat or breath.
It is not any of the body's cavities,
and none of the six kinds of consciousness are the self either. ^9-59


Someone might ask: "If awareness of sound were permanent,
it would have to be perceiving sound at every single moment.
But without something to be aware of, what exactly is being known?
On what basis could we even call it 'awareness' at all?" ^9-60


If something without any actual awareness could still be called "awareness,"
then even a piece of wood would absurdly have to be considered aware.
So it becomes clear that without its object actually present,
there simply is no awareness of that object at that time. ^9-61


If the very same awareness could also perceive shapes and colors,
why would it not then also be able to hear sounds at the same moment?
If it cannot, because sound is not present to it right then,
then clearly it has no awareness of sound in that moment either. ^9-62


How could something whose very nature is to grasp sound
somehow also grasp visual shapes and colors?
Someone might say: "One and the same person can be called both a father and a son" —
but that is only a label based on relationship, not something ultimately, fundamentally true. ^9-63


After all, qualities like courage, dust, or darkness
are not themselves considered a "son" or a "father."
In the same way, the very nature said to grasp sound
is never actually observed grasping visual shapes and colors instead. ^9-64


Like an actor who takes on different appearances,
that same underlying nature could not be considered permanent if it changes like that.
And if it is truly one and the same nature appearing in different roles,
then this "sameness" itself would be something entirely new, never seen before. ^9-65


Someone might say: "Its different appearances are not truly, ultimately real."
Then please describe what its own true, essential nature actually is.
If you say, "Its nature is simply awareness itself,"
then, by that reasoning, everything that has ever been born would absurdly have to be one single thing. ^9-66


Even things with a mind and things without a mind
would then absurdly have to be considered the same, since both equally "exist."
And if you say their individual differences are simply illusions,
then what common, unchanging basis could there possibly be for calling them "similar" at all? ^9-67


Something without a mind cannot be the self,
precisely because it lacks a mind, just like a clay pot.
Someone might say: "But it does have a mind at times" —
then, when it becomes aware, it would absurdly stop being what it was before, ending it. ^9-68


If nothing at all changes about the self,
then what real effect could awareness have had on it in the first place?
An unchanging thing with no real function or activity at all
would end up being just like empty space, mistakenly called "the self." ^9-69


Someone might say: "If there is no self at all,
then it doesn't make sense for actions to have results."
"After all, once an action is done, it's finished — so whose result would it become?" ^9-70


But actually, both sides — those who believe in a self and those who don't —
agree that an action and its later result exist as separate things in time,
and neither side needs a fixed "self" persisting between them to make sense of this;
they simply belong to the same ongoing stream of causes and effects. So this argument really proves nothing new. ^9-71


It's not actually possible for the very same, unchanging cause
to be observed together with its own effect at the same time.
It is only because they belong to a single, connected stream
that we speak of "the one who acts" and "the one who experiences the result." ^9-72


A past mind, or a future mind,
cannot be the true self, since neither of them actually exists right now.
And if you say the mind that is currently arising is the self,
then once that mind has passed, the self would have disappeared along with it. ^9-73


Just as, if you break a banana tree trunk apart piece by piece,
searching for some solid, essential core, you will never find one,
in the very same way, if we examine things carefully, piece by piece,
we will never find any truly solid, essential "self" either. ^9-74


Someone might ask: "But if there are no truly existing beings at all,
then for whom exactly should we feel compassion?"
We continue to speak of "beings" for the sake of achieving the true goal of helping them,
even though, on the deepest level, this is understood to be a kind of convenient, compassionate way of speaking, not a claim about ultimate reality. ^9-75


It is true that there are no truly existing beings, in the ultimate sense, to be the "result" of compassion.
But we still speak this way for the sake of that same ultimate goal.
So, in order to truly calm suffering,
we should not reject this compassionate way of speaking, even understanding its deeper nature. ^9-76


The pride that is the very root of suffering
grows precisely from confusion about a truly existing "self."
Someone might ask: "Then is there really no way to counter this at all?"
Meditating on the lack of a truly solid self is the very best remedy there is. ^9-77


The body is not the same as the feet.
The thighs and the waist are not the body either.
The stomach and the back are not the body,
and the chest and shoulders are not the body either. ^9-78


The ribs and hands are not the body.
The armpits and shoulders are not the body either.
The internal organs are not the body,
and the head and neck are not the body either —
so then, what exactly is "the body" here? ^9-79


If the body were somehow present, in some small part,
in each and every one of these different parts,
then, since the parts remain located within themselves,
where, exactly, would the whole body itself be located? ^9-80


If you say the whole body exists, entirely,
within each part like the hand,
then there would end up being just as many separate whole bodies
as there are individual body parts. ^9-81


If the body doesn't exist separately, whether outside or inside these parts,
then how could it exist within the hand and the other parts either?
And if the body is nothing apart from the hand and the other parts,
then how could it really exist as anything at all? ^9-82


So it turns out that the very idea of "the body"
is simply a confused assumption projected onto the hand and other parts, which have no body of their own.
It is a bit like mistaking a certain arrangement of stones for a person,
just because of a particular shape they happen to form. ^9-83


For as long as the right conditions come together,
the body appears to us as if it were a real, solid person.
In the same way, for as long as the parts like the hand exist,
the body appears there too, in just the same illusory way. ^9-84


In just the same way, since the hand itself
is really just a collection of fingers, what exactly is "the hand"?
And since each finger is itself
just a collection of joints, ^9-85


and each joint can be divided into still smaller parts,
and those parts can be divided into tiny particles,
and even those particles have directional parts of their own that can be further divided,
and even those directional parts have no true solid core — so, in the end, not even a single particle truly, solidly exists. ^9-86


So who among those who examine things carefully
would still feel real attachment to a form that is, in truth, just like something seen in a dream?
And when the body turns out to have no truly solid existence in this way,
then what exactly is "male" and what exactly is "female," in any ultimate sense? ^9-87


If suffering, examined this way, is truly, inherently real,
then why does it not also spoil real joy and happiness whenever it occurs?
And if happiness is truly real, then why do good foods and other pleasant things
not bring joy even to someone who is grieving or in pain? ^9-88


Someone might say: "In that case, the suffering is simply overpowered by something stronger."
But if suffering, in that moment, is not actually being experienced as suffering,
then how can something that has no experiencer of it, in the true sense,
really be called "a feeling" at all? ^9-89


Someone might say: "But a subtle form of that suffering still remains,
and it is only the coarser part that is calmed."
But then that "subtle remainder" is really just a lesser degree of ordinary happiness by comparison —
and if it were truly a lesser form of that very suffering, it would just be that same suffering in a smaller amount. ^9-90


And if a feeling arises only because its opposing condition (like relief) has arisen,
then it means the suffering, in truth, never actually arose as something solid and independent in the first place.
Doesn't this show that believing so strongly in the solid reality of "feeling"
is really just a deeply held assumption, not something ultimately, solidly true? ^9-91


That is exactly why we should meditate
on this kind of careful, wise examination, as the true remedy for that assumption.
Deep meditative concentration that comes from this kind of careful analysis
is truly the very food that nourishes a meditator's practice. ^9-92


Someone might ask: "If a sense faculty and its object come into contact only through some kind of gap or intermediary between them,
then where exactly do they actually meet?
And if there is no gap between them at all, and they are essentially just one thing,
then what exactly is meeting what?" ^9-93


One tiny particle cannot actually enter into another tiny particle,
since they would then have to occupy exactly the same space, which does not really allow for "meeting" in any meaningful sense.
If they don't actually enter each other, they cannot truly blend together,
and if they don't truly blend together, there is no real "contact" between them at all. ^9-94


How could it possibly make sense to say that something without any parts at all
can still come into "contact" with something else?
If you have ever actually observed genuine contact happening
between two things that truly have no parts whatsoever, please point it out. ^9-95


It doesn't really make sense, either, for consciousness, which has no physical form or body of its own,
to come into physical "contact" with anything.
And a collection of such things, since none of them are truly solid on their own,
cannot come into real contact either — just as we examined carefully before regarding the body. ^9-96


So, since real contact in this ultimate sense doesn't actually happen,
where, exactly, could feeling possibly come from?
And if that's the case, what was the whole point of all this effort?
And what exactly is harming what, in any of this? ^9-97


Since there is truly no one who experiences feeling in this solid, ultimate way,
and feeling itself has no solid, independent existence either,
then, understanding this situation clearly,
why would craving not naturally begin to fade away? ^9-98


Whatever we see or touch
is, by its very nature, like something in a dream or an illusion.
Because it arises together with the mind, in the very same moment,
it is not something the mind sees as if from a separate distance. ^9-99


Since the mind arises earlier or later relative to its object,
it becomes something remembered, not something truly experienced directly, in an ultimate sense.
The mind cannot truly experience itself directly,
and it cannot truly be experienced by something else either. ^9-100


So there is really no truly existing "experiencer of feeling" anywhere,
and therefore no truly, solidly existing "feeling" either, in that ultimate sense.
So, given that this whole collection of body and mind has no fixed, solid self within it,
how could it possibly be truly harmed by anything, in that deepest sense? ^9-101


The mind does not truly reside in the senses,
nor in objects like shapes and colors, nor somewhere in between.
It is not found inside, and not found outside,
and it cannot be found located anywhere else either. ^9-102


Whatever is not the body, and not something separate from it,
not mixed with it, and yet not found anywhere apart from it either,
and is not even the smallest bit of anything solid at all —
that is exactly why the true nature of beings is already, in itself, a state of perfect peace beyond suffering. ^9-103


If awareness existed before its object,
what exactly would it have been focused on when it first arose?
And if awareness and its object arise together, at exactly the same moment,
what would it have been focused on then either? ^9-104


And if awareness only comes after its object,
then what, exactly, would that later awareness have arisen from?
So, in this way, we can never quite pin down
how anything actually, truly "arises" in an ultimate sense. ^9-105


Someone might object: "But if that's the case, doesn't even everyday, ordinary reality fall apart?
Then how could there even be two levels of truth at all?"
But everyday reality is understood and validated
simply through the everyday understanding of ordinary people, not through this kind of ultimate analysis. ^9-106


Someone might say: "That's just other people's confused thinking about reality,
not necessarily your own accepted view of everyday truth."
But if that confused way of thinking eventually leads to a definite result,
then, in that sense, it can still be said to exist as a workable, everyday truth; if not, then it has no real basis even as an everyday truth. ^9-107


Careful analysis and the thing being analyzed
actually depend on each other for their meaning.
Just as we rely on commonly accepted terms and ideas
whenever we engage in any kind of careful examination or explanation at all. ^9-108


But when we then use careful analysis
to examine that very process of careful analysis itself,
this leads to an endless chain,
since we would need to analyze the analysis of the analysis, without end. ^9-109


But once the object of that analysis has already been carefully examined and understood,
there is no longer any separate, solid basis left for the analyzing process itself to stand on.
And since it lacks any solid basis to stand on, that process itself does not truly, solidly arise either —
this, too, is what we mean by peace beyond suffering, or true cessation. ^9-110


Which of these two positions is actually, ultimately correct
is a genuinely difficult question to settle with total certainty.
Someone might ask: "If everything depends on being known by a valid mind to be established as real,
then what, in turn, establishes that this validating awareness itself truly, solidly exists?" ^9-111


And if, instead, awareness is established as valid based on its object,
then what establishes that the object itself truly, solidly exists?
And if the two are said to depend on and validate each other in this way,
then, in truth, neither one can be said to independently, solidly exist on its own either. ^9-112


If there is no son, there cannot truly be a father either —
so where, exactly, would the very idea of "son" have come from in the first place?
Without a son, there is no father;
in just the same way, neither one, examined this way, can be said to solidly exist independently. ^9-113


Someone might say: "A sprout grows from a seed,
and through that sprout, we come to understand the seed must have existed.
In the same way, since awareness arises from its object,
why should we not understand from this that the object truly exists?" ^9-114


But we actually come to understand a seed existed
through some other awareness, different from the sprout itself, that connects the two —
so then, what other awareness, in turn,
allows us to understand that this very sprout-based awareness itself truly exists? ^9-115


For now, on the ordinary, everyday level, people simply
observe causes leading to their effects directly with their own eyes —
just as, for example, the many different parts of a lotus plant, like its stem and petals,
are understood to arise from correspondingly different causes. ^9-116


Someone might ask: "But what actually creates the different causes themselves in the first place?"
They arise, in turn, from still earlier, correspondingly different causes before them.
"But then why is a cause able to produce its particular effect at all?"
Simply because of the specific power that earlier cause itself possesses, inherited from its own causes. ^9-117


Someone might say: "A creator god is the true cause of the world."
Then please first explain clearly what exactly this creator god actually is.
If you say, "The basic elements themselves are this creator," well, perhaps in some loose sense,
but then why bother arguing so much over a mere name for something we already agree exists in another form? ^9-118


But the basic elements are many, separate things,
impermanent, without their own independent will, and not divine in the way a creator god is imagined to be.
They are things that can be walked upon and are not particularly pure,
so they clearly cannot be this imagined all-powerful, permanent creator god. ^9-119


A creator god cannot be like empty space either, since space has no independent will or activity of its own.
It also cannot be a fixed, permanent "self," since we have already shown that no such fixed self truly exists.
And it makes no sense to describe an inconceivable, unimaginable creator
using ordinary describable qualities and reasoning in the first place — what would even be the point? ^9-120


And what exactly is it that this creator god is even supposed to have created?
If you say it created the basic elements like earth, and so on,
then wouldn't this "creator god" itself, and even the natural elements, have to be permanent and unchanging too, in that case?
This runs into all the same problems as before: awareness would need to arise independently from its object... ^9-121


and happiness and suffering, since beginningless time, actually come about through people's own actions,
not from some outside creator — so please explain clearly what exactly this creator god is supposed to have created, beyond what already arises from causes and conditions.
If this ultimate cause itself has no real beginning,
then how could its effects have a real, definite beginning either? ^9-122


Someone might ask: "Why doesn't a permanent creator god act constantly, at every moment, to create everything?"
If it truly does not depend on anything else at all to act,
and yet it hasn't already created everything, if nothing else besides it exists to hold it back or delay it,
then what exactly could it possibly still be waiting for or depending on? ^9-123


And if you say this creator god does depend on certain conditions in order to act,
then it is really the coming-together of those conditions that is the true cause, not some separate, all-powerful creator god at all.
Without those conditions coming together, this creator would have no power to create anything;
and without the creator, in that case, there is really no separate "power to create" left to speak of either. ^9-124


If this creator god acts even against its own wishes,
that would mean it is actually under the control of something else, some other force.
And if it acts only according to what it wishes,
then it is really just following its own wishes and desires — so in what real sense is it still an all-powerful, independent creator at all? ^9-125


Those non-Buddhist philosophers who claim that tiny, indivisible particles are permanent
have already been refuted earlier in this very examination.
Followers of the Sāṃkhya school, meanwhile, claim that a permanent underlying "Nature"
is the true cause of the whole visible world. ^9-126


They describe this underlying "Nature" as consisting of three qualities held in perfect balance —
called courage, activity or dust, and darkness or dullness —
and call this balanced state the "Principal" cause of everything.
Yet they also say that the visible, unequal world of experience arises from this supposedly balanced, equal source. ^9-127


But it doesn't really make sense for a single, unified thing
to simultaneously have three completely different natures within it — so this "Nature" cannot truly exist as described.
And in the same way, these three so-called qualities cannot truly, independently exist either,
precisely because each one is said to be genuinely distinct and separate from the other two. ^9-128


And if these qualities don't actually exist as described,
then the existence of sound and other sensory experiences, said to arise from them, becomes extremely doubtful and far-fetched too.
And it makes no real sense for something without any mind at all, like a piece of cloth,
to somehow possess real qualities like happiness or suffering within it, the way this theory claims. ^9-129


"But things arise having the very nature of their own cause,"
they might say — but haven't we already examined and refuted this kind of claim carefully above?
"Your own so-called cause, this underlying Nature, is itself said to be made of happiness and other such qualities,"
and yet, from what you describe as its "nature," things like cloth simply do not actually arise in reality. ^9-130


"But happiness and so on are said to come specifically from things like cloth" — that gets it backwards;
without that cloth already existing, there would be no happiness experienced through it in the first place.
And this so-called "happiness" and other qualities are also claimed to be permanent and unchanging,
yet we never actually observe them remaining fixed and unchanging in our experience at all. ^9-131


If happiness and the other qualities are said to be clearly, vividly present within things at all times,
then why is that clear happiness not always directly experienced as such?
And if you say it becomes extremely subtle at certain times instead,
then how exactly can the very same thing be both "coarse" one moment and "subtle" the next? ^9-132


Since something becomes "subtle" only by first losing its "coarse" form,
this shows that both the coarse and subtle states are, in fact, impermanent and changing, not fixed.
So, in that very same way, why not simply accept
that all things, without exception, are impermanent and constantly changing? ^9-133


If the "coarse" state of happiness is really not fundamentally different from happiness itself,
then it clearly follows that even vivid, obvious happiness must also be impermanent, not eternal.
Someone might object: "Something that has never truly existed at all
simply cannot newly arise, precisely because it had no prior existence to arise from." ^9-134


But then, according to your own theory, happiness would have to "arise" without ever having been clearly, vividly present beforehand,
even though you don't want to accept that — yet this is exactly what your own position implies.
And if you say the effect is somehow already fully present, hidden within its cause beforehand,
then eating ordinary food would absurdly mean eating something already unclean, since that food supposedly already secretly contains its own eventual waste product. ^9-135


"With the same money used to buy cotton cloth, buy instead the cotton seeds,
and then simply distribute those seeds among people as if they were already the finished cloth" —
someone might object that ordinary, confused people simply fail to see this deeper connection,
but that the wise, through their special understanding, can perceive this hidden identity between cause and effect. ^9-136


But this kind of "special understanding" is really something ordinary people could just as easily have too,
if it were genuinely valid — so why do they not actually perceive it in this way at all?
And if ordinary people's understanding is not considered a valid way of knowing things here,
then even their ordinary, everyday seeing of things as vivid and real should equally not be considered reliably true either. ^9-137


And if this ordinary way of knowing is not considered a valid means of understanding reality,
then wouldn't whatever it measures and understands about the world also have to be considered unreliable and not truly established?
For this very reason, meditating on the ultimate emptiness of all things,
in the way we have been discussing, actually turns out to make complete and total sense after all. ^9-138


Without first mentally engaging with something as if it were a solid, real object at all,
we cannot actually grasp or understand its ultimate lack of solid existence either.
So, whatever solid "thing" is ultimately shown to be unreal upon careful examination,
its very "unreality," understood clearly this way, is also, in the end, simply an unreal concept, useful only as a tool, not an ultimate truth in itself. ^9-139


So, just as when we think, in a dream, "My child has died,"
and the thought "the child does not exist" arises in our mind afterward,
that very thought "the child does not exist" is itself simply another kind of mental construction,
just as much a construction as the original thought that the child truly existed — both are equally unreal, in the end. ^9-140


So, examining things carefully and thoroughly in this way,
we find that nothing at all truly exists without depending on some cause or condition.
And nothing exists as something truly separate and independent, or as something existing only when combined with other things either;
nothing truly rests permanently in any of these ways at all. ^9-141


Nothing has truly, solidly "come" from anywhere else,
and nothing truly, solidly "remains" fixed in place, or "goes" anywhere else either.
So this whole world, which confused beings take to be so solidly, truly real,
is, in the end, really no different from a magical illusion. ^9-142


Whatever is created through a magician's illusion,
and whatever is created through ordinary causes and conditions in the everyday world —
we should examine carefully, in both cases,
exactly where these things are supposed to have come from, and exactly where they are supposed to be going. ^9-143


Whatever appears only when certain nearby conditions happen to be present,
and does not appear at all when those conditions are absent,
is, in that sense, exactly like a reflection or an image created through some clever trick —
so how could such a thing possibly be considered truly, solidly real? ^9-144


If something already truly, solidly exists on its own,
what real need would there even be for a cause to bring it into being at all?
And on the other hand, if something does not exist at all in the first place,
then what real use would a cause be for that either? ^9-145


Even hundreds of millions of causes, working together,
cannot actually change something that has no existence at all into something that truly, solidly exists.
And in that state of not yet truly existing, how could it suddenly count as something real?
And what exactly would it even turn into, since it wasn't a real thing in the first place? ^9-146


If something didn't exist at the earlier moment, then it isn't really possible for it to suddenly, truly "exist" now either —
so when, exactly, could something ever become truly, solidly existent in this ultimate sense?
And if this "thing" never actually, truly arose as something solid in the first place,
then it could never really be said to have "stopped" or "ceased" to exist either, since it was never solidly there to begin with. ^9-147


And since nothing ever solidly ceased to exist in that ultimate sense,
there is also no real moment where something could be said to have truly, solidly "existed" either.
So, in this way, nothing ever truly, solidly comes into being at all,
otherwise, things would absurdly have to possess two completely contradictory natures at once. ^9-148


So, in this way, there is truly no such thing as ultimate "arising,"
and there is no truly, solidly existing "thing" either, for that very same reason.
And so, all these many different kinds of beings in the world
are, in the deepest sense, never truly born and never truly cease to exist at all. ^9-149


All the different kinds of beings in this world are, in truth, just like something experienced in a dream.
Examined carefully, they are really just like the empty, coreless trunk of a banana tree, with no solid center to be found.
And in this deepest, truest sense, there is really no fundamental difference at all
between someone who has reached liberation and someone still caught in ordinary rebirth. ^9-150


So, given that all things are, in this deep sense, empty of any solid, independent existence,
what is there really to gain, and what is there really to lose?
Who is there, in any ultimate sense, to be honored and respected,
and who is there to be looked down on or treated with contempt? ^9-151


Where, really, do happiness and suffering ultimately come from?
What is there, in the deepest sense, to feel truly glad about, and what is there to feel truly upset about?
And if we search carefully, in this ultimate way,
who exactly is doing the craving, and what exactly is being craved? ^9-152


If we examine this living world of ours carefully,
who exactly is it that will truly die here?
What is it that is yet to come, and what has already come and gone?
And who, in this deepest sense, are truly our relatives and friends? ^9-153


I hope that people like myself may come to understand
that everything is, in this deep sense, just like open space, without any solid, fixed nature to grasp onto.
People who want only their own personal happiness
end up caught up in endless conflict and fleeting excitement because of this deep misunderstanding, ^9-154


becoming terribly agitated one moment and overjoyed the next.
Because of this same confusion, people struggle, strain, argue,
harm one another, and even wound each other,
living out difficult, troubled lives filled with wrongdoing. ^9-155


Even after being reborn again and again into fortunate circumstances,
and enjoying many pleasures along the way,
once they die, they still fall, again and again,
into the long, terrible suffering of hard rebirths. ^9-156


In this endless cycle of rebirth, there are so many dangerous pitfalls,
and the deep, true nature of reality is simply not understood there at all, in the way we have described.
And within that same cycle, things constantly contradict and clash with one another —
so this deep, true nature of reality is, sadly, rarely realized there. ^9-157


And because of all this, beings experience
a vast, unimaginable, boundless ocean of suffering, impossible to fully describe.
Within that same ocean, our own strength and ability to help is often so limited,
and even our own lifespan there is so terribly short. ^9-158


And within that same difficult existence, so much effort goes toward
just staying alive and healthy, working, and dealing with hunger and exhaustion,
sleep, various kinds of harm, and, in just the same way,
so much time is wasted, pointlessly, keeping company with immature people. ^9-159


Life slips away quickly, pointlessly, this way,
while real wisdom and insight remain so very hard to gain.
And within that same difficult existence, how could anyone possibly find
a real, reliable way to overcome this deeply ingrained habit of distraction and confusion? ^9-160


And within that same existence, harmful forces work hard,
in a sense, to make sure beings keep falling into terrible, difficult rebirths.
There are so many wrong paths to be misled by there,
and it is genuinely difficult to move beyond confusion and doubt. ^9-161


And having once again finally gained this rare and precious human life,
it is extremely difficult to ever encounter a Buddha's teaching again.
And it is genuinely so hard to free ourselves from the flood of disturbing emotions —
oh, how sad it is that suffering just continues on and on, one link after another, in this endless chain! ^9-162


So, even though beings are caught up in such immense suffering like this,
so many of them still fail to recognize their own suffering for what it truly is.
For beings caught in this endless flood of suffering,
oh, how deeply this truly deserves our compassion and concern. ^9-163


It is a bit like someone who keeps bathing themselves in freezing water again and again,
and then keeps jumping into fire again and again, over and over,
even while, in the very midst of such immense suffering,
mistakenly believing themselves to be perfectly happy and fine. ^9-164


In this way, people go about their lives as though old age and death could never touch them,
right up until the very moment when death, like an executioner, suddenly arrives,
and then they fall, unbearably, and without warning,
straight into a hard and terrible rebirth. ^9-165


I wonder — when will I finally be able
to calm this burning fire of suffering for beings like this,
by pouring down a great rain of goodness and happiness,
born wonderfully from the clouds of my own accumulated merit? ^9-166


I wonder — when will I finally be able, with real respect and devotion,
to gather a vast collection of goodness in a way completely free of clinging to it as solidly real,
and, for the sake of those who suffer precisely because of that kind of solid clinging,
finally be able to reveal to them the true nature of emptiness itself? ^9-167


This is the end of the ninth chapter, "The Perfection of Wisdom," from the Way of the Bodhisattva. ^9-a
